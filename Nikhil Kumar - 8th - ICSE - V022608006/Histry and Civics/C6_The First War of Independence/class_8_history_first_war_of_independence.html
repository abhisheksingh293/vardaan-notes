THE FIRST WAR OF INDEPENDENCE

1. Introduction & Nature of the Uprising

The uprising of 1857 was a major upheaval that shook the foundations of the British rule in India. It was the clearest expression of the cumulative effect of the general discontentment among the people against an oppressive foreign rule.

  • British View: Termed merely as a 'Sepoy Mutiny' by British historians like Sir James Outram and P.E. Roberts, implying it was limited to disgruntled soldiers.
  • Indian Nationalist View: Termed as the 'First War of Independence' by leaders like Veer Savarkar and A. Mehta, highlighting the mass participation of both Hindus and Muslims, peasants, craftsmen, and rulers.
  • Even the British Prime Minister, Disraeli, recognised it as a national uprising in the Parliament.

2. Causes of the Uprising

Political Causes

The expansionist policies of the British made all Indian rulers suspicious of their true intentions.

  • Annexation Policies: Under the Subsidiary Alliance and the Doctrine of Lapse, native kings were reduced to subordinates. Kingdoms like Satara, Jhansi, Sambalpur, Nagpur, and Awadh were unjustly annexed.
  • Disrespect to Rulers: The British discontinued granting pensions and titles to Indian rulers. Nana Saheb (adopted son of Baji Rao II) was deprived of his pension and title.
Concept

Doctrine of Lapse: An annexation policy devised by Lord Dalhousie. If an Indian ruler died without a natural biological male heir, the kingdom would "lapse" and automatically be annexed by the British East India Company.

Economic Causes

The British policies heavily exploited India's wealth, ruining various sections of the society.

  • Ruin of Peasants: High land revenue rates and aggressive collection by zamindars made peasant lives miserable. Many lost their lands and famines became frequent.
  • Ruin of Artisans: Large-scale import of cheap British machine-made goods destroyed Indian handicrafts. Indian goods faced heavy duties in England, decreasing their demand.
  • Unemployment: People dependent on royal patronage lost their livelihoods due to the displacement of the old ruling class.

Social and Religious Causes

British interference in Indian religious traditions created deep insecurity.

  • Social Reforms: The abolition of Sati, legalisation of widow remarriage, and introduction of women's education were viewed by orthodox Hindus as an attack on their social order.
  • Religious Insecurity: The attempts of Christian missionaries to convert people induced widespread fear. Lands belonging to temples and mosques were taxed.
  • Racial Discrimination: Indians were denied high-ranking jobs and were constantly humiliated by the British.

Military Causes

The Indian sepoys formed the bulk of the British army but faced immense discrimination.

  • Unequal Pay & Promotion: Indian sepoys were paid less than their European counterparts and could not rise beyond the rank of a Subedar.
  • General Service Enlistment Act (1856): Made it mandatory for new recruits to serve overseas, violating Hindu norms which considered overseas travel a caste taboo.
  • Religious Disrespect: The British forbade sectarian marks like the tilak, turban, or beard. They also discontinued the bhatta (overseas allowance).

The Immediate Cause

The atmosphere was highly volatile, and a new weapon provided the final spark for the uprising.

Fact

The Greased Cartridges: In 1856, the British introduced the Enfield Rifle. The cartridges had to be bitten off before use. A rumour spread that the grease was made from the fat of cows and pigs, severely hurting the religious sentiments of both Hindus and Muslims.

  • On 29 March 1857, a sepoy named Mangal Pandey revolted at Barrackpore. He was arrested and hanged on 8 April.
  • In May 1857, 85 soldiers in Meerut refused the rifles and were imprisoned. The next day, sepoys mutinied, liberated their colleagues, and marched to Delhi.

3. Main Centres and Leaders

The uprising quickly spread across North and Central India, led by prominent local rulers.

  • Jhansi: Led by Rani Lakshmibai after the British refused to recognise her adopted son. She fought bravely alongside Tatya Tope and died fighting on 17 June 1858.
  • Kanpur: Led by Nana Saheb. He expelled the British, declared himself Peshwa, and was supported by Tatya Tope and Azimullah.
  • Arrah (Bihar): Led by Rajput chieftain Kunwar Singh of Jagdishpur.
  • Delhi: The sepoys marched to Delhi and proclaimed the Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar as the leader of the uprising.

4. Suppression of the Uprising

The British reacted with utmost brutality to crush the rebellion.

  • Governor-General Lord Canning mobilised his best commanders: Sir John Lawrence, Campbell, Outram, Neill, Rose, and Havelock.
  • Delhi was stormed and recaptured in September 1857. Bahadur Shah Zafar was deported to Rangoon, and his sons were shot dead at Khooni Darwaza, ending the Mughal Empire.
  • Villages were burnt, and rebels were publicly hanged or blown from cannons. The uprising was completely suppressed within a year.

5. Causes of the Failure

Despite mass participation, the uprising failed to achieve its ultimate goal due to several factors.

  • Limited Spread: It was confined mostly to Northern and Central India. The South, West, and East remained largely unaffected.
  • Lack of Coordination: The rebels lacked centralised planning, foresight, and a common coordinated strategy against the British.
  • Outdated Weapons: Indians fought with primitive weapons like swords and spears against the sophisticated modern artillery of the British.
  • Lack of Native Support: Many Indian rulers (Scindias, Holkars, Nizam of Hyderabad, Rajput princes) and the newly educated Indians remained loyal to the British and stayed away from the uprising.

6. Effects of the Uprising

The uprising of 1857 marked a turning point in Indian history.

Important

Transfer of Power (1858): The rule of the East India Company officially ended. The administration of India was taken over directly by the British Crown (Queen Victoria).

  • The Board of Control and Directors were replaced by the Secretary of State for India.
  • The title of Governor-General was changed to Viceroy.
  • Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1858): Promised Indian princes no further annexations and assured non-interference in India's social and religious practices.
  • The British army was completely reorganised to prevent future mutinies.
  • Most importantly, the uprising sowed the very seeds of Indian nationalism, acting as a profound source of inspiration for future freedom struggles.
