Carbon and Its Compounds

Carbon is considered the third most important element after oxygen and hydrogen. The word is derived from the Latin word Carbo, meaning a black rock that can burn. It is a non-metal with the atomic number 6, atomic mass 12, and its symbol is C. All living creatures are based on it.

1. Occurrence of Carbon

In the earth's crust, there is only 0.02% carbon (present as minerals), and in the atmosphere, 0.03% is present as carbon dioxide. Carbon occurs in nature in both free and combined states.

  • Free State: It occurs as coal, diamond, and graphite. Diamond is the purest form, while coal is an impure form (60% to 90% carbon).
  • Combined State: It occurs in plants and animals (carbohydrates, fats, proteins), the atmosphere (carbon dioxide), fossil fuels (methane, natural gas, petroleum), dissolved in water, and in carbonates (limestone, marble, chalk).

2. Classification of Compounds

Compounds are generally classified into two main groups:

Concept

Organic Compounds: Compounds containing molecules of carbon atoms. Examples include Hydrocarbons (Methane, Butane), Alcohols (Ethyl alcohol), Acids (Acetic acid), and Sugars (Glucose).

Inorganic Compounds: Compounds containing molecules of elements other than carbon atoms, or specific carbon-containing minerals. Examples include Calcium carbonate (CaCO3), Sodium bicarbonate, and Calcium carbide.

3. Allotropes of Carbon

Fact

Allotropy is the ability of a chemical element to exist in two or more different forms in the same physical state. These different forms are known as allotropes.

Pure carbon exists in two main types of forms: Crystalline forms (Diamond, Graphite, Fullerenes) and Amorphous forms (Coke, Charcoal, Lampblack, etc.).

Crystalline Forms

  • Diamond: The purest and hardest natural substance known. Each carbon atom is strongly linked to four other carbon atoms in a tetrahedral manner. It is a bad conductor of heat and electricity due to the absence of free electrons. Uses: Gems/jewellery, cutting glass, drilling rocks, and drawing tungsten wires.
  • Graphite: A soft, greyish, and greasy substance. Carbon atoms are linked in a hexagonal network in flat layers, held together by weak forces (allowing them to slide). It is a good conductor of heat and electricity. Uses: Pencil leads, making electrodes, high-temperature dry lubricant, and nuclear reactor moderators.
  • Fullerenes: Hollow, cage-like, spherical, or cylindrical molecules. The most common is Buckminsterfullerene (C60), which consists of 20 hexagonal and 12 pentagonal rings. Uses: Insulators, superconductors, lubricants, and in cancer/AIDS therapy.

Amorphous Forms

  • Coke: Produced by the destructive distillation of coal (heating coal in the absence of air). Uses: As a fuel and as a vital reducing agent in the iron and steel industries.
  • Soot or Lampblack: A soft black powder produced by burning carbon-rich substances (turpentine, kerosene) in an insufficient supply of air. Uses: Making printing ink, black paint, and as a filler for rubber in tyres.
  • Charcoal: Formed by burning wood in a limited supply of air.
    • Wood Charcoal: Highly porous and readily adsorbs gases and coloured impurities.
    • Animal/Bone Charcoal: Used for decolourising brown sugar.
    • Sugar Charcoal: The purest form of amorphous carbon, obtained by dehydrating cane sugar with conc. sulphuric acid.

4. Carbon Dioxide (CO2)

Air contains about 0.039% carbon dioxide. It is essential for all life forms as the basis for carbohydrates and proteins via photosynthesis.

  • Preparation: In the laboratory, CO2 is prepared by the action of dilute hydrochloric acid on marble chips (Calcium carbonate).
    CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2
  • Physical Properties: Colourless, odourless, 1.5 times heavier than air, sparingly soluble in water, and can be liquefied under pressure to form solid CO2 (Dry Ice).
  • Chemical Properties: It is an acidic oxide. It reacts with bases to form salts and turns moist blue litmus red. It neither burns nor supports combustion.
Concept

Lime Water Test: When CO2 is bubbled through freshly prepared lime water [Ca(OH)2], it forms insoluble calcium carbonate (CaCO3), which gives a milky appearance to the solution. This is the standard test for the presence of CO2.

Uses of Carbon Dioxide: Aerated drinks, purifying cane sugar, manufacturing washing soda, fire extinguishers, and as a refrigerant (Dry ice).

5. Carbon Monoxide (CO)

Carbon monoxide is a colourless, tasteless, and highly poisonous gas. It is a product of burning carbon in a limited supply of air.

  • Preparation: Prepared in the laboratory by dehydrating formic acid (HCOOH) with concentrated sulphuric acid.
  • Properties: It is slightly lighter than air, stable, and neutral to litmus. It is combustible and burns with a pale blue flame.
  • Reducing Agent: CO reduces heated metallic oxides to their respective metals. This property is crucial in the Blast Furnace for extracting iron.
    Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2
Important

Asphyxia (Harmful Properties): Carbon monoxide is highly poisonous. When inhaled, it combines with hemoglobin in the blood to form carboxyhemoglobin instead of oxyhemoglobin. This prevents oxygen transport, causing starvation of oxygen in body tissues, leading to suffocation (asphyxia) and death.
