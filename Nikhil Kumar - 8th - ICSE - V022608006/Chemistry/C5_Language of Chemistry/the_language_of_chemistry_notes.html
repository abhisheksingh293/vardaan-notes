The Language of Chemistry

1. Symbols of Elements

In chemistry, every element is identified by a unique shorthand representation. The abbreviation of the name of an element to its initial letter or letters is called the symbol of an element.

Initially, Alchemists and scientists like John Dalton proposed early visual symbols for elements. Today, we use a more systematic naming system recognized globally.

Concept

What does a symbol of an element represent?

  • The specific name of the element.
  • Exactly one atom of that element.

Rules for Writing Symbols

  • The symbol for an element is typically the first letter of its English name, written in uppercase (e.g., H for hydrogen, O for oxygen).
  • If names of multiple elements begin with the same letter, the first two letters are often used. The first letter is uppercase, and the second is lowercase (e.g., Al for aluminium, Ca for calcium).
  • Some elements use the letters of their Latin names. For example, Hg for mercury (Hydrargyrum), Cu for copper (Cuprum), and Au for gold (Aurum).

2. Valency

Atoms of elements combine with other atoms to form molecules. The valency of an element is defined as a measure of its combining power with different atoms. It represents the number of electrons an atom donates, accepts, or shares to achieve a stable electronic configuration.

Types of Valency

  • Electrovalency: The number of electrons lost or gained by an element to acquire the nearest stable noble gas configuration. An atom that loses electrons exhibits electropositive valency, while an atom that gains electrons exhibits electronegative valency.
  • Covalency: When an element shares electrons with other atoms (of the same or different elements) to acquire a stable configuration, it is called covalency.
Important

Variable Valency: Certain elements can combine with different atoms to form multiple compounds, exhibiting more than one valency. For instance, iron (Fe) exhibits a valency of 2 in ferrous compounds (like FeCl₂) and a valency of 3 in ferric compounds (like FeCl₃). Other examples include copper (Cu) and lead (Pb).

3. Radicals

A radical is a group of atoms of different elements that behave as a single unit having a net electrical charge. They do not have an independent existence and are always present in combination with other elements or radicals.

  • Basic Radicals (Cations): Radicals with a positive charge (e.g., Ammonium NH₄⁺).
  • Acidic Radicals (Anions): Radicals with a negative charge (e.g., Sulphate SO₄²⁻, Nitrate NO₃⁻, Carbonate CO₃²⁻).

4. Chemical Formulae

A chemical formula is a symbolic representation of a molecule of an element or a compound. It gives the exact number of atoms of the same or different elements present in one molecule.

Writing a Chemical Formula (Criss-Cross Method)

To write the formula of a compound, follow these steps:

  1. Write the symbols or formulae of the constituent elements or radicals side-by-side. Place the cation on the left and the anion on the right.
  2. Write their respective valencies as superscripts.
  3. Criss-cross the valencies and write them as subscripts.
  4. If the valency numbers are divisible by a common factor, divide to simplify the formula (e.g., Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ criss-cross to Ca₂O₂, which simplifies to CaO).

5. Chemical Equations

A chemical equation is the symbolic representation of a chemical reaction in the form of symbols and formulae. The starting substances (reactants) are written on the left-hand side, and the newly formed substances (products) are written on the right-hand side, separated by an arrow (→) indicating the direction of the reaction.

Fact

Law of Conservation of Mass: Formulated by Antoine Lavoisier, this law states that "mass can neither be created nor destroyed." In any chemical reaction, the total mass of the reactants must equal the total mass of the products. This is why chemical equations must always be balanced.

Limitations of a Chemical Equation

A standard skeleton chemical equation does not convey all details about a reaction. Its limitations include:

  • It does not mention the physical states of the substances. This is overcome by adding (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas, and (aq) for aqueous.
  • It gives no information regarding the concentration of reactants (e.g., dilute or concentrated).
  • It does not specify the required conditions such as temperature, pressure, or the presence of a catalyst.
  • It provides no indication regarding the speed of the reaction or whether heat is absorbed or evolved.
