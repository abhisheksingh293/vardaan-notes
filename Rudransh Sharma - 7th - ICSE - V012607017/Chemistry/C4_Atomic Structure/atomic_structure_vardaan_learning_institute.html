Atomic Structure

1. Introduction to the Atom

Concept

An atom is the smallest particle of an element which takes part in chemical reactions but does not exist independently. The properties of an element depend on its constituent atoms.

The major contribution regarding the structure of an atom was given by John Dalton. He put forward his atomic theory in the year 1808 which suggested:

  • Matter is made up of atoms.
  • Atoms are indivisible (meaning they cannot be divided further).
  • Atoms of an element are identical in size, mass, and chemical properties.
  • Atoms can neither be created nor destroyed.
Fact

Later experiments showed that atoms are divisible and are made up of three subatomic particles: Electrons (negatively charged, discovered by J.J. Thomson in 1897), Protons (positively charged, discovered by E. Goldstein), and Neutrons (neutral particles, discovered by James Chadwick in 1932).

2. Models of an Atom

Rutherford's Model of an Atom (1911)

Based on his scattering experiments, Ernest Rutherford suggested that:

  • Each atom consists of a small, dense, and positively charged central core called the nucleus where protons are present.
  • The nucleus is surrounded by a much larger region of empty space in which negatively charged electrons are moving at a high speed.

Bohr's Model of an Atom (1913)

Neils Bohr provided an advanced concept of atomic structure:

  • Negatively charged electrons revolve around the nucleus in fixed circular paths called orbits, energy levels, or shells. These are represented as K, L, M, N.
  • Each shell is associated with a fixed amount of energy. The shell closest to the nucleus (K-shell) possesses minimum energy.
Important

According to the Bohr-Bury scheme, the maximum number of electrons that can be accommodated in any shell is determined by the formula 2n² (where 'n' is the shell number). However, the outermost shell of an electrically neutral atom can hold a maximum of only 8 electrons.

3. Molecules and Atomicity

A molecule is formed when two or more atoms combine together chemically. It is the smallest particle of an element or a compound that can exist independently.

Atomicity is defined as the number of atoms that combine together to form a molecule. Based on atomicity, molecules are classified into:

  • Monoatomic: Molecules made up of only one atom (e.g., Helium He, Neon Ne).
  • Diatomic: Molecules made up of two atoms (e.g., Hydrogen H₂, Oxygen O₂).
  • Triatomic: Molecules made up of three atoms (e.g., Ozone O₃).
  • Tetraatomic: Molecules made up of four atoms (e.g., Phosphorus P₄).
  • Polyatomic: Molecules made up of more than four atoms (e.g., Sulphur S₈).

4. Atomic Number, Mass Number, and Isotopes

  • Atomic Number (Z): Defined as the total number of protons present inside the nucleus of an atom. For neutral atoms, it is equal to the number of electrons.
  • Mass Number (A): Defined as the sum of protons and neutrons present inside the nucleus.
  • Calculating Neutrons: Number of neutrons = Mass Number (A) - Atomic Number (Z).

Isotopes are atoms of the same element which have the same atomic number but different mass numbers. For example, Hydrogen has three isotopes: Protium (¹H₁), Deuterium (²H₁), and Tritium (³H₁).

5. Radicals and Valency

A radical is an atom or a group of atoms having an electrical charge. They can be positive (basic radicals like Sodium ion Na⁺) or negative (acidic radicals like Chloride ion Cl⁻).

Valency is the measure of an element's combining power. It is defined as the number of electrons lost, gained, or shared by an atom to attain a stable inert gas configuration.

  • Positive Valency: Atoms (mostly metals) that lose electrons form positive ions (cations). Example: Aluminium loses 3 electrons to form Al³⁺.
  • Negative Valency: Atoms (mostly nonmetals) that gain electrons form negative ions (anions). Example: Oxygen gains 2 electrons to form O²⁻.
  • Variable Valency: Some elements exhibit more than one valency (e.g., Copper shows +1 as Cuprous and +2 as Cupric).

6. Chemical Bonding

Atoms combine to achieve stability either by losing, gaining, or sharing electrons.

  • Electrovalent (Ionic) Bond: Formed between a metal and a nonmetal by the complete transfer of one or more electrons (e.g., Sodium Chloride, NaCl).
  • Covalent Bond: Formed between nonmetals by the mutual sharing of one or more electrons (e.g., Chlorine molecule, Cl₂).
