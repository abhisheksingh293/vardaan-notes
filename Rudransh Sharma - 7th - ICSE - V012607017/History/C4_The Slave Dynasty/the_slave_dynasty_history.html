The Slave Dynasty

1. Background & Introduction

After the death of Muhammad Ghori in 1206 CE, who left no clear heir to the throne, his general Qutb-ud-din Aibak succeeded him. He established a new dynasty that came to be known as the Slave Dynasty or Mamluk (Arabic word meaning slave or owned) Dynasty.

This marked the beginning of the powerful Delhi Sultanate, which ruled through five major dynasties:

  • The Slave Dynasty (1206-1290 CE)
  • The Khalji Dynasty (1290-1320 CE)
  • The Tughluq Dynasty (1320-1413 CE)
  • The Sayyid Dynasty (1414-1451 CE)
  • The Lodi Dynasty (1451-1526 CE)

2. Important Rulers of the Slave Dynasty

Qutb-ud-din Aibak (1206-1210 CE)

Originally a slave, Qutb-ud-din rose to become Muhammad Ghori's trusted general and the first Sultan of the Slave Dynasty. He successfully controlled internal revolts and foreign invasions.

  • He built India's oldest mosque adjacent to the Qutb Minar, called Qubbat-ul-Islam (sanctuary of Islam) or Quwwat-ul-Islam (might of Islam).
  • He also built the Arhai Din Ka Jhonpra mosque in Ajmer.
  • He started the construction of the famous Qutb Minar but passed away before its completion.
Fact Qutb-ud-din Aibak was widely known as 'Lakh Baksh' (giver of lakhs) because of his extreme generosity and large donations.
Important His rule was short-lived, as he died in a tragic accident in 1210 CE while playing Changan (polo).

Shams-ud-din Iltutmish (1211-1236 CE)

Iltutmish, a former slave and son-in-law of Aibak, succeeded to the throne in 1211 CE. He is regarded as the real consolidator and saviour of the Delhi Sultanate.

  • Conquests: Defeated Yalduz (ruler of Ghazni) and Qubacha (ruler of Multan), suppressed the Khalji revolt in Bengal, and defeated the Rajput rulers of Gwalior, Malwa, Ujjain, Ranthambore, and Mandu.
  • Administration: Issued silver coins and received recognition from the Khalifa (Caliph) in Baghdad, which enhanced his authority.
  • Architecture: Completed the Qutb Minar, built his own tomb in Delhi, and constructed a mosque at Badaun.
Concept Iqtadari System: Introduced by Iltutmish, under this system, territories called Iqtas were given to nobles for revenue collection and administration, helping him secure their support.
Strategic Move In 1221 CE, when Mongol leader Genghis Khan pursued the Shah of Persia to the Indus, Iltutmish diplomatically refused to give the Shah shelter. This brilliant move saved India from a devastating Mongol attack.
Fact The famous book, Tabaqat-i-Nasiri, which reflects medieval life, was written during the reign of Iltutmish by the scholar Minhaj-us-Siraj.

Razia Sultana (1236-1240 CE)

Before his death, Iltutmish nominated his daughter, Razia, as his successor. After briefly being sidelined by her brother Ruknuddin, she became Sultan in 1236 CE.

  • She successfully restored law and order and personally led the army in battles.
  • She took strong action against corrupt nobles.
  • Her close association with Yakut, an Abyssinian slave, angered the orthodox Turkish nobles.
  • Rebellion forced her to marry Malik Altunia, the rebel governor of Bhatinda. Together they marched on Delhi but were defeated and killed in 1240 CE.

Nasir-ud-din Mahmud (1246-1265 CE)

Following the chaos after Razia's death, the influential Turkish nobles, known as the Chalisa, placed Nasir-ud-din Mahmud (Iltutmish's youngest son) on the throne.

  • He ruled for two decades but delegated all administrative work to his capable Prime Minister and father-in-law, Balban.
  • Balban effectively managed the kingdom, controlled revolts, fortified borders against Mongols, and restored peace.
Concept The Chalisa (Group of Forty): A council of forty highly influential Turkish nobles who acted as the major decision-makers in state affairs during the Slave Dynasty.

Ghias-ud-din Balban (1266-1286 CE)

Balban officially became Sultan in 1266 CE. He ruled with a strong authority and iron will, addressing serious threats from rebellious nobles and Mongol invasions.

  • He constructed strong forts along the North-West frontier and maintained a well-equipped army.
  • He ruthlessly suppressed rebellions, including that of Tughril Beg in Bengal.
  • He believed in the divine right of kingship (the king as God's representative on earth).
  • To weaken the nobles, he introduced strict court rituals like sijdah (kneeling and touching the ground with the forehead in front of the Sultan).
Policy 'Blood and Iron' Policy: Balban implemented this harsh policy to deal with rebellions and traitors. It involved strict discipline, ruthless punishments, and absolute central authority to crush any opposition.

3. Decline of the Slave Dynasty

Balban successfully improved law and order, allowing trade and agriculture to flourish. However, after his death in 1286 CE, his successors proved to be weak and inefficient. The dynasty quickly collapsed. In 1290 CE, Jalaluddin Khalji, a noble, overthrew and killed Balban's grandson Kaikubad. This event marked the end of the Slave Dynasty and the rise of the Khalji Dynasty.
