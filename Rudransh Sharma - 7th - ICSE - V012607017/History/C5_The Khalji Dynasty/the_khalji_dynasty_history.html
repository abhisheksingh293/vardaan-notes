The Khalji Dynasty

1. Background & Introduction

After the death of Balban, the Delhi Sultanate entered a period of instability due to weak successors. This disorder opened the door for a new ruling family to seize power—the Khaljis.

The Khaljis were originally Turks who had settled in Afghanistan. They adopted Afghan customs and manners before Mongol invasions pushed them into Hindustan. They established the second dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate, ruling from 1290 CE to 1320 CE.

2. Jalaluddin Firoz Khalji (1290-1296 CE)

Jalaluddin Firoz Khalji was the founder of the Khalji Dynasty. He ascended the throne in 1290 CE at the advanced age of seventy. He inherited a completely disordered kingdom where the authority of the Sultan was no longer feared or respected.

  • To win over the nobles, he distributed wealth brought from Devagiri by his nephew, Alauddin.
  • Despite his generosity, he was assassinated by his own nephew and son-in-law, Alauddin Khalji, who subsequently captured the throne.

3. Alauddin Khalji (1296-1316 CE)

Alauddin had earlier served as the governor of Awadh and Kara under Jalaluddin. After executing his uncle, he became the Sultan in 1296 CE. His reign is famous for extreme military expansion and strict administrative reforms.

Military Expansion & Conquests

Alauddin Khalji's empire was the largest in India since the time of Ashoka. It stretched from the Brahmaputra in the East to the Arabian Sea in the West, and from the Himalayas in the North up to Madurai in the South.

  • North India: He conquered Gujarat and Malwa in 1297 CE. He also successfully captured Ranthambore and Chittor in Rajasthan.
  • South India: He launched major expeditions into the Deccan and the far South, capturing Dwarasamudra and Madurai.
  • Mongol Defense: Alauddin faced over a dozen Mongol invasions. He responded by strengthening border forts and assigning defence duties to highly capable generals like Zafar Khan.
Important Alauddin's successful South Indian campaigns were led by his trusted general, Malik Kafur. Also known as 'hazar dinari', Kafur was bought as a slave for a thousand dinars and rose to become a leading military commander.

Realising that the South was too far off to be ruled directly from Delhi, Alauddin made the southern rajas his vassals (subordinate rulers) and demanded heavy annual tributes from them to maintain his vast army.

Administration & Control

Alauddin was a strict and highly efficient administrator. To prevent conspiracies and revolts among the nobility, he took extreme measures:

  • He curbed the power of nobles and took back all lands and gifts previously granted to them.
  • Social gatherings, extravagant marriages, and the public drinking of wine were strictly discouraged.
  • A strong, wide-reaching network of spies was established to report any signs of rebellion directly to the Sultan.
Concept Military Reforms: To strengthen his massive standing army, Alauddin introduced Dagh (the branding of horses to prevent substitution) and Chehra (detailed identity cards for soldiers).

Economic Reforms

To maintain his large standing army without increasing the soldiers' salaries, Alauddin implemented a series of severe economic and market reforms.

  • Special officers were appointed to collect taxes directly in cash.
  • The state's share of agricultural produce was raised from one-third to half, particularly in the fertile Doab region (between the Ganga and Yamuna rivers).
  • Market Control: Essential goods had rigidly fixed prices. Markets were supervised by officials, grain was stored in government granaries to deal with shortages, and harsh punishments were given to defaulters and hoarders.

Cultural Contributions & Architecture

Despite his ruthless policies, Alauddin's reign was marked by significant cultural growth. Though religious-minded, he ensured that religion did not interfere with state affairs.

  • He built several grand structures including the Alai Darwaza (gateway to the Qutb Minar), a madrasa near the Qutb Minar, the Siri Fort, the Palace of a Thousand Pillars, and Hauz Khas (a royal water reservoir).
Fact Alauddin patronised many scholars and poets, the most famous being Amir Khusrau. Khusrau was a renowned poet and musician, widely celebrated as the 'Father of Qawwali'.

4. Decline of the Dynasty

After Alauddin's death in 1316 CE, his successors were unable to hold the vast empire together. His son, Qutb-ud-din Mubarak Shah, ruled for four years but proved to be weak and incompetent. This instability led to the ultimate fall of the Khalji Dynasty in 1320 CE, paving the way for the rise of the Tughluq Dynasty.
