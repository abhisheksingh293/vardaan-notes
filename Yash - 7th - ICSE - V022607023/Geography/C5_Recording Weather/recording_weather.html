Recording Weather

1. Introduction to Meteorology

Weather significantly affects our daily lives, influencing how we plan our holidays, our food habits, and our clothing. To understand and predict these conditions, we study Meteorology, which is the scientific study of weather and climate.

Fact Scientists who collect weather data using various instruments to prepare weather reports are known as Meteorologists. The headquarters of all meteorological stations in India is located at Pune in Maharashtra.

2. Elements of Weather & Their Measurement

Weather is composed of several elements. Meteorologists use specific instruments housed in weather stations to continuously record data for these elements.

A. Air Temperature

Temperature is recorded using the Six's Maximum and Minimum Thermometer. It consists of a U-shaped glass tube with two glass bulbs containing alcohol and mercury. As temperature changes, these liquids expand and contract, moving metal indicators to register the highest and lowest temperatures of the day.

  • Temperatures are measured using two main scales: Celsius (°C) and Fahrenheit (°F).
  • Diurnal range of temperature: The difference between the maximum and minimum temperature recorded during the day.
  • Annual range of temperature: The difference between the lowest and highest temperature of a year.
Concept Stevenson Screen: To obtain accurate "shade temperatures" without the influence of direct sunlight or precipitation, thermometers are placed inside a Stevenson Screen. It is a wooden box, painted white, placed on a raised stand, with louvered sides that prevent direct heat radiation but allow air to circulate freely.

B. Atmospheric Pressure

Air has weight and exerts pressure on the Earth's surface. This is measured using a Barometer. The unit of measurement for pressure is millibars. The normal atmospheric pressure at sea level is 1013 millibars.

  • Mercury Barometer: Accurate but large and not very portable as it contains liquid mercury.
  • Aneroid Barometer: A convenient, portable metal box with a partial vacuum. It expands and contracts based on pressure, moving a pointer.
  • Barograph: Used to record air pressure continuously on a paper graph using a pen instead of a pointer.

C. Humidity

Humidity refers to the amount of water vapour present in the air. We measure relative humidity using a Hygrometer (also known as a Wet and Dry Bulb Thermometer).

  • The dry bulb thermometer measures the normal air temperature.
  • The wet bulb thermometer is wrapped in wet muslin constantly dipped in water.
  • The difference in readings between the two determines the relative humidity. A greater difference means lower humidity (drier air). If there is no difference, relative humidity is 100%, indicating the air is fully saturated and rain is likely.

D. Clouds

Unlike other elements, cloud cover is measured by direct observation rather than mechanical instruments. Meteorologists record the expanse of clouds in the sky in units called oktas (fractions of the sky covered).

E. Rainfall (Precipitation)

The amount of rain that falls over a given area is measured using a Rain Gauge. The standard unit of measurement is millimetres.

  • It consists of a metal cylinder fitted with a metal funnel leading into a collecting vessel.
  • The self-recording instrument for measuring rainfall continuously is called a Pluviograph.
Important Precaution: A rain gauge must be placed in an open grass area, far away from obstructions like trees or tall buildings. This prevents water dripping from leaves or roofs from entering the gauge and causing inaccurate readings.

F. Wind

Wind has two main characteristics that must be recorded: direction and speed.

  • Wind Direction: Measured using a Wind Vane (or weathercock). The arrow always points in the direction from which the wind is blowing.
  • Wind Speed: Measured using an Anemometer. It consists of three or four cups mounted on a mast that rotate with the wind. Speed is measured in knots or km/h on the Beaufort scale, developed by Sir Francis Beaufort.

G. Sunshine

A Sunshine Recorder (or sundial) is used to record the amount of sunshine at a given location. It uses a glass sphere that concentrates the sun's rays to scorch a mark on a time sheet, recording the total hours of sunlight in a day.

3. Maps and Weather Forecast

Meteorological data collected from satellites, radars, and weather stations is processed by computers and represented on weather maps. Forecasts are essential for farmers, sailors, fishermen, and the general public to plan activities and safeguard lives.

Isohyets and Isotherms

To easily read weather maps, meteorologists use isolines, which are lines linking different places that share a common value (the prefix 'iso' means equal).

  • Isohyets: Lines joining places having the same amount of rainfall over a given period.
  • Isotherms: Lines joining places having the same temperature (usually adjusted to sea level equivalent).

4. Glossary of Important Terms

  • Absolute Humidity: The actual amount of water vapour present in a unit volume of air.
  • Relative Humidity: The ratio of the amount of water vapour in the air compared to the maximum amount the air can hold at that specific temperature.
  • Cloud Cover: The portion of the sky covered with clouds.
