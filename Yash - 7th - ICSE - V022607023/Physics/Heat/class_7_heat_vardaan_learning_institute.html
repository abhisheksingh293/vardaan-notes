HEAT

1. Heat and Temperature

You feel a hot gush of air on a sunny day or warmth from a cup of tea. This sensation of hotness is due to heat energy. Heat always flows from a hot body to a cold body until both bodies attain the same temperature (thermal equilibrium).

Concept

Heat: The energy transferred between two bodies in contact due to the difference in their temperatures. It is the total amount of energy in a substance.

Temperature: The measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of a body. It indicates the average kinetic energy of molecular motion.

Fact

Units of Heat:

  • The SI unit of heat is the Joule (J).
  • Other units include the Calorie (cal) and Kilocalorie (kcal).
  • Conversion: 1 calorie = 4.186 J (approx. 4.2 J).

2. Measurement of Temperature

Temperature is measured using an instrument called a thermometer. It is based on the principle that liquids expand on heating.

Thermometric Liquids

Mercury is the most commonly used thermometric fluid because:

  • It is opaque, shiny, and easily visible.
  • It does not stick to the walls of the glass capillary tube.
  • It expands uniformly on heating.
  • It has a wide range of use: freezing point is -39°C and boiling point is 357°C.

Temperature Scales

The three main scales to measure temperature are:

  • Celsius Scale (°C): Freezing point is 0°C; Boiling point is 100°C.
  • Fahrenheit Scale (°F): Freezing point is 32°F; Boiling point is 212°F.
  • Kelvin Scale (K): The SI unit of temperature. Freezing point is 273 K; Boiling point is 373 K. Zero Kelvin (0 K) is called Absolute Zero.
Important

Scale Conversions:

  • Celsius to Kelvin: K = 273 + °C
  • Fahrenheit to Celsius: °C = (5/9) × (°F - 32)
  • Celsius to Fahrenheit: °F = (9/5 × °C) + 32

3. Effects of Heat

When an object is heated, the energy causes its molecules to vibrate more vigorously. This leads to three primary effects:

A. Change in Temperature

Supplying heat increases the temperature of a body, depending on its mass and the material it is made of.

B. Change in State of Matter

  • Melting/Fusion: Solid changes to liquid at a fixed temperature (Melting Point).
  • Boiling/Vaporisation: Liquid changes to gas at a fixed temperature (Boiling Point).
  • Freezing/Solidification: Liquid changes to solid at a fixed temperature by releasing heat.
  • Condensation: Gas changes to liquid at a fixed temperature by liberating heat.
  • Sublimation: Solid changes directly into a gas (e.g., camphor).
  • Deposition: Gas changes directly into a solid (e.g., naphthalene vapours cooling).

C. Thermal Expansion

The increase in the length, area, or volume of a material upon heating is called thermal expansion. Gases expand the most, liquids expand less than gases, and solids expand the least.

  • Solids: Gaps are left between rail tracks to prevent derailment in summer; bimetallic strips bend due to different rates of expansion.
  • Liquids: Thermometers work on the thermal expansion of mercury or alcohol. Note: Water behaves anomalously—it contracts on heating from 0°C to 4°C.
  • Gases: A balloon tied over a bottle mouth inflates when the bottle is heated due to the rapid expansion of air inside.

4. Modes of Heat Transfer

Heat always flows from a higher temperature to a lower temperature. This transfer occurs in three different ways:

Concept

1. Conduction: Transfer of heat through direct contact without the actual movement of molecules. It is the fastest in solids.

2. Convection: Transfer of heat by the actual upward movement of heated molecules. It occurs only in fluids (liquids and gases).

3. Radiation: Transfer of heat without any physical medium. Heat travels as electromagnetic waves in a straight line at high speed.

A. Conduction (Good and Bad Conductors)

  • Good Conductors: Allow heat to flow easily (e.g., Copper, Iron, Silver). They are used to make cooking utensils.
  • Bad Conductors (Insulators): Do not allow heat to flow easily (e.g., Wood, Air, Plastic, Glass). Used for utensil handles, igloos (trapped air), and mud houses to keep temperatures moderate.

B. Convection (Currents in Nature)

When a fluid is heated, it expands, becomes lighter, and rises. Colder, denser fluid moves down to take its place, forming a convection current.

  • Sea Breeze: During the day, land heats faster than the sea. Hot air rises from land, and cool air from the sea rushes in.
  • Land Breeze: At night, land cools faster. Hot air over the sea rises, and cool air from the land moves toward the sea.
  • Household Uses: Air conditioners are placed near the ceiling so dense cool air sinks, while heaters are kept on the floor so hot air rises.

C. Radiation (Absorbers and Reflectors)

All hot objects radiate heat. The amount of heat absorbed or radiated depends on the colour and texture of the surface:

  • Dark/Black Surfaces: Excellent absorbers and excellent radiators of heat. This is why the bottoms of cooking pans and the insides of solar cookers are painted black.
  • Shiny/White Surfaces: Poor absorbers and good reflectors of heat. This is why light-coloured clothing is preferred during summer.

5. The Thermos Flask

Invented by James Dewar, the thermos flask is a device that minimizes heat transfer through all three modes, keeping hot liquids hot and cold liquids cold.

  • Double-walled vacuum: The vacuum between the glass walls prevents heat loss by conduction and convection.
  • Glass and insulating cork: Being bad conductors, they further prevent conduction.
  • Silvered shiny walls: The reflective inner surfaces prevent heat loss by radiation.
