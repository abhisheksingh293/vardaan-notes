Atomic Structure

1. Introduction to Atoms

The atoms of a particular element are all of one type and different from the atoms of every other element. All atoms, however, are thought to be made up of certain smaller particles that are common to all elements.

1.1 Atom

The word atom comes from the Greek word 'atomos' which means indivisible. It was coined by Greek philosopher Democritus. An atom is the basic unit of matter. Elements, which are pure substances, are made up of atoms, i.e., an atom is the smallest particle of an element that exhibits the properties of that element.

Fact

Size of an Atom
The size of an atom varies from 0.1 to 0.5 nanometres. (1 nanometre = 10⁻⁹ metres).

1.2 Composition of an Atom

John Dalton originally explained that atoms cannot be broken down by any chemical means. However, later on, scientists proved that an atom is composed of even smaller particles known as subatomic particles. These are:

  • Electrons: Negatively charged particles revolving around the nucleus in fixed orbits or shells.
  • Protons: Positively charged particles present inside the central part of the atom called the nucleus.
  • Neutrons: Particles with no electrical charge (they are neutral), also present inside the nucleus.
Concept

Why is an Atom Electrically Neutral?
In a normal atom, the number of negatively charged electrons is exactly equal to the number of positively charged protons. Because the opposite charges balance each other perfectly, the atom as a whole has no net charge and is electrically neutral.

2. Structure of an Atom

An atom consists of two main parts:

  1. The Nucleus: The small, dense, central core of the atom containing protons and neutrons. Almost the entire mass of the atom is concentrated here.
  2. Orbits (or Shells): The imaginary paths around the nucleus in which electrons revolve at very high speeds.

3. Atomic Number and Mass Number

3.1 Atomic Number (Z)

The atomic number of an element is the number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom of that element. Since an atom is neutral, the atomic number is also equal to the number of electrons.

Atomic Number (Z) = Number of Protons = Number of Electrons

3.2 Mass Number (A)

The mass number (or atomic mass number) of an element is the total sum of the number of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus of an atom of that element. Protons and neutrons together are called nucleons.

Mass Number (A) = Number of Protons + Number of Neutrons

Important

Calculating Neutrons
You can find the number of neutrons in an atom by subtracting the Atomic Number (Z) from the Mass Number (A).
Number of Neutrons = Mass Number (A) - Atomic Number (Z)

4. Electronic Configuration

The arrangement of electrons in the various orbits or shells of an atom is called its electronic configuration.

In general, the maximum number of electrons that an orbit or shell can accommodate is determined by the formula 2n², where 'n' is the orbit number from the nucleus.

  • First Orbit (K shell): n = 1. Maximum electrons = 2 × (1)² = 2.
  • Second Orbit (L shell): n = 2. Maximum electrons = 2 × (2)² = 8.
  • Third Orbit (M shell): n = 3. Maximum electrons = 2 × (3)² = 18.

4.1 Valence Electrons and Valence Shell

The outermost shell of an atom is known as the valence shell. The electrons present in this outermost shell are called valence electrons. This shell determines the combining capacity or valency of the element.

5. Molecules

A molecule is defined as the smallest particle of an element or a compound that is capable of independent existence and shows all the properties of that substance.

Atoms of the same element or different elements combine to form a molecule because independent atoms (except noble gases) are generally unstable.

5.1 Atomicity

The number of atoms present in a single molecule of that particular element is known as its atomicity.

  • Monoatomic molecules: The elements which exist as a single atom. Examples: Sodium (Na), Potassium (K), Helium (He), Neon (Ne).
  • Diatomic molecules: The molecules of elements that contain two atoms. Examples: Hydrogen (H₂), Oxygen (O₂), Nitrogen (N₂).
  • Triatomic molecules: The molecules of elements that contain three atoms. Example: Ozone (O₃).
  • Polyatomic molecules: Molecules containing more than three atoms. Examples: Phosphorus (P₄), Sulphur (S₈).

6. Formation of Ions

When an atom loses or gains electrons to achieve a stable electronic configuration, it becomes a charged particle called an ion.

  • Cation (Positive Ion): Formed when an atom loses one or more electrons. It has more protons than electrons, resulting in a net positive charge. (e.g., Na⁺, Mg²⁺). Metals generally form cations.
  • Anion (Negative Ion): Formed when an atom gains one or more electrons. It has more electrons than protons, resulting in a net negative charge. (e.g., Cl⁻, O²⁻). Non-metals generally form anions.

7. Valency

Valency is defined as the combining capacity of an element. It is the number of electrons an atom of the element loses, gains, or shares to achieve a stable (noble gas-like) electronic configuration in its outermost shell.

  • Electropositive valency: Exhibited by atoms that lose electrons (metals).
  • Electronegative valency: Exhibited by atoms that gain electrons (non-metals).

7.1 Radicals

A radical is a group of atoms of different elements that behave as a single unit and carry a net electrical charge (positive or negative).

  • Positive radicals (Cations/Basic radicals): Carry a positive charge. Example: Ammonium (NH₄⁺).
  • Negative radicals (Anions/Acid radicals): Carry a negative charge. Examples: Hydroxide (OH⁻), Sulphate (SO₄²⁻), Carbonate (CO₃²⁻).
