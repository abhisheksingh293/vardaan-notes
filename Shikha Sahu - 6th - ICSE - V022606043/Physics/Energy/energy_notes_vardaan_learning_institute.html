ENERGY - SIMPLE MACHINES

1. Introduction to Work and Energy

In physics, work is said to be done when a force applied on an object actually displaces it through a certain distance. For example, a horse pulling a cart or a child riding a bicycle does work.

Concept

The ability to do work is called energy. Energy is defined as the capacity of a physical system to perform work.

In activities such as reading, thinking, or studying, there is no displacement or change of position involved. So, no work is done in the language of physics when you are studying!

2. What are Machines?

Machines play an important role in our daily life, making it much easier. They help us to work more efficiently in lesser time.

Concept

Tools and objects that help us to perform the same amount of work with much less effort than if we did the work manually are called machines. A machine is a device which makes our work easier and faster.

3. Simple vs. Complex Machines

A. Simple Machines

A simple machine is a tool that helps us in doing our work more easily and efficiently. Examples include a hammer, a sewing needle, a screwdriver, a knife, a bottle-opener, pliers, and even a broom.

Important

A simple machine has no energy of its own and cannot do work by itself. A machine can do work only when work is done on it.

B. Complex Machines

There are machines in which two or more simple machines work together. These are called complex machines. They can do much more complex work and may transform energy from one form to another. Examples include a wall clock, a sewing machine, a bicycle, and a washing machine.

4. Functions of a Simple Machine

A simple machine helps us in many ways:

  • A machine multiplies force: A heavy load can be raised using a machine by applying even less force (e.g., a screw jack lifting a car).
  • A machine helps to change the point of application of force: The wheel of a bicycle is rotated with the help of a chain by applying force on the pedal.
  • A machine helps to change the direction of the force: A person can easily draw a bucket full of water from a well with the help of a rope moving over a pulley, applying force downwards to move the bucket upwards.
  • A machine can change the speed: In a bicycle, the speed of moving wheels is different from the speed with which you rotate the pedals.
  • A machine enables us to do dangerous and unsafe work: Using fire tongs, we can lift a piece of burning coal from a fire.

5. Terms Related to a Simple Machine

  • Effort (E): The force applied to the machine.
  • Load (L): The force exerted by the machine on an object, on which the work is to be done.
  • Fulcrum (F): A fixed point about which the machine can turn while doing work.
  • Effort arm: The perpendicular distance between the effort point and the fulcrum.
  • Load arm: The perpendicular distance between the load point and the fulcrum.
  • Input energy: The energy supplied to the machine for doing mechanical work.
  • Output energy: It is useful work done or the energy produced by a machine.
Concept

Principle of machine: For an ideal machine, the output energy (output work) must be equal to the input energy (input work).

Formula

Mechanical Advantage (M.A.) is measured by the ratio of the load to the effort.
M.A. = Load (L) / Effort (E)
Since both load and effort are forces measured in newtons, mechanical advantage is a pure number and has no units.

Conditions on which mechanical advantage depends:

  • If effort < load, then M.A. > 1 (Force multiplier).
  • If effort > load, then M.A. < 1 (Speed multiplier).
  • If effort = load, then M.A. = 1 (Changes direction of effort).

Efficiency of a machine (η): In actual practice, no machine is an ideal machine due to friction and the weight of moving parts. Efficiency is the ratio of output work to input work, usually expressed as a percentage.

Efficiency = (Output / Input) × 100%

6. Types of Simple Machines

There are six main types of simple machines: lever, inclined plane, screw, wedge, pulley, and wheel & axle.

A. Lever

A lever is a rigid rod (or bar) which moves freely about a fixed point called a fulcrum (F). The weight to be moved is the load (L), and the force applied is the effort (E).

Principle

Principle of a lever: The turning effect of the effort at the fulcrum is equal to the turning effect of the load at the fulcrum.
Effort × Effort arm = Load × Load arm

Mechanical advantage of a lever can also be calculated as:
M.A. = Effort arm / Load arm

Types of Levers:

  • First class lever: The fulcrum is situated between the load and the effort. Examples: see-saw, scissors, crowbar, pair of pliers.
  • Second class lever: The load is situated between the effort and the fulcrum. Load arm is always shorter than the effort arm (M.A. > 1). Examples: wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle-opener.
  • Third class lever: The effort is situated between the load and the fulcrum. Effort arm is always smaller than the load arm (M.A. < 1, speed multiplier). Examples: sugar tongs, forceps, fishing rod, human forearm.

B. Inclined Plane

An inclined plane is a rigid sloping surface over which heavy loads can be raised or lowered to a certain height or depth. It acts as a force multiplier (M.A. > 1). Examples: ramps in hospitals, sloping roads on hills, playground slides.

C. Wedge

A wedge is a simple machine that has two or more inclined surfaces which taper either to form a sharp edge or a pointed edge. It is generally used to split or pierce materials. Examples: knives, axes, sewing needles, iron nails.

D. Pulley

A pulley is a flat, circular disc (metallic or wooden) with a groove on its edge, capable of rotating around an axle. A rope passes through the groove.

  • Single fixed pulley: Has a fixed axis of rotation. Used to change the direction of the applied force (M.A. = 1). Used to draw water from wells.
  • Single movable pulley: The axis of rotation is not fixed. The load is attached to the pulley. Used to lift very heavy loads (M.A. = 2).

E. Screw

A screw is a spiral (winding) inclined plane wound around a rod. It has grooves called threads. It takes less effort to insert a screw than a nail, and it forms a stronger grip. Examples: corkscrew, screw jack, nut and bolt.

F. Wheel and Axle

An arrangement consisting of two cylinders of different diameters joined together. The larger cylinder is the wheel, and the smaller is the axle. Examples: door knobs, bicycle pedals, steering wheels.

7. Care of Machines

Every machine requires proper care for efficient and longer use:

  • Regularly clean and check for any deficiency.
  • Cover properly when not in use to protect from dust and moisture.
  • Paint iron parts to prevent rusting.
  • Adjust moving parts periodically.
  • Regularly lubricate moving parts to minimize friction and wear and tear.
