LANGUAGE OF CHEMISTRY

1. Chemical Symbols of Elements

Concept

A symbol is the shorthand representation for the atom of a specific element. Each chemical element has its own unique symbol.

In the 19th century, a Swedish chemist, John Jacob Berzelius, systematically assigned the first letter of the name of an element in a capital letter as the symbol for that element (e.g., O for Oxygen). This formed the basis of the IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) system.

Rules for Naming Symbols

  • Each element is denoted by a symbol, usually the first letter of its English or Latin name written in capital. (e.g., Hydrogen is H).
  • When the first letter of more than one element is the same, the symbol is denoted by two letters. The first letter is capital, and the second is small. (e.g., Carbon is C, Cobalt is Co, Calcium is Ca).
  • These symbols also represent exactly one atom of that element.
Fact

Some symbols are derived from Latin or Greek names. For example:
Sodium is Na (Natrium)
Potassium is K (Kalium)
Iron is Fe (Ferrum)
Copper is Cu (Cuprum)
Silver is Ag (Argentum)
Gold is Au (Aurum)

2. Valency and Radicals

Concept

Valency is the combining capacity of an atom of an element (or a radical) with the atoms of other elements or radicals to form molecules.

In modern terms, valency can be defined as the number of electrons lost, gained, or shared by an atom of an element when it combines to form compounds.

  • In Hydrogen Chloride (HCl), Hydrogen and Chlorine share 1 electron. Valency of both is 1.
  • In Water (H₂O), 1 Oxygen atom combines with 2 Hydrogen atoms. The valency of Oxygen is 2.

Variable Valency

Certain elements exhibit more than one valency, known as variable valency. When naming them:

  • The suffix -ous is used for the lower valency state (e.g., Ferrous for Iron(II)).
  • The suffix -ic is used for the higher valency state (e.g., Ferric for Iron(III)).
  • Nowadays, these are represented by Roman numerals in brackets, such as Copper (I) or Copper (II).

Radicals / Ions

Concept

A radical is an atom (or group of atoms) that behaves as a single unit with a positive or negative charge on it. Radicals are also called ions.

  • Basic Radicals (Cations): They have a positive charge. All metallic ions and ammonium (NH₄⁺) are basic radicals.
  • Acid Radicals (Anions): They have a negative charge. Most non-metallic ions (e.g., Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻) are acid radicals.

3. Molecular Formula of Compounds

A molecular formula of a compound is the symbolic representation of one molecule. It shows the number of atoms of each element present.

Writing Chemical Formula (Criss-Cross Method)

To derive the molecular formula of a compound:

  • Step I: Write the symbols of the positive and negative radicals side by side.
  • Step II: Write their respective valency numbers at the top right corner.
  • Step III: Interchange (criss-cross) the valency numbers and write them as subscripts. Ignore signs (+/-).
  • Step IV: Reduce the valency numbers to the simplest ratio if they have a common factor.
  • Step V: If a polyatomic radical receives a number greater than 1, enclose it in brackets (e.g., Ca(OH)₂).
Fact

Example: Aluminum Oxide
Symbols: Al and O
Valencies: Al(3), O(2)
Criss-Cross Formula: Al₂O₃

4. Atomic Mass and Molecular Mass

The mass of an atom is called its atomic mass. Since atoms are extremely small, atomic mass is measured relative to a standard unit.

The standard reference is 1/12th the mass of a Carbon-12 atom. This unit is called the atomic mass unit (amu).

Molecular Mass

The molecular mass is the algebraic sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms present in a given molecule.

  • Example: Sulphur Dioxide (SO₂).
  • Atomic mass of S = 32 amu, O = 16 amu.
  • Molecular mass = 32 + (2 × 16) = 32 + 32 = 64 amu.

5. Chemical Equations

Concept

A chemical equation is the symbolic representation of a chemical reaction using symbols and formulae of the substances involved.

  • Reactants: The starting materials that undergo a change. (Written on the left).
  • Products: The new substances formed. (Written on the right).

Skeletal vs. Balanced Equations

An unbalanced equation, where the number of atoms on the reactant side is not equal to the product side, is called a skeletal equation (e.g., H₂ + Cl₂ → HCl).

Important

Law of Conservation of Mass: Matter can neither be created nor destroyed. It can only be transformed. Therefore, every chemical equation must be balanced.

A balanced chemical equation has an equal number of atoms of each element on both the reactant and product sides (e.g., H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl).

6. Making Chemical Equations More Informative

A simple chemical equation does not tell us the physical state, time, concentration, or heat changes involved. It can be made more informative by adding specific notations:

  • Physical States: Indicate states using (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas, and (aq) for aqueous solution.
  • Energy Changes: Use + heat (Exothermic) or - heat (Endothermic) on the product side. The triangle symbol (Δ) over the arrow indicates heat must be applied.
  • Concentration: Add dil. (dilute) or conc. (concentrated) before acids/bases.
  • Catalysts: Write the name of the catalyst (like Fe or Pt) above the arrow.
  • Gas Evolution / Precipitation: Use an upward arrow (↑) for gas evolved, and a downward arrow (↓) for a precipitate formed.
