CHEMICAL REACTIONS

1. Introduction to Chemical Reactions

Any chemical change in matter which involves transformation into one or more substances with entirely different properties is called a chemical reaction.

A chemical reaction involves the breaking of chemical bonds between atoms or groups of atoms of reacting substances and the rearrangement of atoms, making new bonds to form new substances. This occurs with the absorption or release of energy, normally in the form of heat and light.

Concept

Reactants: The substances that take part in a reaction (i.e., which undergo change).

Products: The new substances formed as a result of the chemical reaction.

Chemical Bond: The attractive force that holds the atoms or ions of a molecule together.

2. Characteristics of Chemical Reactions

Chemical reactions are characterized by specific observable changes:

  • Evolution of a gas: In many reactions, one of the products is a gas. The formation of gas bubbles in a liquid during a reaction is called effervescence.
  • Change of colour: Certain chemical reactions are characterized by a clear change in the color of the reactants.
  • Formation of precipitates: Insoluble solid substances formed during a reaction that settle down at the bottom of the test tube are called precipitates.
  • Change of state: A reaction might start with solid or liquid reactants and end up with gaseous products (and vice versa).
  • Change in energy: Every chemical reaction involves a change in energy, usually in the form of heat and light being released or absorbed.
Fact

Example of Color Change: When a piece of iron is dropped into a blue copper sulphate solution, the blue color fades and eventually turns into a light green solution (iron sulphate).

Example of Precipitation: When silver nitrate is added to sodium chloride, a white precipitate of silver chloride is formed.

3. Conditions Necessary for Chemical Reactions

Chemical reactions require certain conditions to take place effectively:

  • Close contact: Substances must be mixed together to directly come into contact.
  • In solution form: Mixing substances in solution form brings reacting molecules in contact with each other at a faster rate.
  • Heat energy: Some reactions require external heat to begin (e.g., heating iron and sulphur powder to form iron sulphide).
  • Light energy (Photochemical reactions): Reactions like photosynthesis occur only in the presence of light.
  • Electricity (Electrochemical reactions): For example, water decomposes into hydrogen and oxygen when electric current is passed through it.
  • Pressure: Some reactions require high pressure (e.g., forming ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen).
Important

Catalyst: A substance that either increases or decreases the rate of a chemical reaction without itself undergoing any chemical change.

  • Positive Catalyst: Increases the rate of reaction (e.g., Manganese dioxide in the decomposition of potassium chlorate).
  • Negative Catalyst: Decreases the rate of reaction (e.g., Phosphoric acid slows the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide).
  • Promoters: Substances that improve the efficiency of a catalyst (e.g., Molybdenum acting with Iron).

4. Types of Chemical Reactions

Chemical reactions are broadly classified into four main types:

A. Combination (Synthesis) Reaction

A reaction in which two or more substances combine to form a single substance. (A + B → AB)

  • Two elements can combine (e.g., Carbon + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide).
  • An element and a compound can combine.
  • Two or more compounds can combine (e.g., Ammonia + Hydrogen chloride → Ammonium chloride).

B. Decomposition Reaction

A reaction in which a compound breaks up, typically on heating, into two or more simpler substances. (AB → A + B)

Because most are carried out by heating, they are often called thermal decomposition reactions. A compound can break up into elements, both elements and compounds, or simpler compounds.

C. Displacement Reaction

A reaction in which a more active element displaces a less active element from a compound. (A + BC → AC + B)

  • A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution (e.g., Zinc displaces Copper from Copper sulphate).
  • A metal more reactive than hydrogen displaces hydrogen gas from an acid.
  • A more reactive non-metal displaces a less reactive non-metal.

D. Double Displacement Reaction

A chemical reaction in which two compounds in their aqueous state exchange their ions or radicals to form new compounds. (AB + CD → AD + CB)

These reactions are of two main types:

  1. Precipitation Reaction: Two aqueous compounds react to form an insoluble solid (precipitate).
  2. Neutralization Reaction: A base or an alkali reacts with an acid to produce a salt and water only.
Concept

Neutralization Importance: Neutralization is used in daily life to treat indigestion (antacids), oral hygiene (bases in toothpaste neutralize mouth acid), treating insect stings, and treating acidic/basic soils for agriculture.

5. Metal Activity Series

A list in which metals are arranged in a decreasing order of their chemical reactivity is called the metal reactivity series. The most active metal (Potassium) is at the top, and the least active (Platinum) is at the bottom.

Fact

Hydrogen's Position: Although a non-metal, Hydrogen is included in the activity series because, like metals, it loses an electron to become positively charged (H+) in most chemical reactions.

6. Chemical Reactions on the Basis of Energy Change

Depending on whether energy is absorbed or released, reactions are classified as:

  • Exothermic Reaction: Heat is given out, causing a rise in temperature. Examples include respiration, rusting, burning of fuels, and all neutralization reactions.
  • Endothermic Reaction: Heat is absorbed, causing a fall in temperature. Examples include the dissolution of ammonium chloride in water and the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate.

7. Oxides

An oxide is a compound that contains oxygen chemically combined with a metal or a non-metal.

A. Metallic Oxides

Formed by the combination of a metal and oxygen. Most metallic oxides are Basic Oxides.

  • Some basic oxides dissolve in water to produce soluble bases called alkalis (e.g., Sodium hydroxide). Note: All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.
  • Amphoteric Oxides: Certain metallic oxides (like Zinc oxide, Lead monoxide, Aluminium oxide) show both acidic and basic properties depending on what they react with.

B. Non-Metallic Oxides

Formed by the chemical combination of a non-metal and oxygen.

  • Acidic Oxides: They turn moist blue litmus paper red and dissolve in water to produce acidic solutions (e.g., Carbon dioxide, Sulphur dioxide).
  • Neutral Oxides: They do not change the color of indicators and do not react with acids or bases (e.g., Carbon monoxide, Nitric oxide, Water).
