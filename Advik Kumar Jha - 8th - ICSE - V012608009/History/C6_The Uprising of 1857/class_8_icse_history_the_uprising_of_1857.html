The Uprising of 1857

1. Nature of the Uprising

The Uprising of 1857 was a widespread expression of resistance against British authority that shook the foundations of British rule in India. It began with the army but soon spread across northern and central India, involving peasants, soldiers, artisans, nobles, and rulers.

  • British writers (like Sir James Outram and PE Roberts) viewed it as a mere Sepoy Mutiny caused by the introduction of greased cartridges, arguing it lacked mass participation.
  • Indian nationalists (like Veer Savarkar and A. Mehta) called it the 'First War of National Independence' due to the combined participation of Hindus and Muslims united by their shared hatred for British rule.

2. Causes of the Uprising

Political Causes

The expansionist policies of the British East India Company severely threatened the Indian rulers:

  • Subsidiary Alliance: Introduced by Lord Wellesley, it forced Indian rulers to accept the British as the supreme power, maintain British troops at their own expense, and place a British resident in their court.
  • Doctrine of Lapse: Introduced by Lord Dalhousie, this policy dictated that if a ruler died without a natural heir, their kingdom would be annexed. Jhansi was annexed under this policy.
  • Awadh was annexed by Lord Dalhousie on the pretext of misgovernance.
  • The British declared that Bahadur Shah II would be the last Mughal ruler, denying his successors imperial titles or the use of the Red Fort.
  • Pensions and titles of Indian rulers were discontinued. Nana Saheb (adopted son of Baji Rao II) was deprived of his pension.
Concept

Expansionist Policies: Strategies used by the British to systematically swallow independent Indian territories. By using both outright wars (like the Battle of Buxar) and cunning legal tools (like the Doctrine of Lapse), the British eliminated local sovereignty.

Economic Causes

  • Peasants suffered under oppressive revenue policies, excessive taxation, and dominance of zamindars and moneylenders.
  • Artisans and craftsmen faced ruin due to the large-scale import of cheap British goods, which displaced Indian handmade products. Conversely, Indian goods faced heavy duties in England.
  • Confiscation of lands of old zamindars (especially in Awadh) added to anti-British sentiment.
  • The constant drain of wealth and a slow pace of industrialisation caused widespread economic misery.

Social and Religious Causes

  • British interference in customs, such as the abolition of sati and the legalisation of widow remarriage, angered orthodox sections of society.
  • Missionary efforts to convert Indians to Christianity added to the fear of losing cultural identity.
  • Lands owned by temples and mosques were heavily taxed.
  • Racial discrimination: Indians were denied promotions, subjected to humiliation, and traditional caste marks (tilaks, turbans) were banned in the army.

Military Causes

  • Indian sepoys formed the bulk of the British army but were paid much less and given fewer promotions than their British counterparts. An Indian could not rise beyond the rank of subedar.
  • General Service Enlistment Act (1856): Issued by Lord Canning, it forced new recruits to serve overseas, violating Hindu religious norms where overseas travel was considered a caste taboo.
Important

The Immediate Cause: In 1856, the British introduced the Enfield rifle. The cartridges were coated with animal fat (believed to be cow and pig fat), which was deeply offensive to both Hindus and Muslims. Soldiers had to bite off the ends before loading. On 29 March 1857, Mangal Pandey called upon fellow sepoys in Barrackpore to revolt. He was hanged on 8 April, providing the final spark for the Uprising.

3. Main Centres and Leaders of the Uprising

The Uprising formally spread when 85 soldiers in Meerut mutinied in May 1857, freed their colleagues, and marched to Delhi.

  • Delhi: The sepoys proclaimed the ageing Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar as the leader of the Uprising.
  • Jhansi & Gwalior: Led by Rani Lakshmibai and Tatya Tope. The Rani fought fiercely after the British refused to recognize her adopted son as the heir.
  • Kanpur: Led by Nana Saheb (who declared himself Peshwa), supported by Tatya Tope and Azimullah.
  • Arrah (Bihar): Led by an old chieftain, Kunwar Singh of Jagdishpur, who organised armed groups and freed parts of Bihar.

4. Suppression and Causes for Failure

The British responded with extreme brutality. Villages were burnt, and rebels were publicly executed or tied to the mouths of cannons. Top military leaders like John Lawrence, Campbell, Outram, and Havelock were called in. By mid-1858, the Uprising was crushed.

Fact

Why Did It Fail? The Uprising failed to spread across the entire country, remaining limited to northern and central India. It suffered from a lack of centralized planning, uncoordinated leadership, and outdated weapons compared to the sophisticated British arsenal. Furthermore, many native rulers (Scindias, Holkars, Nizam) and educated Indians stayed loyal to the British.

5. Effects and Conclusion

Despite its failure, the Uprising of 1857 was a major turning point in modern Indian history:

  • End of Company Rule: The rule of the English East India Company ended, and administration was taken over directly by the British Crown (Queen Victoria) in 1858.
  • The Board of Control was replaced by the Secretary of State for India.
  • The Governor General was given the new title of Viceroy (Lord Canning was the first).
  • The Queen's Proclamation promised no further territorial annexations and pledged not to interfere in the religious practices of Indians.
  • The army was thoroughly reorganised to prevent future mutinies.

Ultimately, the Uprising sowed the seeds of Indian nationalism, serving as a massive inspiration for future generations in the struggle for freedom.
