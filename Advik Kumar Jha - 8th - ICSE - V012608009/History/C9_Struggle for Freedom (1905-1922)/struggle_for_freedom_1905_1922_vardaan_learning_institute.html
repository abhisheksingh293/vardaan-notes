Struggle for Freedom (1905-1922)

1. Rise of Assertive Nationalism

The last decade of the 19th century was filled with suffering for Indians. Famines devastated large parts of the country, and the British government imposed repressive measures rather than helping the people. This led to a transformation of the freedom struggle from a movement led by elites to a mass-based agitation around 1905.

  • Early Voices: Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay kindled nationalism with his composition Vande Mataram. Aurobindo Ghosh published critiques of British rule titled New Lamps for Old. Dadabhai Naoroji and R.C. Dutt exposed how British economic policies drained Indian wealth.
  • Assertive Nationalist Leaders: Known popularly as Lal-Bal-Pal (Lala Lajpat Rai, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, and Bipin Chandra Pal).
  • Bal Gangadhar Tilak: He famously declared, "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it." He used festivals like Ganesh Utsav and Shivaji Jayanti to unite people and published newspapers like Kesari (Marathi) and Mahratta (English).
Concept

Early vs. Assertive Nationalists: The Early Nationalists relied on prayers, petitions, and peaceful appeals, aiming for self-government within the British Empire. The Assertive Nationalists introduced forceful methods like strikes, boycotts, and mass demonstrations, demanding Swaraj (complete self-rule).

2. Partition of Bengal (1905)

Bengal was the largest province in British India and the epicentre of anti-British sentiments. Lord Curzon, the Viceroy, proposed splitting Bengal to suppress this growing nationalism.

  • The Split: Implemented on 16 October 1905, dividing it into East Bengal (Muslim majority) and West Bengal (Hindu majority).
  • Official Reason: Administrative convenience.
  • Public Reaction: Observed as a day of mourning. Following Rabindranath Tagore's suggestion, it was also observed as a day of unity, with people tying rakhis on each other's wrists to express brotherhood.
  • Annulment: Due to fierce resistance, the British annulled the partition in 1911 and shifted the capital from Calcutta to Delhi.
Important

The Real Motive: The true intention behind the Partition of Bengal was to implement the Divide and Rule policy, aiming to split Hindus and Muslims and weaken the solid body of opponents to British rule.

3. Swadeshi and Boycott Movements

The anti-partition movement birthed two powerful tools of resistance:

  • Swadeshi Movement: Encouraged the use of Indian-made goods ('of one's own country') to promote indigenous industries.
  • Boycott Movement: Urged people to completely reject British products. British cloth and sugar were publicly burnt, and people selling British goods faced social boycotts.

4. The Surat Split (1907)

Ideological differences within the Indian National Congress (INC) reached a breaking point at the Surat Session in 1907.

  • Early Nationalists: Led by Rashbehari Ghosh, they wanted to restrict the boycott movement to Bengal and seek reforms through constitutional means.
  • Assertive Nationalists: Led by Aurobindo Ghosh and Tilak, they demanded complete independence and wanted to expand the boycott to all British-run institutions nationwide.
  • Consequence: The Congress split into two factions. The British responded with harsh crackdowns, jailing Tilak for six years and deporting him to Burma.

5. Formation of the Muslim League (1906) & Morley-Minto Reforms (1909)

The British actively encouraged communal divisions to stall the freedom movement.

  • Muslim League: Established in 1906 under Nawab Salimullah of Dhaka and Agha Khan. Its initial aim was to protect the political rights of Muslims and promote loyalty to the British government.
  • Morley-Minto Reforms (1909): Also known as the Indian Councils Act. It introduced communal electorates (separate electorates for Hindus and Muslims), a direct attempt to divide the two communities. While it expanded legislative councils, their powers remained strictly advisory.

6. First World War (1914) & The Lucknow Pact (1916)

Indian leaders supported the British war effort, hoping it would be rewarded with Swaraj.

The Home Rule Movement

When the British showed no intention of granting freedom, nationalist agitation revived.

Fact

The Home Rule Movement (1916) was launched by two prominent leaders: Bal Gangadhar Tilak (after returning from exile) and Annie Besant (an Irish member of the Theosophical Society).

The Lucknow Pact (1916)

Held in Lucknow, this session was a landmark event for Indian unity:

  • Congress Reunion: Early and Assertive nationalists reunited after the 1907 split, largely due to Annie Besant's efforts.
  • Congress-League Unity: The INC and the Muslim League signed the Lucknow Pact. The Congress accepted separate electorates for Muslims, marking a peak period of Hindu-Muslim unity against the British.

7. Gandhiji's Advent on India's Political Scene

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi returned to India from South Africa in 1915, bringing with him his powerful weapon of Satyagraha (a non-violent method of protest based on truth and readiness to suffer). His early successful local movements included:

  • Champaran Satyagraha (1917): Supported indigo farmers in Bihar against excessive taxes.
  • Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918): Supported textile workers demanding better wages.
  • Kheda Satyagraha (1918): Led peasants in Gujarat demanding revenue suspension due to crop failure.

Gandhiji transformed the nationalist movement into a true mass movement involving peasants, workers, and women. He fought against untouchability (calling the oppressed Harijans) and promoted the charkha and khadi for self-reliance.

8. Tragic Events of 1919

The British implemented policies that caused nationwide outrage following World War I.

Government of India Act (1919)

Also known as the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms. It introduced a system of double government in the provinces (transferred and reserved subjects) but failed to grant self-government, leaving real power with the British Governors.

Rowlatt Act (March 1919)

An unjust law that allowed the British government to arrest anyone without a warrant and detain them without trial. It was met with nationwide strikes and protests.

Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (13 April 1919)

A peaceful gathering assembled at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar to protest the arrest of leaders Dr. Satya Pal and Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew. General Dyer blocked the only exit and ordered his troops to fire upon the unarmed men, women, and children. Nearly a thousand people died. In protest, Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood.

9. Khilafat and Non-Cooperation Movements (1920-1922)

Khilafat Movement

Led by the Ali Brothers (Maulana Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali), it was organized to protest the British treatment of the Sultan of Turkey (the Khalifa) after WWI. Gandhiji saw this as a golden opportunity to unite Hindus and Muslims.

Non-Cooperation Movement (NCM)

Launched in 1920 under Gandhiji's leadership, it aimed to paralyse the British administration peacefully. Methods included:

  • Surrendering British titles and honours.
  • Boycotting elections, government schools, courts, and foreign goods.
  • Massive nationwide strikes (hartals).
Important

Chauri Chaura Incident (1922): The Non-Cooperation Movement was abruptly called off by Gandhiji in 1922. In Chauri Chaura (Gorakhpur), a protest turned violent after police fired on peasants. The angry mob set a police station ablaze, killing 22 policemen. Committed to absolute non-violence, Gandhiji suspended the movement immediately.
