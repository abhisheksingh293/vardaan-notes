Pressure, Winds, Storms and Cyclones

1. Understanding Pressure

Concept

Pressure is defined as the amount of force acting per unit area of an object. The pressure exerted on a surface depends on two factors: the force applied and the area over which it acts.

Formula: Pressure = Force / Area on which force acts

The standard SI unit of pressure is the pascal (Pa), named after the scientist Blaise Pascal. (1 pascal = 1 N/m²).

From the formula, we can conclude that for a given force, the smaller the area of contact, the higher the pressure exerted on a body. Conversely, the larger the area, the lower the pressure.

Applications of Pressure in Daily Life

  • Skis: Skis are flat and long to deliberately increase the area of contact with the snow. This larger surface reduces the pressure exerted by the skier's weight, allowing them to glide without sinking.
  • Tractors: Rear wheels of tractors are made broader than the front wheels to reduce the pressure on the soft soil, preventing the heavy vehicle from sinking.
  • Camels: Camels walk easily on sandy soil because they have broad, flat feet that spread their weight over a larger area, reducing pressure on the sand.
  • Nails: A nail has a pointed tip. When hammered, the force is applied on a very small area at the tip, producing a large pressure that easily pierces wood.

2. Pressure Exerted by Liquids

Liquids and gases also exert force on the walls of their container. The pressure exerted by a liquid has specific characteristics:

  • Liquids exert pressure on the walls of the container holding them.
  • The pressure exerted by water increases with depth. The deeper you go, the greater the pressure from the liquid above.
  • Liquids exert pressure equally in all directions at a given depth.
  • Equal liquid column heights produce equal pressure at the same depth, irrespective of the container's diameter.
Fact

Deep-Sea Divers: Because liquid pressure increases dramatically with depth, deep-sea divers must wear special protective suits to shield their bodies from the crushing pressure of the deep ocean water.

3. Atmospheric Pressure

The envelope of air that completely surrounds the Earth is called the atmosphere. It extends many kilometres up from the surface.

The pressure exerted by the weight of the atmosphere on any object is called atmospheric pressure.

Effects of Atmospheric Pressure

  • Rubber Suckers: When you press a rubber sucker against a smooth surface, you force most of the air out from underneath it. The atmospheric pressure from the outside pushes down on it, keeping it firmly stuck to the wall.
  • Nosebleeds at High Altitudes: As you go higher up mountains, the atmospheric pressure reduces. Mountaineers often suffer from nosebleeds because, at high altitudes, the pressure exerted by their own blood in the body becomes higher than the external atmospheric pressure, causing delicate blood vessels to burst.
Fact

Air pressure is accurately measured using a scientific instrument called a Barometer.

4. Winds and Air Pressure

Concept

Wind is moving air. Air naturally moves from a region of high pressure to a region of low pressure. The greater the difference in pressure, the faster the air moves.

High-speed winds result in lowering of air pressure. This is a crucial scientific principle. For example, if high-speed winds blow over the roofs of a house, and the roof is weak, the low pressure created above the roof (due to the fast wind) and the higher normal pressure inside the house can literally lift the roof off and blow it away.

Expansion of Air: Air expands on heating and occupies more space, making it lighter. Cold air is denser and heavier. Therefore, warm air rises up, creating an area of low pressure, while cooler air from surrounding areas rushes in to fill its place. This continuous cycle sets up convection currents in the air.

5. Storms, Thunderstorms, and Lightning

Thunderstorms frequently develop in hot, humid tropical areas like India. The rising temperatures produce strong upward rising winds. These winds carry water droplets upwards, where they freeze and fall down again. The swift, violent movement of the falling water droplets along with the rising air creates lightning and sound.

Lightning is a sudden jump of electric charges through the air. This electric discharge can occur between two oppositely charged clouds or between a charged cloud and the ground.

  • Lightning Conductor: This is a safety device installed at the highest point of a building to protect it from the damaging effects of lightning. It typically consists of a metallic rod connected to a copper plate buried deep in the earth, which safely routes the electric charge into the ground.

6. Cyclones

A cyclone is a huge and powerful storm. It is a very low-pressure system accompanied by very high-speed winds revolving around a central point. Factors like wind speed, wind direction, temperature, and humidity contribute heavily to the development of cyclones.

The central, calm area of this swirling storm is called the 'eye' of the cyclone. While the eye is calm, the heavy clouds and violent winds surrounding it cause massive destruction.

Destruction Caused by Cyclones

Cyclones cause severe damage to houses, electric poles, telecommunication systems, and crops, leading to massive loss of life and property. The intense rainfall causes flooding, which pollutes sources of drinking water and can trigger outbreaks of waterborne diseases like dysentery and typhoid.

Important

Safety Precautions:
• Do not take shelter under an isolated tree during lightning.
• If you are outside during a storm, squat low on the ground with your hands on your knees; do not lie flat.
• During a cyclone warning, follow instructions from the meteorological department and evacuate to safer places if instructed.
