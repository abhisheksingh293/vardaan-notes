Cells and Microorganisms

Concept

Cell: The basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms. All living things are made up of one or more of these units, much like bricks in a building.

1. Discovery and Variety of Cells

Before the invention of lenses and microscopes, the microscopic world was hidden. The cell was discovered by English scientist Robert Hooke in 1665 when he observed slices of cork under a microscope. He noticed tiny compartments resembling a honeycomb and named them "cells."

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, known as the Father of Microbiology, was the first to observe tiny living things like bacteria and blood cells using improved microscopes.

Number of Cells

  • Unicellular: Organisms made of a single cell. Examples: Amoeba, Yeast, Bacteria.
  • Multicellular: Organisms made of multiple cells. Examples: Plants, Animals, Humans.

Size and Shape of Cells

Fact

The size of cells varies from a micrometre (millionth of a metre) to a few centimetres. The yolk of an ostrich's egg is the single largest cell known in the living world!

The shape of a cell is related to its specific function:

  • Irregular shape: Amoeba and White Blood Cells (WBCs).
  • Spindle-shaped: Muscle cells (long and thin).
  • Long and branched: Nerve cells (enables them to send messages over long distances).
  • Plant cells: Can be rectangular, oval, or bean-shaped (like guard cells).

2. Levels of Organisation

In a living organism, biological organisation follows a specific hierarchy:

Cell ➔ Tissue ➔ Organ ➔ Organ System ➔ Organism

  • Tissue: A group of similar cells performing a specific function.
  • Organ: Different tissues organised together.
  • Organ System: Several organs working together.

3. Cell Structure and Function

A typical cell consists of three main basic parts:

1. Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane)

The thin, outer covering of the cell. It is present in both plant and animal cells. It is semi-permeable (allows selective movement of substances), living, and provides shape and protection.

Note: Plant cells have an additional rigid, non-living covering made of cellulose called the Cell Wall.

2. Nucleus

The spherical-shaped structure generally present in the centre. It is the control centre of the cell that regulates all activities and growth.

3. Cytoplasm

A jelly-like (gelatinous) liquid that occupies the space between the cell membrane and the nucleus. Most chemical reactions occur here. It contains tiny components called Organelles:

  • Vacuole: A fluid-filled sac. Plant cells have a single large vacuole that provides turgidity and stores substances. Animal cells usually lack them, or they are very small.
  • Mitochondria: The powerhouse of the cell. Responsible for cellular respiration and energy release.
  • Plastids: Found only in plant cells.
    • Leucoplasts: Colourless (store food in roots/stems).
    • Chromoplasts: Coloured (found in flowers and fruits).
    • Chloroplasts: Green pigment (chlorophyll) responsible for photosynthesis.

4. Comparison of Plant and Animal Cells

By observing an onion peel (plant) and human cheek cells (animal) under a microscope, scientists have identified clear distinctions:

Cell Structure Plant Cell Animal Cell
Cell Wall Present (rigid) Absent
Cell Membrane Present Present
Nucleus Present (located at the periphery) Present (located at the centre)
Vacuole One large central vacuole present Absent or numerous small vacuoles
Plastids Present (e.g., Chloroplasts) Absent

5. What are Microorganisms?

Microorganisms (or microbes) are tiny living things that can only be seen under a microscope. They live everywhere: in water, soil, air, and inside our bodies. They are primarily identified as:

  • Protozoa: e.g., Amoeba (irregular shape, false feet), Paramoecium (slipper-shaped).
  • Algae: e.g., Spirogyra, Chlorella.
  • Fungi: e.g., Yeast (unicellular), Bread mould (multicellular).
  • Bacteria: e.g., Lactobacillus (rod-shaped).
Important

Viruses: Viruses are unique because they are neither strictly living nor non-living. They only begin to multiply upon entering the body of a host cell. (e.g., Bacteriophage).

6. Microorganisms and Us (Useful Microbes)

While some microbes cause diseases, many are incredibly useful to humans and the environment:

  • Curdling of Milk: A bacterium called Lactobacillus helps convert lactose in milk to lactic acid, turning milk into curd.
  • Cooking and Baking: Yeast (a fungus) is used to make bread, cakes, and idlis. During respiration, yeast reproduces rapidly and releases carbon dioxide gas, making the dough rise and become fluffy.
  • Increasing Soil Fertility: Rhizobium bacteria (found in the root nodules of leguminous plants) fix atmospheric nitrogen into usable nitrates. They are known as biological nitrogen fixers.
  • Cleaning the Environment: Bacteria and fungi act as decomposers, breaking down dead plant and animal matter into simple inorganic substances (manure).
  • Source of Biogas: Anaerobic bacteria decompose wastewater and animal waste in oxygen-free environments, releasing gases like Methane, which is used as a fuel source.
Fact

Superfood & Superbugs: Microalgae like Spirulina are rich in protein and Vitamin B12. Indian-American scientist Dr. Ananda Mohan Chakrabarty developed a "superbug" (a strain of Pseudomonas bacteria) that can consume oil spills and toxic pollutants!
