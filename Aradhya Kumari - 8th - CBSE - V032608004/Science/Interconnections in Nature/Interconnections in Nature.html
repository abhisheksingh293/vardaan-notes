INTERCONNECTIONS IN NATURE

1. Habitat and its Components

A habitat is a place where an organism lives. Different habitats provide different conditions for organisms to live and survive, as all plants and animals in a habitat interact with each other and adapt to their surroundings.

  • Biotic components: The living components of the environment, which include plants, animals, and microorganisms.
  • Abiotic components: The physical, non-living components of the environment, including air, water, sunlight, soil, and temperature.

Levels of Organization in an Ecosystem

  • Population: A group of the same type of living organisms living in a habitat at a given place and time.
  • Community: Different populations that share a common habitat, interact with each other, and depend on one another for survival.
Concept Ecosystem: An ecosystem is a community of living organisms (biotic components) and their physical environment (abiotic components) interacting with each other. Ecosystems can be aquatic (ponds, lakes, oceans) or terrestrial (forests, farms).

2. Interactions Among Components

Biotic and abiotic components are constantly interacting with each other for survival. Animals depend on plants for food and oxygen (the end product of photosynthesis), while plants depend on animals for carbon dioxide (the end product of respiration) and help in seed dispersal.

Who Eats Whom?

  • Autotrophs (Producers): Plants are called producers because they prepare their own food through the process of photosynthesis.
  • Heterotrophs (Consumers): Animals that cannot prepare their own food and depend on plants or other organisms.
  • Herbivores: Primary consumers that depend only on plants for food (e.g., cow, deer).
  • Carnivores: Secondary or tertiary consumers that eat other animals (e.g., lions, tigers).
  • Omnivores: Animals that eat both plants and animals (e.g., dog, crow, bear).

Food Chains and Webs

  • Food Chain: A linear sequence of organisms through which nutrients and energy pass as one organism eats another.
  • Food Web: Several food chains that are interlinked to form a complex network in an ecosystem.

3. Waste Management in Nature

  • Decomposers: Microorganisms like bacteria and fungi that decompose complex substances in dead plants and animals into simpler ones. This process, called decomposition, helps return important nutrients to the soil.
  • Saprotrophs: Organisms like mushrooms that grow on dead plants or trees during the rainy season and play an important role in decomposition.
  • Scavengers: Animals that eat dead and decaying animals. They are often called natural scavengers as they help keep the environment clean.

4. Types of Relationships in an Ecosystem

Organisms compete for resources like food, water, sunlight, and space, which keeps the ecosystem balanced by controlling population size.

  • Mutualism: A relationship in which both organisms benefit. For example, honeybees take nectar from flowers and, in return, help the flowers undergo pollination.
  • Commensalism: A relationship in which one organism benefits while the other is not affected. For example, orchids growing on trees receive nutrition while the tree branch remains unaffected.
  • Parasitism: A relationship in which one organism benefits while the other is harmed. For example, ticks feed on a dog's blood, causing the dog to suffer from skin rashes and irritation.
Important One Change Leads to Another: An ecosystem remains stable only when interactions among organisms keep populations balanced. For instance, in the 1980s, the large-scale export of Indian bullfrog legs led to a decline in frog populations. This caused a rise in agricultural pests, forcing farmers to use harmful pesticides that damaged soil and water quality. The Indian government eventually banned the export of frog legs to prevent further ecological damage.

5. Human Impact and Conservation

The balance of nature is disturbed when natural resources are overused or misused. Ecosystems face threats from deforestation, invasive species, pollution, and unsustainable land use.

  • Protected Areas: To save nature for future generations, India has developed protected areas like national parks (Jim Corbett, Manas), wildlife sanctuaries, and biosphere reserves (Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve).
  • Sustainable Farming: Overusing synthetic fertilizers reduces soil fertility and harms the environment. Farmers are adopting sustainable practices like growing different crops (crop rotation), adding organic compost, using cover crops, and utilizing drip irrigation to protect food security for the future.
Fact The Sundarbans, located where the Ganges and Brahmaputra meet, hosts the largest mangrove forests in the world. These trees absorb carbon dioxide and slow down strong winds during storms. Because of its immense ecological importance, UNESCO declared the Sundarbans a World Heritage Site in 1987.
