THE RISE OF THE MARATHAS

1. Historical Background and Origins

The Marathas emerged as a dominant political force in Indian history during medieval times, significantly altering the balance of power across the country. Originating from the Deccan Plateau, they established a vast empire through strategic warfare and efficient administration.

  • Geographical Roots: The Marathas belonged to the Deccan region, specifically in present-day Maharashtra.
  • Linguistic Identity: They spoke the Marathi language, which has possessed a rich literary tradition dating back to the 12th century.
  • Early Rulers: Prior to the 14th century, the Yadava kings ruled most of Maharashtra from their capital at Devagiri (Daulatabad). This rule was eventually overthrown by the Khiljis of Delhi.

Role of the Bhakti Movement

Between the 7th and 17th centuries, the Bhakti movement laid a strong cultural foundation for the Marathas.

  • Saints from various societal backgrounds composed songs and poems in simple languages that everyone could understand.
  • Prominent saints like Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Tukaram, and Ramdas translated deep philosophical texts, such as the Upanishads and Bhagavad Gita, into Marathi.
  • This spiritual movement helped bring people together, creating a unified society with a strong cultural base.
Important

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj was born in 1630 CE to Shahji and Jijabai. He was born in the Shivneri Fort, earning him the title of a child of the mountains and warriors. His coronation took place in 1674 CE at Raigad Fort, formally establishing him as an independent ruler.

2. Foundation of Maratha Power & The Rise of Shivaji

At the age of 16, Shivaji began capturing neglected forts, strengthening their defenses, and laying the groundwork for an independent kingdom known as Swarajya.

Military Tactics and Expansion

  • Guerrilla Warfare (Ganimi Kava): Shivaji mastered guerrilla tactics, using small, swift units to launch surprise attacks from hills and narrow passes. This earned his troops the nickname "Mountain Rats" by the Mughals.
  • The Pratapgad Victory: A defining moment where Shivaji defeated the Bijapur general Afzal Khan using concealed armor and tiger claws (Wagh Nakh), demonstrating his exceptional planning and intelligence.

Conflicts with the Mughal Empire

  • Shaista Khan Episode: Shivaji led a daring night raid against Mughal general Shaista Khan, forcing him to flee Maharashtra.
  • Sacking of Surat: To retaliate against Mughal aggression, Shivaji attacked the wealthy port city of Surat but strictly avoided harming places of worship.
  • Treaty of Purandar (1665): After a setback against Rajput general Jai Singh, Shivaji was compelled to sign this treaty, surrendering a significant part of his kingdom to the Mughals.
  • Escape from Agra: Imprisoned by Aurangzeb in Agra, Shivaji and his son Sambhaji cleverly escaped by hiding inside large baskets meant for sending gifts.

3. Maratha Administration

The administrative framework established by Shivaji evolved into one of the most efficient systems in early modern India.

Civilian Administration (Ashta Pradhana Mandala)

The king was the supreme authority, advised by a council of eight key ministers known as the Ashta Pradhana Mandala.

  • Peshwa: Prime Minister.
  • Amatya: Finance Minister.
  • Sachiv: Secretary.
  • Mantri: Intelligence Minister.
  • Senapati: Commander-in-Chief.
  • Sumant: Minister of Foreign Affairs.
  • Nyayadhish: Chief Justice.
  • Panditrao: Head of Religious Affairs.

Revenue System

  • Shivaji replaced the Jagirdari system with the Ryotwari system, enabling farmers to pay taxes directly to the state, eliminating corrupt middlemen.
  • Revenue assessment followed Malik Ambar's Kathi method, using a standard measuring rod.
Concept

Chauth and Sardeshmukhi: A distinctive taxation system where neighboring kingdoms were required to pay Chauth (25% tax) and Sardeshmukhi (an additional 10% tax) as protection levies. This heavily strengthened the Maratha economy.

Military and Naval Structure

  • Cavalry: Divided into Bargirs (state-provided equipment) and Shiledars (self-maintained equipment).
  • Forts: The primary source of Maratha strength, serving as bases for guerrilla warfare and protecting trade routes.
  • Navy: Built to protect the western coastline against the Portuguese, British, and pirates. Kanhoji Angre became a legendary naval commander, earning the title "Samudra Senapati" (Admiral of the Sea).

4. The Marathas After Shivaji

After Shivaji's death in 1680, his son Sambhaji became the Chhatrapati, followed by Rajaram. Despite intense Mughal campaigns led by Aurangzeb, the Marathas fiercely defended their territory.

Fact

Maharani Tarabai's Deposit Locker System: During her leadership against Aurangzeb, Maratha fort commanders would pretend to surrender during the rainy season and accept bribes from the Mughals. After the Mughals refilled the forts with supplies, Tarabai would creatively recapture them, ensuring the Maratha treasury remained strong.

  • Rise of the Peshwas: Over time, real administrative power shifted from the Chhatrapati to the Peshwa (Prime Minister). Leaders like Peshwa Bajirao I and Nanasaheb rapidly expanded Maratha influence across India.
  • Ahilyabai Holkar: A revered female ruler of Indore who rebuilt numerous temples across India (like Kashi Vishwanath), improved agriculture, and established peace in her kingdom.
  • Decline: Following a major defeat at the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761, and subsequent Anglo-Maratha Wars, the British eventually took control of the Maratha territories by 1818.

5. In Focus: Thanjavur

Thanjavur, in present-day Tamil Nadu, became a critical center of Maratha power in southern India.

  • Captured in 1674 by Venkoji (Ekaji), Shivaji's half-brother.
  • It evolved into a hub of cultural blending between Maratha, Tamil, and Telugu traditions.
  • Serfoji II: A notable Thanjavur Maratha ruler who patronized Carnatic music, established the Dhanwantari Mahal for medical treatments, and set up one of the earliest native Indian printing presses.
