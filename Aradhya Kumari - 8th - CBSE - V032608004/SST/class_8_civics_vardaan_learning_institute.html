The Parliamentary System: Legislature and Executive

1. Introduction to Democratic Governance

After independence, India adopted a democratic system of governance. The foundation of this system is the Universal Adult Franchise, which grants every citizen the equal right to vote, regardless of their status or social background. This principle establishes absolute political equality.

In a parliamentary democracy, citizens elect their representatives to the Lok Sabha. The political party or alliance that secures a majority of seats forms the government. The Parliament acts as the supreme law-making body of the country.

Concept

Bicameral Legislature: The Indian Parliament consists of two houses (bi = two, cameral = chamber). This system ensures a balance between national unity and regional diversity, reflecting India's federal structure where power is shared between the center and states.

2. Composition of the Parliament

According to the Indian Constitution, the Parliament is comprised of three integral parts:

  • The President: An integral part of the Parliament. All bills passed by the two houses require the President's assent to become law.
  • The Rajya Sabha (Upper House / Council of States): Represents the interests of the states and union territories. It provides continuity and stability to the Parliament.
  • The Lok Sabha (Lower House / House of the People): Consists of members directly elected by the citizens. It represents the people of India as a whole and plays the key role in forming the government.
Fact

Parliament Buildings: The old Parliament House, built during the colonial era, has been renamed Samvidhan Sadan. To meet modern, technological needs, the new Parliament building, inaugurated in 2023, is known as Sansad Bhavan.

Presiding Officers of Parliament

Both houses require presiding officers to maintain order and smooth functioning during proceedings.

  • Speaker of the Lok Sabha: Elected by Lok Sabha members. The Speaker presides over sittings, maintains discipline, and decides who gets to speak.
  • Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha: The Vice-President of India acts as the ex-officio Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha, guiding proceedings according to parliamentary rules.

3. Functions of the Parliament

The Parliament serves three primary functions: lawmaking, ensuring executive accountability, and maintaining financial control over the government.

A. The Lawmaking Function

Lawmaking is the primary function of Parliament. Proposed laws are introduced as bills. A bill must be approved by both houses and receive the President's assent to become an Act (a law). Bills are heavily discussed, examined by Standing Committees, and amended clause-by-clause before voting.

Important

Journey of a Bill: Introduction in House ➔ Examination by Standing Committee ➔ Detailed Discussion ➔ Voting ➔ Sent to Other House ➔ Presidential Assent ➔ Official Gazette Notification.

B. Executive Accountability

Parliament not only makes laws but ensures they are implemented properly. The Executive (Prime Minister and Council of Ministers) is accountable to the Parliament. Accountability is maintained through:

  • Question Hour: The first hour of a sitting where members ask critical questions to ministers about government actions and policies.
  • Parliamentary Committees: Small groups of MPs that examine government spending, policies, and actions in deep detail.
  • No-Confidence Motion: If the Lok Sabha passes this motion, it indicates a lack of faith in the government, and the Prime Minister and the entire Council of Ministers must resign immediately.

C. Financial Control

The government cannot spend public money or impose taxes without Parliament's approval. The Union Budget is presented annually by the Finance Minister and must be passed by Parliament. Furthermore, the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) independently audits government accounts to ensure public funds are used efficiently.

4. The Union Executive

While the Legislature makes laws, the Executive is responsible for implementing them and running the day-to-day administration.

The President (Head of State)

The President is the constitutional and ceremonial head of the country, indirectly elected by an Electoral College. The President acts mostly on the advice of the Prime Minister, though they possess discretionary powers (e.g., deciding whom to invite to form a government if no party wins a clear majority in the Lok Sabha).

The Prime Minister (Head of Government)

The Prime Minister is the de facto (real) executive head. Appointed by the President, the PM is the leader of the majority party in the Lok Sabha. The PM coordinates ministries, shapes national policies, and advises the President.

Concept

Collective Responsibility: The Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers take decisions jointly. They are collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. If the Lok Sabha loses confidence in them, the entire government must step down.

Civil Servants

A permanent group of officials (bureaucrats) who assist ministers in implementing policies on the ground. They ensure government departments function efficiently.

5. The Judiciary: Role of Checks and Balances

The Judiciary operates independently to interpret and apply laws, ensuring the Constitution is upheld. At the apex is the Supreme Court, followed by High Courts and lower district courts.

  • Guardian of Fundamental Rights: Citizens can approach the courts if their rights are violated by the government or other entities.
  • Checks and Balances: The Judiciary prevents the misuse of power by both the Legislature and Executive, maintaining a constitutional balance in the democracy.

6. Federalism: Legislative and Executive at the State Level

India follows a federal structure, dividing powers between the Union (Central) Government and State Governments based on three specific lists:

  • Union List: Subjects of national importance (e.g., Defence, Currency). Only Parliament can make laws on these.
  • State List: Subjects of local/state importance (e.g., Police, Public Health). The State Legislature makes these laws.
  • Concurrent List: Shared subjects (e.g., Education, Forests). Both Union and State can make laws, but the Union law prevails in case of a conflict.

The State Structure

The state government mirrors the central structure:

  • Constitutional Head: The Governor (appointed by the President).
  • Real Executive Head: The Chief Minister (Leader of the majority in the Vidhan Sabha).
  • Legislature: The Vidhan Sabha (Legislative Assembly - Lower House). Some states have a bicameral legislature with a Vidhan Parishad (Legislative Council - Upper House).

7. Challenges to Effective Functioning

For a democracy to thrive, its legislature must run smoothly. However, modern parliaments face several hurdles:

  • Loss of Productive Time & Absenteeism: Frequent disruptions and members failing to attend sessions weaken informed decision-making.
  • Disorderly Behaviour: Uncooperative conduct and shouting during crucial periods like Question Hour prevent effective accountability.
  • Criminalisation of Politics: The rising number of elected representatives with pending criminal cases raises concerns about leadership quality.
  • Decline in Debate Quality: Discussions often become highly argumentative or politically biased, overshadowing critical public issues.
