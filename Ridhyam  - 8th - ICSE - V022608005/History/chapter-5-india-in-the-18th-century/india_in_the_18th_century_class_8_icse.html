India in the 18th Century

1. Decline of the Mughal Empire

The mighty Mughal Empire, known for its immense military strength and cultural achievements, started to break apart in the early 18th century following the death of Aurangzeb in 1707. The fifty years following his death saw nine emperors, known as the "Later Mughals", ascending the throne in quick succession. The central authority severely weakened, allowing regional rulers to carve out independent states.

The Later Mughals

Muhammad Shah had the longest reign of the Later Mughals (nearly 30 years), but he was a weak ruler overly fond of a life of luxury. His successors, including Ahmad Shah, Alamgir II, and Shah Alam II, failed to prevent the degeneration of the empire. Shah Alam II even joined forces with Mir Qasim and Shuja-ud-Daula but faced a crushing defeat by the English East India Company at the Battle of Buxar in 1764, effectively turning the emperor into a British pensioner.

Causes for the Decline of the Mughal Empire

  • Aurangzeb's Policies: He abandoned the policies of religious tolerance, reimposed jizya (pilgrim tax on non-Muslims), and engaged in continuous, resource-draining wars in the Deccan, alienating the Rajputs, Marathas, and Sikhs.
  • Weak Successors: The Later Mughals were largely incapable, leaving administrative duties to scheming nobles.
  • Defective Law of Succession: The absence of the law of primogeniture (where the eldest son automatically succeeds) led to brutal wars of succession. Powerful nobles became "king-makers" (e.g., the Sayyid brothers).
  • Rivalry Among the Nobles: The nobility was splintered into factions (Iranis, Turanis, and Hindustanis) that constantly plotted against each other, further destabilizing the throne.
  • Weak Military Administration: Soldiers demonstrated loyalty primarily to their immediate mansabdar (commander) rather than the emperor. Discipline collapsed.
  • Economic Deterioration: Agriculture was ruined by endless wars and heavy taxes. Trade, commerce, and handicrafts suffered heavily.
Concept The Jagirdari Crisis
By the end of the 17th century, there was a massive increase in the number of mansabdars requiring jagirs (land grants) as payment. Because adequate jagirs were unavailable, crown land was converted into jagir land, slashing the emperor's revenue. This financial uncertainty weakened the military, as mansabdars could no longer maintain the required troops.
Fact Foreign Invasions
Political instability attracted foreign invaders. The Persian ruler Nadir Shah invaded in 1739, occupying Delhi for 57 days. He plundered massive wealth, including the legendary Kohinoor diamond and Shah Jahan's jewel-studded Peacock Throne. Later, Ahmad Shah Abdali raided India multiple times between 1748 and 1761.

2. Rise of Independent Kingdoms

The disintegration of central authority allowed regional governors and chieftains to assert independence, breaking the Mughal Empire into several semi-independent states.

Awadh

Founded by Saadat Khan (Burhan-ul-Mulk) in 1722. He successfully suppressed rebellious zamindars and reformed land revenue. Awadh witnessed prolonged peace and economic prosperity, giving rise to the distinct Lucknowi culture (known for the Imambaras and arts).

Bengal

Murshid Quli Khan, appointed governor in 1717, gradually made Bengal independent. Alongside successors like Shuja-ud-din, he gave Bengal a long period of peace that promoted trade. However, they failed to recognize the imperialistic threats posed by the English East India Company.

Mysore

Hyder Ali, a soldier who rose through the ranks, overthrew the corrupt ministers of King Chikka Krishna Raj in 1761. He established a modern arsenal in Dindigul with French assistance. He was succeeded by his son, Tipu Sultan, a brave general who modernized the economy, introduced a new calendar, and died fighting the British in 1799.

Hyderabad and The Rajput States

  • Hyderabad: Founded in 1724 by Kilich Khan, popularly known as Nizam-ul-Mulk (Asaf Jah), who consolidated power by keeping the Marathas at bay and establishing an efficient administration.
  • The Rajput States: The most significant state was Amber under Raja Sawai Jai Singh (1699-1743). A reformer and man of science, he founded the city of Jaipur and constructed astronomical observatories (Jantar Mantar) in Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Varanasi, and Mathura.

The Sikhs

Under the tenth Guru, Guru Gobind Singh, the Sikhs transformed into a formidable political and military force. The struggle was carried on by Banda Bahadur. Eventually, the Sikhs organized into 12 rival misls (confederacies). Maharaja Ranjit Singh (Chief of the Sukerchakia misl) later united them to establish a powerful kingdom in Punjab.

Important Treaty of Amritsar (1809)
Maharaja Ranjit Singh signed this pivotal treaty with Lord Minto (British Governor-General). It fixed the river Sutlej as the boundary between the Sikh kingdom and British territories, allowing Ranjit Singh to expand his empire northwards up to Peshawar and Kashmir without British interference.

3. The Marathas

After Chhatrapati Shivaji's death, the Maratha state eventually transitioned into an empire driven by the Peshwas (Chief Ministers). Balaji Vishwanath, the first powerful Peshwa, forced the Mughals to recognize Shahu's rule and grant the rights to collect chauth and sardeshmukhi (taxes) in the Deccan.

He was succeeded by Baji Rao I, who expanded Maratha control over Malwa and Gujarat, and later by Balaji Baji Rao, who shifted the capital to Pune and brought the Maratha Empire to its zenith.

The Third Battle of Panipat (1761)

The aggressive expansion of the Marathas brought them into direct conflict with the Afghan invader Ahmad Shah Abdali. The decisive Third Battle of Panipat (1761) resulted in a catastrophic defeat for the Maratha forces. This defeat ended the Maratha dream of replacing the Mughals as the supreme power in India and cleared the path for the British East India Company to establish its all-India empire.
