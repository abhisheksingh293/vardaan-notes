A Period of Transition

1. Introduction to the Period of Transition

The time gap between the end of the Medieval Age and the beginning of the Modern Age is known as the Period of Transition. However, this period differs from country to country based on its state of development.

  • In Europe, the Modern Period is generally considered to have begun in the 15th century.
  • In India, the Modern Period began much later, roughly in the mid-18th century.

2. Developments in Europe

Several crucial developments in Europe paved the way for the emergence of the modern world:

  • Decline of Feudalism: The socio-economic system of the medieval period declined, leading to new freedoms and the growth of New Learning.
  • Crusades: These holy wars increased contact and trade between Eastern and Western countries, boosting the wealth and prosperity of European nations. Money replaced land as the new measure of wealth.
  • European Contact with Eastern Civilisations: As trade grew, Europeans were inspired by Greek and Roman pasts, reviving their art, architecture, and philosophy.
  • Discovery of New Routes: To increase commercial profits and find Oriental goods without interference, explorers embarked on voyages, discovering new sea routes that changed the course of history.
  • Invention of the Printing Press: By the mid-15th century, the printing press allowed books to be produced in large numbers, making knowledge accessible to the masses.

3. The Renaissance

The term Renaissance means rebirth or revival. It refers to a movement that began in Italy in the 14th century, aimed at reviving the classical heritage of Europe from which it had been separated for centuries.

Concept

Humanism: A fundamental feature of the Renaissance. It refers to a system of thought that considered solving human problems with reason as more important than purely religious beliefs. It shifted the focus of study from spirituality to worldly affairs and the dignity of man.

Key Features of the Renaissance

  • Spirit of Enquiry: Promoted questioning the 'why' and 'how' of things, leading to scientific approaches and explorations.
  • Rationalism: Scholars rejected superstitions and blind authority, advocating rational thinking.
  • Glorification of Human Form: Artists portrayed humans capable of understanding their own happiness, deeply studying anatomy.

Impact of the Renaissance

  • Painting & Sculpture: Artists made realistic artworks. Michelangelo painted directly on plastered walls (frescoes) and sculpted the famous Pieta. Leonardo da Vinci and Raphael were also prominent.
  • Literature: Writers began using local languages instead of Latin, focusing on human life rather than just spiritual themes.
  • Science:
    • Copernicus: Proved the Earth revolves around the Sun.
    • Galileo: Invented the telescope.
    • Isaac Newton: Discovered the laws of gravitation.
    • William Harvey: Discovered the circulation of blood.

4. The Reformation & Counter-Reformation

The Reformation was a 16th-century religious movement launched by Christians against the objectionable and corrupt practices of the clergymen, primarily targeting the Pope.

  • Causes: People were dissatisfied with paying high taxes (like tithes) and buying pardon certificates called Indulgences.
  • Impact: It split the Christian Church into the Roman Catholics and the Protestants. Martin Luther openly criticised the Pope and launched this movement.

Counter-Reformation: A movement introduced by dedicated Christians (like the Popes) to restore the credibility of the Catholic Church. Ignatious Loyola founded the Society of Jesus to win back adherents.

5. The Industrial Revolution

The Industrial Revolution brought a transition from manual, small-scale home production to large-scale machine production in factories. It began in England around 1750.

Fact

Major Inventions: The Industrial Revolution was fueled by groundbreaking inventions, such as the Flying Shuttle (John Kay), the Spinning Jenny (James Hargreaves), the Steam Engine (James Watt), and the Telegraph (Alexander Graham Bell).

Reasons it Began in England

  • England had a large colonial empire to provide cheap raw materials and serve as ready markets.
  • Plentiful deposits of iron and coal fueled new machines.
  • A long period of domestic peace encouraged economic growth.
Important

Negative Impacts of the Industrial Revolution: While it created national wealth, it also led to ruthless exploitation of workers. Men, women, and children worked 12 to 15 hours a day in miserable living conditions, leading to the rise of slums and unchecked capitalism.

6. Imperialism

Imperialism is a system by which a powerful nation establishes its control over another country, exploiting its resources for economic gain. The conquered country becomes a colony.

  • Causes: The need for raw materials, markets for finished products, accommodation of surplus population, and national prestige (the so-called "White Man's Burden").
  • Positive Effects: Imperialist nations built roads, railways, improved sanitation, and introduced new technologies in the colonies.
  • Negative Effects: Destroyed agrarian economies, evicted farmers, ruined traditional cottage industries, and created widespread poverty among the colonised people.

7. Developments in India & Sources of Information

During this period of transition, India witnessed major shifts including the decline of the Mughal Empire, the defeat of the Marathas in the Third Battle of Panipat, and the gradual conquest of India by the British.

Sources for Reconstructing History

  • Primary Sources: Direct evidence such as official records (papers of government agencies), historical works, records of European companies, and archaeological sources (buildings, monuments).
  • Secondary Sources: Works produced using primary sources, involving analysis and interpretation. These include historical works by contemporary historians, novels, and biographies.
