REPRESENTATION OF GEOGRAPHICAL FEATURES

1. Introduction to Topographical Maps

Geography is the study of the inter-relationship between physical features on the earth's surface and human activities. It uses various tools such as globes, models, charts, and topographical maps to study the earth.

Concept

A Topographical Map is a large-scale map which shows elevation, relief, drainage, vegetation, and man-made features like roads, railways, and settlements. These are extensively used for engineering, public works, commercial and residential planning, as well as in defence and navigation.

2. Understanding Relief and Contours

On a toposheet, the study of relief includes a description of mountains, plateaus, plains, slopes of the land, and the highest as well as the lowest points. All these features can be easily identified with the help of contours.

Concept

Contour: An imaginary line joining places having the same height above sea level. It makes it possible to represent three-dimensional shapes on a two-dimensional surface.

Contour Interval: The space between the contour lines representing a set distance.

Basic Features of Contours

  • They are continuous curved lines joining places of the same height.
  • Contours are drawn at a constant contour interval.
  • The pattern of contour lines directly resembles the shape of the land.
  • The value of the contours is written towards the ascending side or directly on the contours themselves.
  • Contours are always shown in brown colour on a map.
Important

The spacing of the contours expresses the steepness of the slope:

  • Steep Slope: Contours are crowded together, indicating a great change in height over a short distance.
  • Gentle Slope: Contours are widely spaced apart, indicating a gradual change in height.

3. Contour Representation of Relief Features

Relief is an essential aspect of physical geography. By observing the arrangement of contour lines, we can identify various landforms:

  • Mountain: An elevation which rises abruptly above the surrounding areas. Contours are closely spaced with increasing contour values towards the inner summits.
  • Conical Hill: A natural elevation smaller than a mountain. Its base is broad and almost circular with a narrow peak. Contours showing a conical hill are almost circular and drawn at regular intervals.
  • Plateau (Tableland): An elevated plain with a relatively level top surface which falls down rapidly at its edges. Contours are concentrated on its sides, while the central part is mostly empty of contours.
  • Ridge: A high, elongated hill represented on the map by elliptical contours. The line of greatest elevation is called the ridge line.

4. Col, Saddle, and Gap

These terms describe different types of passages or depressions found in mountainous regions:

  • Col or Pass: A col is a short, steep-sided depression on a ridge. A passage provided by a col in a mountain is called a pass. It serves as a lowland between two mountain peaks, and roads usually follow these paths.
  • Saddle: When the depression between mountain peaks becomes broad and low, it resembles the shape of a horse's saddle, hence the name.
  • Gap: Generally used for a pass which has been lowered by the action of running water. Gaps are similar to passes but are usually more rugged and difficult to navigate.

5. Settlements on Toposheets

A settlement is a place where people live and interact through various activities such as agriculture, manufacturing, and trading.

Fact

On a coloured toposheet, all types of settlements are shown in red colour. This includes roads, huts, deserted villages, temples, churches, mosques, post offices, and police stations.

Classification by Duration

  • Temporary Settlements: Occupied for a short period of time.
  • Permanent Settlements: Occupied for a long duration.

Classification by Pattern

  • Nucleated or Compact Pattern: Houses are built close to one another along narrow streets. Found closer to water sources, fertile soil areas, river confluences, or industrial sites. They are often known as Modern or Model Settlements.
  • Dispersed or Scattered Pattern: Characterised by isolated dwellings spread out over a large area. These are typical of sparsely populated areas like highlands, regions with poor soils, lack of water, or adverse climatic conditions.
