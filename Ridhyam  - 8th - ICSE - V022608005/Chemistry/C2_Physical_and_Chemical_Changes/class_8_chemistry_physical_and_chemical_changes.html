PHYSICAL AND CHEMICAL CHANGES

1. Introduction

In our daily life, we come across numerous changes taking place in our surroundings. These changes involve one or more substances transforming due to various factors. Examples include the melting of glaciers, formation of curd from milk, ripening of fruits, and rusting of iron.

Matter can be classified based on how it transforms, which brings us to the study of the different types of changes.

2. Classification of Changes

Changes happening around us can be systematically divided into different categories based on their origin, speed, and reversibility.

Natural and Man-made Changes

Concept Natural Changes are those that occur naturally and are not under human control (e.g., changes in seasons, volcanic eruptions). Man-made Changes are those initiated and controlled by humans (e.g., burning fossil fuels, cooking food).

Periodic and Non-periodic Changes

  • Periodic Changes: Changes that repeat themselves after a fixed interval of time. (e.g., occurrence of day and night, heartbeat).
  • Non-periodic Changes: Changes that do not repeat themselves at regular intervals. (e.g., earthquakes, breaking of glass).

Slow and Fast Changes

  • Slow Changes: Changes that take a considerable amount of time to complete. (e.g., growth of a tree, rusting of iron).
  • Fast Changes: Changes that occur very quickly or almost instantly. (e.g., bursting a firecracker, dropping a glass).

Reversible and Irreversible Changes

  • Reversible Changes: Changes that can be undone by reversing the conditions, bringing the substance back to its original state. (e.g., melting of ice, stretching a rubber band).
  • Irreversible Changes: Changes that cannot be reversed. (e.g., souring of milk, burning of paper).

3. Physical Changes

Concept A Physical Change is a temporary change in which no new substance is formed. Only the physical properties (like state, size, or shape) of the substance undergo a change.

Key characteristics of a physical change include:

  • No new substance is formed.
  • The chemical composition of the substance remains entirely unchanged.
  • It is usually a temporary and reversible change.
  • There is no change in the mass of the substance undergoing the change.
  • Generally, no net energy (heat or light) is absorbed or evolved.
Fact Common examples of physical changes include: boiling of water to form steam, melting of wax, dissolving sugar in water, and the magnetization of an iron piece.

4. Chemical Changes

Concept A Chemical Change is a permanent change in which new substances with entirely different physical and chemical properties are formed.

Key characteristics of a chemical change include:

  • One or more entirely new substances are formed.
  • The chemical composition of the original substance is altered.
  • It is generally a permanent and irreversible change.
  • Energy in the form of heat or light is usually absorbed or evolved during the reaction.
  • The mass of individual substances may alter, though the total mass of the system is conserved.
Fact Common examples of chemical changes include: the rusting of iron, digestion of food in our stomach, curdling of milk, and the burning of magnesium ribbon.

5. Simultaneous Physical and Chemical Changes

In certain scenarios, physical and chemical changes can occur together simultaneously.

Important When a candle burns, it undergoes both physical and chemical changes. The melting of the solid wax into liquid is a physical change (reversible). However, the burning of the wax vapors to produce carbon dioxide, water vapor, and heat/light is a chemical change (irreversible).
