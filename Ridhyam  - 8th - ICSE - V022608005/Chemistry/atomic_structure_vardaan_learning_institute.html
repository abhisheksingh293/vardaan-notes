ATOMIC STRUCTURE

1. Introduction

Concept An Atom is the fundamental building block of matter. Initially, it was considered indivisible and the smallest unit of matter that takes part in a chemical reaction.

The concept that matter is composed of tiny, indivisible particles was first proposed by Indian and Greek philosophers.

Fact Indian philosopher Maharshi Kanad proposed that all matter is made of very small particles called 'Parmanu'.

2. Dalton's Atomic Theory of Matter

In 1808, John Dalton published his atomic theory, which served as the foundation for modern chemistry. The main postulates are:

  • Elements are made of extremely small particles called atoms.
  • Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and other properties.
  • Atoms of different elements vary in mass and other properties.
  • Atoms cannot be subdivided, created, or destroyed.
  • Atoms of different elements combine in simple whole-number ratios to form chemical compounds (e.g., two atoms of hydrogen combine with one atom of oxygen to form water, H2O).
  • In chemical reactions, atoms are combined, separated, or rearranged.

3. Discovery of Subatomic Particles

Contrary to Dalton's theory, atoms are not indivisible. They consist of smaller particles known as subatomic particles: electrons, protons, and neutrons.

Discovery of Electrons (Cathode Rays)

In 1879, William Crookes observed that when electricity is passed through a discharge tube containing gas at very low pressure, blue rays are emitted from the cathode. These were called cathode rays.

Fact J.J. Thomson (1897) discovered that cathode rays consist of negatively charged particles, which were later named electrons.
  • Charge: Negatively charged (-1).
  • Mass: Approximately 1/1837 of the mass of a hydrogen atom (considered negligible).
  • Location: Revolve around the nucleus.
  • Symbol: e-

Discovery of Protons (Canal Rays)

In 1886, Eugen Goldstein discovered positively charged rays in a discharge tube, moving in the opposite direction to cathode rays. These were called Canal Rays or Positive Rays, consisting of positively charged particles called protons.

  • Charge: Positively charged (+1).
  • Mass: 1 amu (equal to the mass of a hydrogen atom).
  • Location: Inside the nucleus.
  • Symbol: p+

Discovery of Nucleus

Concept Ernest Rutherford conducted the Alpha Particle Scattering Experiment by bombarding a thin gold foil with fast-moving alpha particles.

Observations & Conclusions:

  • Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil, indicating that most of the space inside an atom is empty.
  • A few particles were deflected by small angles, showing the presence of a positively charged center.
  • A very small number of particles bounced back, indicating that the positively charged mass is concentrated in a tiny, dense, and heavy central core called the nucleus.

Discovery of Neutrons

Fact In 1932, James Chadwick discovered a new subatomic particle with no charge but a mass nearly equal to that of a proton. He named it the neutron.
  • Charge: Neutral (no charge).
  • Mass: 1 amu (almost equal to the mass of a proton).
  • Location: Inside the nucleus.
  • Symbol: n

4. Atomic Models

J.J. Thomson's Model

Proposed in 1904, it is also known as the "plum pudding" model. It states that an atom is a positively charged sphere with electrons embedded in it, like plums in a pudding. The total positive charge equals the total negative charge, making the atom electrically neutral.

Rutherford's Nuclear Model

Based on his scattering experiment, Rutherford proposed that an atom consists of a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the center, with electrons revolving around it at high speeds in circular paths. The radius of the nucleus is about 105 times less than the radius of the atom.

Bohr's Atomic Model

Concept Niels Bohr proposed that electrons revolve around the nucleus in fixed circular paths called orbits or shells, without radiating energy.
  • The shells are denoted by letters K, L, M, N... or numbers n = 1, 2, 3, 4... starting from the nucleus.
Important The maximum number of electrons that can be accommodated in a shell is given by the formula 2n2, where 'n' is the shell number.

For K shell (n=1): 2(1)2 = 2 electrons
For L shell (n=2): 2(2)2 = 8 electrons
For M shell (n=3): 2(3)2 = 18 electrons

5. Atomic Number and Mass Number

Concept Atomic Number (Z) is the number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom.
Mass Number (A) is the total number of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus.
  • In a neutral atom: Number of Protons = Number of Electrons = Atomic Number (Z).
  • Mass Number (A) = Number of Protons (p) + Number of Neutrons (n)
  • Number of Neutrons (n) = Mass Number (A) - Atomic Number (Z)

Symbolic Representation: An element is represented as AZX, where X is the symbol of the element, Z is the atomic number, and A is the mass number.

6. Electronic Configuration

The systematic arrangement of electrons in different orbits or shells around the nucleus of an atom is called its electronic configuration.

Fact Example: Sodium (Na) has an atomic number of 11. Its 11 electrons are arranged as:
K shell = 2, L shell = 8, M shell = 1.
Electronic Configuration = 2, 8, 1.

7. Isotopes and Isobars

Concept Isotopes are atoms of the same element having the same atomic number but different mass numbers (due to a different number of neutrons).
  • Because they have the same number of electrons and protons, isotopes have identical chemical properties but different physical properties (like mass and density).
Fact Examples of Isotopes:
Hydrogen has three isotopes: Protium (11H), Deuterium (21H), and Tritium (31H).
Carbon has isotopes: 126C and 146C.
Concept Isobars are atoms of different elements having the same mass number but different atomic numbers.
  • Because they are atoms of different elements, they have different physical and chemical properties.
  • Example: Argon (4018Ar) and Calcium (4020Ca) both have a mass number of 40 but different atomic numbers.
