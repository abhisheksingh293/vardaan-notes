LIGHT, SHADOWS AND REFLECTIONS

Light is essential for our eyes to see the beautiful world around us. Light can even travel through the vast vacuum of space to reach Earth from the Sun. Let us explore the properties of light, how shadows are formed, and the phenomenon of reflection.

1. Sources of Light

Objects that give out or emit their own light are known as sources of light. We can classify them based on their origin and their ability to emit light.

Concept

Luminous Objects: Objects that produce or emit their own light (e.g., Sun, stars, electric bulb, candle flame). Light from these objects enters our eyes directly.

Non-luminous Objects: Objects that do not produce their own light (e.g., tables, trees, moon, Earth). We see them when light from a luminous object bounces off them and reaches our eyes.

Natural vs. Artificial Sources

  • Natural Sources: Sun, stars, lightning, natural fire.
  • Artificial Sources: Turned-on torch, lighted electric bulb, burning oil lamp.
Fact

Certain living beings like fireflies, glow-worms, algae, and jellyfish can emit their own natural light. This fascinating natural process is called bioluminescence.

2. Rectilinear Propagation of Light

When a light bulb is turned on, light travels to all parts of the room. Experiments show that light always chooses the most direct route.

  • Light travels in a straight-line path. This property is called the rectilinear propagation of light.
  • Ray of light: The path travelled by light in a straight line, represented by a line with an arrowhead indicating direction.
  • Beam of light: A group of light rays.
  • Parallel beam: Rays traveling parallel to each other in the same direction.
  • Divergent beam: Rays spreading out in different directions.
  • Convergent beam: Rays meeting at a single point.

3. Transparent, Translucent, and Opaque Materials

Based on whether light can pass through a material, we classify objects into three categories:

  • Transparent Materials: Allow light to pass through them almost completely. We can see through them clearly (e.g., clear glass, clean air, clean water, cellophane paper).
  • Translucent Materials: Allow light to pass through them partially. We can see through them, but not clearly (e.g., frosted glass, butter paper).
  • Opaque Materials: Do not allow light to pass through them at all. Light is completely blocked (e.g., wood, stone, walls).

4. Shadows and Their Formation

Because light travels in a straight line, it cannot bend around the corners of an object that blocks it.

A shadow is a dark patch formed when an opaque object is placed in the path of light, blocking it from reaching a surface.

Conditions necessary to form a shadow:

  1. A source of light.
  2. An opaque object to block the light.
  3. An opaque surface or screen where the shadow can be obtained.

Characteristics of a Shadow:

  • It is always formed in the direction opposite to the light source.
  • It is always a dark patch, regardless of the colour of the object.
  • It is two-dimensional (2D) or flat.
  • It shows only the outline of the object, not its internal details or patterns.
  • Opaque objects form dark shadows, while translucent objects form lighter/fainter shadows. Transparent objects usually do not cast shadows.
Important

Factors affecting the shape and size of a shadow:

  • Distance between light and object: As the object moves further from the light source, the shadow becomes smaller.
  • Distance between object and screen: As the distance between the object and screen increases, the shadow becomes larger.
  • Orientation of the object: Changing the angle of the object relative to the light source changes the shape of the shadow (e.g., a cylinder can cast a circular or rectangular shadow).

5. Reflection of Light and Plane Mirrors

When you throw a ball at a hard wall, it bounces back. Light behaves similarly when it strikes surfaces.

The bouncing off and change in the direction of light upon striking a surface (like a plane mirror, wall, or water) is called reflection of light. Reflection is the reason we can see all non-luminous objects.

Characteristics of an Image Formed by a Plane Mirror:

  • Erect: The image is upright (same vertical orientation as the object).
  • Same Size: The image is exactly the same size as the object.
  • Same Distance: The image appears to be at the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front of it.
  • Laterally Inverted: The left side of the object appears as the right side of the image, and vice versa. This phenomenon is called lateral inversion.
  • Virtual: The image cannot be captured on a physical screen.

6. The Pinhole Camera

A pinhole camera is a simple lightproof box with a tiny hole on one side and a screen (like butter paper) on the opposite side. It works on the principle of rectilinear propagation of light.

Characteristics of a Pinhole Camera Image:

  • Inverted: The image formed is upside down. Rays from the top of the object travel through the hole to hit the bottom of the screen, and vice versa.
  • Real: Because the image is captured directly on a screen, it is a real image.
  • Same Colour: The image retains the natural colour of the object.
  • Size variation: A smaller image is formed when the object is far from the pinhole, and a larger image is formed when the object is brought closer.

7. Applications of Plane Mirrors

Because of the predictable way light reflects off plane mirrors, they are used in various optical instruments:

  • Periscope: An instrument that uses multiple mirrors to see objects that are not in our direct line of vision (e.g., over a wall). It uses two mirrors placed parallel to each other at 45-degree angles inside a hollow tube.
  • Kaleidoscope: An optical instrument using three or more mirrors joined edge-to-edge to form a tube. When small objects like beads are placed inside, the mirrors produce multiple reflections, creating beautiful, symmetrical patterns used by designers.
