Heat

1. Introduction

In our everyday life, we frequently use the terms 'hot' and 'cold'. When we stand in front of the sun rays or sit near a fire, we feel hot. Similarly, if we touch a lump of ice, we feel cold.

Concept

Heat is a form of energy which causes the sensation of hotness and coldness. Heat always flows from a body at a higher temperature to a body at a lower temperature. It refers to the combined kinetic energy of all molecules moving inside a body.

Temperature is the measurement of the degree of hotness or coldness of an object. Temperature governs the direction of flow of heat.

When two bodies with different temperatures come in contact, heat energy flows until the temperature of both bodies becomes equal, achieving a state known as thermal equilibrium.

2. Units of Heat

Since heat is a form of energy, it is measured in the same units as energy.

  • SI Unit: The standard international unit of heat energy is Joule (J).
  • Common Unit: A commonly used unit of heat energy is calorie (cal) or kilocalorie (kcal).
Fact

1 kilocalorie (kcal) = 1000 calories (cal).

Relation between Joule and Calorie: Experimentally, it has been determined that:
1 calorie = 4.186 J (Approximately 4.2 J).
This means 4.2 J of heat can raise the temperature of 1 g of water by 1°C.

3. Temperature and Thermometers

The degree of hotness or coldness of a body is called its temperature. Ordinarily, we distinguish hot bodies from cold ones by our sense of touch, but it is not a reliable method. The SI unit of temperature is Kelvin (K).

A device used for measuring the temperature of different objects is called a thermometer. Mercury and alcohol are generally used as thermometric liquids.

Types of Thermometers

  • Laboratory Thermometer: Used for laboratory purposes. Its temperature falls automatically when taken out of the hot substance because there is no kink in the capillary tube.
  • Clinical Thermometer: Used for measuring human body temperature. A kink near the bulb prevents the mercury level from falling on its own, allowing the user to read the temperature after removing it from the mouth.
Important

Why use Mercury? Mercury is preferred because it expands uniformly at all temperatures, is a good conductor of heat, does not stick to the glass tube, and is an opaque, shining liquid that is easily visible.

4. Interconversion of Three Temperature Scales

The three thermometric scales are Celsius (°C), Fahrenheit (°F), and Kelvin (K).

  • Celsius Scale: Lower fixed point (ice point) is 0°C. Upper fixed point (steam point) is 100°C. Divided into 100 equal parts.
  • Fahrenheit Scale: Lower fixed point is 32°F. Upper fixed point is 212°F. Divided into 180 equal parts.
  • Kelvin Scale: Lower fixed point is 273 K. Upper fixed point is 373 K. Divided into 100 equal parts.

Formula for conversion:

C / 100 = (F - 32) / 180 = (K - 273) / 100

To convert Celsius to Fahrenheit directly: °F = (9/5 × °C) + 32

Fact

The normal body temperature of a healthy person on the Fahrenheit scale is 98.6°F, which is exactly equal to 37°C.

5. Effects of Heat

Heat energy causes a number of effects in substances:

  • Change in Temperature: When a substance is supplied with heat energy, its temperature rises. When it loses heat, its temperature falls.
  • Thermal Expansion and Contraction: Substances generally expand on heating (thermal expansion) and contract on cooling. This expansion can be linear (length), superficial (area), or cubical (volume).
    • Example: Gaps are left between railway tracks to allow for linear expansion during summer.
  • Chemical Change: Many chemical reactions take place when reactants are heated. Burning (combustion) is a chemical change where heat breaks bonds to form new products.

Change of State

Heat may cause a change in the physical state of a substance.

Concept

Melting (Fusion): Solid changes into a liquid by absorption of heat.

Freezing (Solidification): Liquid changes into a solid by release of heat.

Boiling (Vaporisation): Liquid changes into a gas at a fixed temperature by absorption of heat.

Condensation: Gas changes into a liquid by release of heat.

Sublimation: A solid directly changes into a gaseous state on heating without becoming a liquid (e.g., Naphthalene, Camphor).

6. Transfer of Heat

Heat flows from a body at a higher temperature to a body at a lower temperature through three distinct modes: Conduction, Convection, and Radiation.

I. Conduction

Conduction is the process of transfer of heat from the hotter end to the colder end of a solid, without any actual visible movement of the material particles (molecules). The vibrating particles collide and transfer energy.

  • Conductors: Materials which allow heat energy to pass easily (e.g., Copper, Silver, Iron). They are used for making cooking utensils and boiler bases.
  • Insulators: Materials which do not allow heat to easily pass (e.g., Wood, Plastic, Glass, Air). Used for utensil handles, winter clothing (trapping air), and igloos.

II. Convection

Convection is the mode of heat transfer in fluids (liquids and gases) wherein the molecules actually move from one part to the other, creating continuous convection currents.

  • Land and Sea Breeze: Natural convection currents in coastal areas regulate temperature. During the day, hot air over land rises, and cool air from the sea rushes in (Sea Breeze). At night, the opposite happens (Land Breeze).
  • Ventilation: Warm air we breathe out is lighter and rises to escape through ventilators near the ceiling, drawing in fresh air through doors/windows.

III. Radiation

Radiation is the fastest mode of heat transfer directly from a hot body to a cold body without heating the space in between. No medium is required for radiation. Heat from the Sun reaches Earth through the vacuum of space via radiation.

Important

Absorption and Reflection: Black and dark-coloured bodies are good absorbers and good radiators of radiant heat. White and light-coloured bodies are poor absorbers (good reflectors) and poor radiators.

7. Thermos Flask (Dewar Flask)

A thermos flask is a special insulated flask invented by Sir James Dewar that keeps a hot liquid hot and a cold liquid cold for hours by minimizing heat loss through all three modes:

  • Conduction is minimized by using a plastic outer case, a non-conducting stopper (cork/plastic), and a glass double wall.
  • Convection is minimized by creating a vacuum in the space between the double glass walls.
  • Radiation is minimized by silvering the inner surfaces of the double walls, which reflects radiant heat back into the flask.
