Light

1. Reflection of Light

The world around us is visible only when light shines on objects and is reflected into our eyes. Without light, we cannot see.

Concept

Reflection of light is the phenomenon of bouncing back of light into its original medium after striking the surface of an object.

Types of Reflection

  • Regular Reflection: Occurs when a parallel beam of light falls on a smooth, highly polished surface (like a mirror). Entire light gets reflected in a definite direction. Parallel rays remain parallel. It forms clear images.
  • Irregular (Diffused) Reflection: Occurs when light falls on a rough, unpolished surface (like a wall or paper). Light gets scattered in different directions. It does not form clear images, but allows us to see everyday objects.

Terms Related to Reflection

  • Incident ray: A ray of light falling on a reflecting surface.
  • Reflected ray: The ray of light that bounces off the reflecting surface.
  • Normal: An imaginary line drawn perpendicular (at 90°) to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence.
  • Angle of incidence (i): The angle between the incident ray and the normal.
  • Angle of reflection (r): The angle between the reflected ray and the normal.

2. Laws of Reflection

The reflection of light from any surface (smooth or rough, plane or curved) obeys two fundamental laws.

Important

The Two Laws of Reflection:

  1. The angle of incidence is always equal to the angle of reflection (∠i = ∠r).
  2. The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence, all lie in the same plane.

3. Mirrors and Images

A mirror is a smooth polished surface which reflects light regularly. Mirrors can be plane (flat reflecting surface) or spherical/curved (concave or convex).

Real and Virtual Images

When light rays starting from a point object meet or appear to meet after reflection, an image is formed. Images are of two types:

Concept

Real Image: Formed when reflected light rays actually intersect. It can be obtained on a screen (e.g., images on a cinema screen).

Virtual Image: Formed when reflected light rays do not actually meet but appear to meet when extended backwards. It cannot be obtained on a screen (e.g., our image in a dressing mirror).

Characteristics of Image Formed by a Plane Mirror

The image formed by a standard flat mirror has specific properties:

  • It is always virtual and cannot be obtained on a screen.
  • It is upright (erect).
  • It is of the same size as the object.
  • It is formed exactly as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it.
  • It is laterally inverted.
Fact

Lateral Inversion: The interchange of the left and right sides of an object in its image. Because of this, the word AMBULANCE is written laterally inverted on the vehicles so drivers ahead can read it correctly in their rear-view mirrors.

Uses of Plane Mirrors

  • Used as looking glasses or dressing mirrors.
  • Used by opticians to double the effective length of the testing room.
  • Used in periscopes (to see over obstacles) and kaleidoscopes (to create multiple pattern images).
  • Used in solar cookers to reflect sunlight into the interior.

4. Speed of Light

Light is the fastest known travelling energy in the universe.

Fact

The speed of light in a vacuum or air is exactly 3 × 108 m/s (3 lakh kilometers per second).

It takes about 500 seconds for light to travel from the Sun to the Earth.

The speed of light decreases when it travels through denser transparent media. For example, its speed in water is 2.25 × 108 m/s, and in glass, it is 2.0 × 108 m/s.

5. Colours of Light

The white light emitted by the Sun is actually a mixture of seven different colours.

Concept

Dispersion: The phenomenon of splitting of white light into its constituent seven colours (VIBGYOR - Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, Red) when passed through a glass prism.

A natural example of dispersion is a rainbow, which is formed when white sunlight is split by small water droplets hanging in the sky after a rain shower.

6. Primary and Secondary Colours

Primary Colours

The three colours that produce white light when combined with the correct intensity are called primary colours. These cannot be obtained by mixing other colours.

  • Red
  • Green
  • Blue

(Red + Green + Blue = White Light)

Secondary Colours (Colour Addition)

Secondary colours are formed by mixing any two primary colours of light.

Important

Secondary Colour Combinations:

  • Red + Blue = Magenta (Purple)
  • Red + Green = Yellow
  • Blue + Green = Cyan (Peacock Blue)

7. Appearance of Colour of Objects

The colour of an object depends on how it interacts with the white light falling on it.

Opaque Objects (Colour Subtraction)

When white light falls on an opaque object, the object's special pigments selectively absorb some colours and reflect others. The colour that reaches our eyes determines the colour of the object.

  • A parrot appears green because it absorbs all colours of white light except green, which it reflects.
  • An object appears White when it reflects all the colours of light.
  • An object appears Black when it absorbs all the colours of light and reflects none (e.g., a crow).

Transparent Objects

A transparent object selectively allows light of certain colours to pass through it while absorbing the rest. A piece of red glass appears red because it allows only red light to pass through. If seen in blue or green light, it will appear black because it absorbs them.
