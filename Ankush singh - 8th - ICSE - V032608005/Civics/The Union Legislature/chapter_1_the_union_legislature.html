Chapter 1: The Union Legislature

1. Introduction to the Government

India is a vast country with diverse cultures and a huge geographical area. It is difficult for a single government to manage the entire administration efficiently. Therefore, our Constitution provides a two-tier system of government: one at the Centre level and the other at the State level.

Concept

Federal System: The two-tier system of government (Centre and State level) functioning together is known as the Federal system of government.

The Indian government operates through three independent yet interconnected branches:

  • Legislative: Its chief function is to enact laws for the country, control the income and expenditure of the government, levy taxes, and control the executive.
  • Executive: It implements the laws enacted by the legislature and looks after the day-to-day administration of the country.
  • Judiciary: It interprets the law, settles disputes, and protects the Fundamental Rights of all citizens.

2. The Union Legislature (Parliament)

The Union Legislature in India is called the Parliament. It is the Supreme Legislative body in our country. The Indian Parliament is bicameral in nature, meaning it consists of two houses.

The Parliament comprises:

  • The President of India
  • The Lok Sabha (House of the People)
  • The Rajya Sabha (Council of States)

3. Rajya Sabha (Council of States)

The Rajya Sabha is the Upper House of the Parliament. It consists of the representatives of the States and the Union Territories, and persons nominated by the President of India. Members are elected indirectly.

Composition of Members

The maximum strength of the Rajya Sabha is 250 members. Currently, it has 245 members.

  • Elected Members (238): Represent the States and Union Territories. They are elected by the Members of the State Legislative Assemblies. The number of seats varies based on the state's population (e.g., Uttar Pradesh has 31, Goa has 1).
  • Nominated Members (12): Nominated by the President of India for their special knowledge in literature, science, art, and social service.

Eligibility and Tenure

  • Must be a citizen of India and at least 30 years of age.
  • Must not hold any office of profit under the government.
  • Term of Office: An individual member's term is 6 years. One-third of its members retire every two years.
Fact

Because members retire in batches and the house is never fully dissolved, the Rajya Sabha is known as a Permanent House.

Presiding Officer and Sessions

The Vice-President of India is the ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha and presides over its meetings. A Deputy Chairman is elected from among the members to act in the Chairman's absence.

Quorum: The minimum number of members required to be present to conduct a meeting is one-tenth of the total members.

4. Lok Sabha (House of the People)

The Lok Sabha is the Lower House of the Indian Parliament. Its members are elected directly by the people through Universal Adult Franchise (any citizen above 18 years can vote). The Prime Minister is the head of the House.

Composition and Eligibility

  • Maximum Strength: 552 members (530 from States, 20 from Union Territories, and 2 nominated Anglo-Indians).
  • Eligibility: Must be a citizen of India, at least 25 years of age, have a sound mind, no criminal conviction, and hold no office of profit.
Important

Tenure: The normal term of the Lok Sabha is 5 years. It is a temporary house and can be dissolved earlier by the President. During an emergency, the term can be extended for one year.

Procedure for Elections

The country is divided into constituencies (geographical areas). One person is elected from each constituency via a secret ballot system. The political party with the maximum number of elected members is invited by the President to form the Government, while others form the Opposition.

Speaker of the Lok Sabha

The presiding officer of the Lok Sabha is the Speaker, elected by the members from amongst themselves (usually from the ruling party). The Speaker maintains discipline, sets the meeting agenda, and conducts the business of the House. In case of a tie during voting on a bill, the Speaker exercises a casting vote.

Note: G.V. Mavalankar was the first Speaker, and Meira Kumar was the first woman Speaker of the Lok Sabha.

5. Powers and Functions of the Parliament

Legislative Powers

The Parliament is a law-making body. Power is divided between the Centre and States through three lists:

  • Union List: Subjects of national importance (Defence, Foreign Affairs, Railways, Banking). Only the Parliament can make laws on these.
  • State List: Subjects like Police, Public Health, and Agriculture. State legislatures make laws here.
  • Concurrent List: Shared subjects like Criminal Law, Education, and Forests. Both can make laws, but Central law prevails in a conflict.

A proposed law is called a Bill. There are three main types:

  1. Money Bills: Deal with taxes and government expenditure. They can only be introduced in the Lok Sabha.
  2. Ordinary Bills: General legislation that can be introduced in either House.
  3. Constitutional Amendment Bills: Deal with amending the Constitution and can be introduced in either House.

Financial and Executive Powers

The Parliament controls the government's income and expenditure. It passes the Budget every financial year, ensuring money is spent for authorized purposes.

The Parliament controls the Executive (Council of Ministers) by asking questions during the Question Hour and Zero Hour. The Lok Sabha can remove the government from power by passing a Vote of No Confidence.

Other Important Functions

  • Electoral Functions: Parliament members participate in the election of the President and Vice-President.
  • Judicial Functions: Parliament has the power to impeach (remove) the President, Vice-President, and judges of the Supreme and High Courts for constitutional violations.
  • Constituent Functions: Under Article 368, the Parliament has the power to amend the Constitution.
