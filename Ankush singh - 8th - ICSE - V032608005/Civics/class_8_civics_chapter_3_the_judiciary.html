Chapter 3: The Judiciary

1. Introduction to the Judiciary

There are three organs of the Indian government: Legislature, Executive, and the Judiciary. The Judiciary is considered the third most important organ of the Union government.

Concept Role of the Judiciary: It settles disputes and interprets laws. It is an independent body, meaning the other organs (Legislature and Executive) cannot interfere with its functioning.

The Judiciary is crucial because it acts as the protector of the Constitution and safeguards the fundamental rights of the people. It administers justice to all citizens.

Independence of the Judiciary

An independent judiciary implies that:

  • Its decisions are respected and not interfered with by other governmental organs.
  • Judges can perform their duties without any fear or favour.
Important Independence does not mean the Judiciary functions arbitrarily or without accountability. It remains accountable to the Constitution of the country.

2. Structure of the Indian Judiciary

The Indian Judiciary has a pyramidal structure with a single, integrated system for the entire country.

Fact The Hierarchy:
1. Supreme Court: The Apex Court in India, located at New Delhi.
2. High Court: The highest court at the state and UT level. (There are currently 25 High Courts in India).
3. District and Session Courts: Generally one in every District/Metropolitan Area.
    - Further divided into Civil Judges and Metropolitan Magistrates.

The lower courts function under the direct superintendence of the higher courts.

3. The Supreme Court of India

It is the supreme judicial body and the apex court of the country. Its final decisions must be abided by all other courts.

Located in New Delhi, it is the highest court protecting citizens' rights and liberties and upholding the rule of law. It is known as the Guardian of our Constitution.

Composition

The Supreme Court consists of the Chief Justice of India and currently 33 other judges. The number of judges is not fixed and can be changed by an Act of Parliament. The Chief Justice heads the Supreme Court.

Qualifications

To be eligible to be a judge of the Supreme Court, one must:

  • Be a citizen of India.
  • AND have been an advocate in any one or more High Courts for at least 10 years.
  • OR have been a judge of one or more High Courts for at least 5 years.
  • OR be a distinguished Jurist in the opinion of the President.

Appointment, Term, and Removal

  • Appointment: The President appoints the Chief Justice. Other judges are appointed by the President after due consultation with the Chief Justice. The senior-most judge is normally elevated to Chief Justice.
  • Term of Office: A Supreme Court judge can hold office until the age of 65 years. They can resign earlier by addressing a letter to the President.
  • Removal: Judges can be removed for discharging unconstitutional functions, displaying unsoundness of mind, or proven incapacity. This is done through a process called impeachment, requiring a motion with the charges to be passed by a two-thirds majority in both Houses of Parliament.

Powers and Functions of the Supreme Court

Types of Jurisdictions:

  • Original Jurisdiction: The power to hear and decide certain cases in the first instance. Examples: Disputes between two or more states, disputes between the Union government and one or more states, cases concerning the violation of the Constitution, and issuing writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
  • Appellate Jurisdiction: Hearing appeals against the judgments of High Courts. It can hear appeals in constitutional, civil, and criminal cases and can review High Court decisions.
  • Advisory Jurisdiction: Under Article 143, the President can seek the advice of the Supreme Court on questions of law or fact of public importance.
  • Revisory Jurisdiction: The power to review its own judgments to correct any errors.

Key Functions:

Concept Court of Record: It has the authority to punish for contempt of court. Its judgments and proceedings are recorded as testimony and recognized as legal precedents and references.
  • Guardian of the Constitution: It interprets the Constitution, can declare laws null if not in accordance with it, and enforces Constitutional principles.
  • Guardian of Fundamental Rights: Protects citizens' fundamental rights. A citizen can move to the Supreme Court if their rights are violated.
  • Judicial Review: The power to examine the validity of laws passed by the Union and States and declare them null and void if found incompatible with the Constitution.

4. High Court of India

High Courts are the highest courts in the states. The Kolkata High Court is the oldest. Some states share a common High Court (e.g., Kerala and Lakshadweep).

Fact Currently, there are a total of 25 High Courts in India. The newest are the Telangana Court and the Andhra Pradesh High Court, established in 2019.

Composition, Tenure, and Eligibility

  • Composition: Consists of a Chief Justice and other judges (no fixed figure). Appointed by the President on the advice of the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court and the Governor of the respective state.
  • Tenure: Holds office until the age of 62 years. Can resign by writing to the President. Can only be removed through impeachment by the President (similar to Supreme Court judges).
  • Eligibility: Citizen of India, distinguished jurist, held a judicial office for 10 years, OR been an advocate of a High Court for a minimum of 10 years.

Powers of the High Court

  • Original Jurisdiction: Empowered to issue writs for enforcing fundamental rights. Has original jurisdiction in cases relating to will, divorce, contempt of court, and admiralty. Election petitions are also heard here.
  • Appellate Jurisdiction: Hears appeals in both civil and criminal cases against decisions of Subordinate Courts.
  • Administrative Powers: Acts as the controller of all subordinate courts. Can ask for details of proceedings and issue rules regarding their working.
  • Power of Judicial Review: Can declare any law or ordinance unconstitutional if against the Constitution.

5. Writs

A writ is a formal written order or direction given by the court to officials to do something or abstain from doing something.

Both the Supreme Court and High Courts can issue writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

Types of Writs in India

  1. Habeas Corpus: Latin for 'to have the body of'. Used to enforce individual liberty against unlawful detention (in prison or private custody).
  2. Mandamus: Means 'We command'. Issued to a public official who has failed to perform their duty or refused to do so, compelling them to resume their work.
  3. Prohibition: Means 'To forbid'. Issued by a higher court to a lower court to prevent the latter from exceeding its jurisdiction or usurping a jurisdiction it does not possess.
  4. Certiorari: Means 'to be certified' or 'to be informed'. Issued by a higher court in authority to a lower court either to transfer a pending case to itself or quash the lower court's order.
  5. Quo-Warranto: Means 'By what authority or warrant'. Issued to prevent a person from holding a public office to which they are not entitled.

6. Subordinate Courts of India

Each state is divided into districts or tehsils. Courts functioning in these districts are called Subordinate Courts.

Types of Subordinate Courts

  • Civil Courts: The Court of the District Judge is the highest civil court in a district. They settle cases like property disputes, breach of agreement/contract, divorce, or landlord-tenant disputes. No punishment as a violation of law is involved. Lower civil courts include Sub-Judge, Additional Sub-Judge, and Munsiff courts.
  • Criminal Courts: When a district court exercises jurisdiction on criminal matters, it is called the Court of Sessions, presided over by a Sessions Judge. They try cases of murder, dacoity, and robbery. Lower courts here are presided over by police, sub-divisional magistrates, etc. A death sentence awarded by a Sessions court must be confirmed by the High Court.

7. Panchayat Courts & Lok Adalats

  • Panchayat Courts: Deal with civil and criminal cases at the village level. Known by regional names like Nyaya Panchayat, Gram Kutchery. Sentences cannot be appealed, but parties can seek legal remedy in higher courts.
  • Lok Adalats: Also called 'People's Court', established for 'speedy justice'. Represented by retired/experienced judges and social members/lawyers. Cases are put forward without advocates and solved through mutual understanding. No provision for appeal against a Lok Adalat decision in any court.

8. Legal Aid

Important Article 39A of the Constitution provides for Free Legal Aid to deprived and weaker sections to promote justice on the basis of equal opportunity. (Articles 14 and 22(1) also mandate the State to guarantee equality before the law).

The Legal Services Authorities Act (1987) came into force in 1995 to establish a nationwide uniform network for providing legal services. Categories eligible for this scheme include:

  • Member of Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe.
  • A woman or a child.
  • A person who is mentally ill or with a physical disability.
  • A beggar or victim of trafficking.
  • A person unable to pay for legal services.
