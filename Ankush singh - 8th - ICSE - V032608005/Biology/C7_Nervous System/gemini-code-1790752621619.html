Nervous System

1. Introduction to Coordination

The human body carries out various vital life processes like digestion, respiration, circulation, and excretion. These processes do not function in isolation; they are closely inter-linked and work synchronously according to the immediate needs of the body.

Concept

Coordination is the process of interlinking and harmonising different bodily activities so that all systems work together smoothly as per internal and external demands.

Types of Coordination

  • Nervous Coordination: Brought about rapidly through electrical impulses traveling along the nervous system (brain, spinal cord, nerves, and sense organs).
  • Chemical Coordination: Slower, long-lasting coordination brought about by chemical messengers called hormones secreted directly into the bloodstream by endocrine glands (e.g., adrenaline release during sudden danger).

2. Major Functions of the Nervous System

  • Environmental Awareness: Gathers real-time sensory information about the external world through the five sense organs (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, skin).
  • Higher Cognitive Abilities: Serves as the biological seat of memory, thinking, learning, and reasoning.
  • Voluntary Motor Control: Regulates and harmonises conscious muscular activities such as writing, speaking, walking, or holding an object.
  • Involuntary Regulation: Seamlessly coordinates autonomous, vital life processes like heartbeat, respiration, and peristalsis without conscious thought.

3. Structural and Functional Unit: The Neuron

The entire nervous system is constructed of specialized conducting cells called neurons (nerve cells). A neuron is incapable of dividing post-differentiation and possesses high sensitivity.

Fact

The human cerebrum alone accommodates approximately 9 to 10 billion neurons, and an individual axon can vary from a few millimetres up to 1 metre in length!

Structure of a Typical Neuron

  • Cyton (Cell Body / Perikaryon): The main central segment containing a conspicuous nucleus, neuroplasm, and metabolic organelles.
  • Dendrites: Short, highly-branched cytoplasmic extensions arising from the cyton that pick up incoming stimuli and convey impulses toward the cell body.
  • Axon: A single, long, cylindrical process that transmits nerve impulses away from the cyton to target tissues or succeeding neurons.
  • Myelin Sheath: An insulating fatty layer enveloping the axon that accelerates impulse speed and prevents cross-leakage of electrical signals between adjacent fibres.
  • Nodes of Ranvier: Microscopic periodic gaps along the myelin sheath where the axon membrane remains exposed.
  • Terminal Branches (Axon Terminals): Fine terminal arborizations ending in swollen synaptic knobs that release chemical messengers.

Types of Neurons (Functional Classification)

  • Sensory Neurons: Conduct incoming impulses from sense organs/receptors to the brain or spinal cord.
  • Motor Neurons: Conduct outgoing motor commands from the brain or spinal cord to effector organs (muscles or glands).
  • Association Neurons (Relay / Interneurons): Located within the central nervous system to link sensory and motor pathways together.

The Synapse and Neurotransmitters

Neurons do not come into physical contact with one another. The microscopic functional junction between the axon terminal of one neuron and the dendrite of the next is called a synapse.

When an electrical impulse reaches the terminal knob, it stimulates the exocytosis of chemical messengers called neurotransmitters (e.g., acetylcholine) into the synaptic cleft. This chemical trigger generates an electrical potential in the next dendrite, ensuring strictly unidirectional conduction of impulses.

4. Structure and Classification of Nerves

A nerve is a cord-like structure composed of a bundle of individual axons (nerve fibres) wrapped together within a common protective tubular sheath (medullary sheath) like an insulated multi-core electric wire.

Type of Nerve Nature of Nerve Fibres Key Example
Sensory Nerve Composed entirely of sensory nerve fibres. Transmits sensation from receptors to CNS. Optic Nerve (from retina of the eye), Olfactory Nerve (nose).
Motor Nerve Composed exclusively of motor nerve fibres. Transmits motor signals from CNS to effectors. Nerves innervating the extrinsic eyeball muscles.
Mixed Nerve Contains both sensory and motor nerve fibres bundled together in the same nerve trunk. Nerve supplying the tongue; all 31 pairs of spinal nerves.

5. Divisions of the Human Nervous System

The human nervous system is structurally grouped into two primary divisions:

  • Central Nervous System (CNS): Comprises the brain and spinal cord, serving as the central processing unit.
  • Peripheral Nervous System (PNS): All nerves branching out from the CNS throughout peripheral body tissues.

I. The Central Nervous System (CNS)

The CNS is the master control center that integrates, interprets, and initiates responses.

1. The Brain

The human brain is delicately housed within the bony cranium (skull). It is divided into three prominent regions:

  • Cerebrum: The largest, topmost segment. Divided along the midline into right and left cerebral hemispheres. Its convoluted surface displays ridges (gyri) and grooves (sulci) to maximize surface area.
    • Outer Cortex (Grey Matter): Packed with cytons/cell bodies of billions of neurons.
    • Inner Region (White Matter): Formed primarily of myelinated axon bundles.
    • Function: Seat of intelligence, conscious thought, reasoning, voluntary muscle command, and sensory perception.
  • Cerebellum ("Little Brain"): Positioned posteriorly below the cerebrum.
    • Function: Coordinates complex voluntary muscular actions and preserves postural equilibrium and balance. While the cerebrum initiates motor intent, the cerebellum executes smooth, precise coordination.
  • Medulla Oblongata: The lowest, cone-shaped segment continuing downward into the spinal cord.
    • Function: Regulates critical involuntary visceral activities: heartbeat rate, respiration, peristalsis, and vascular tone.
Important

Clinical & Exam Points:
• Consumed alcohol impairs the cerebellum, leading to loss of muscular coordination and unsteady, staggered walking.
• Damage or traumatic injury to the medulla oblongata often causes instantaneous cessation of breathing and heartbeat, leading directly to fatal outcomes.

2. The Spinal Cord

The spinal cord extends downwards from the medulla oblongata through the vertebral column (backbone). In contrast to the brain, the tissue distribution is completely inverted:

  • Outer Region: White matter (longitudinal axon tracts).
  • Inner Region: Butterfly/H-shaped Grey matter (neuronal cytons).

Primary Functions of the Spinal Cord:

  • Governs autonomous reflex actions occurring below the neck level.
  • Conducts sensory impulses originating from skin and peripheral organs upward to the brain.
  • Dispatches descending motor directives from the brain downward toward limbs and trunk muscles.

II. The Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)

Consists of nerves extending outward to connect the CNS with all bodily organs:

  • Somatic Nervous System (SNS): Consists of cranial and spinal nerves relaying voluntary motor signals to skeletal muscles and sensory feedback back to the CNS.
    • Cranial Nerves: 12 pairs arising directly from the brain (sensory, motor, and mixed).
    • Spinal Nerves: 31 pairs originating bilaterally from the spinal cord (every spinal nerve is a mixed nerve).
  • Autonomic Nervous System (ANS): A paired longitudinal chain of ganglia and nerves flanking the vertebral column that autonomically governs smooth muscle, cardiac tissue, and visceral glands. It operates via two mutually antagonistic systems:
    • Sympathetic System: Prepares the body for "fight-or-flight" emergency responses (dilates pupils, increases heartbeat, constricts peripheral blood vessels).
    • Parasympathetic System: Restores equilibrium and "rest-and-digest" conditions (constricts pupils, decreases heartbeat, dilates cutaneous vessels).

6. Reflex Actions and the Reflex Arc

Concept

A Reflex Action is a rapid, involuntary, automatic, and instantaneous response to a stimulus executed via the spinal cord without the initial conscious involvement of the brain.

The shortest anatomical path traversed by a nerve impulse from receptor organ to effector organ is designated as the Reflex Arc.

Sequential Flow of the Reflex Arc:

Stimulus (e.g., hot object) → Receptor (sensory receptors in skin) → Sensory Neuron (conducts impulse along spinal nerve) → Spinal Cord / Relay Neuron (interneuron processes signal in grey matter) → Motor Neuron (carries motor command outward) → Effector Organ (biceps muscle contracts) → Response (immediate arm withdrawal).

Types of Reflexes

  • Natural (Inborn / Unconditioned) Reflexes: Genetically inherited and present since birth. They necessitate no prior learning or experience.
    • Protective Reflexes: Blinking of the eyes, sneezing, coughing, vomiting.
    • Functional Reflexes: Swallowing, peristalsis, salivation upon food intake.
  • Conditioned (Acquired) Reflexes: Acquired and refined throughout an individual's lifetime via habitual practice, associative memory, and learning.
    • Examples: Salivation solely at the sight or aroma of favorite food; typing seamlessly on a computer keyboard without glancing at keys; playing musical instruments; cycling.

7. Fundamental Terminology Quick-Review

  • Stimulus: Any detectable change in the internal or external environment provoking a physiological response.
  • Response: The resulting muscular or glandular activity displayed by the organism due to a perceived stimulus.
  • Impulse: A self-propagating wave of electrochemical disturbance travelling along a neuron membrane.
  • Receptor: Specialized sensory cells or organs (eye, ear, skin) designated to detect specific stimuli.
  • Effector: A responsive muscle or secretory gland that translates neural directives into action.
