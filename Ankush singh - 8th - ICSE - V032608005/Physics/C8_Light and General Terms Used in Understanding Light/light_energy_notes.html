LIGHT ENERGY

1. Reflection of Light on Plane Surfaces

The bouncing back of light into the same medium after striking a surface is called the reflection of light. The ray of light that falls on the mirror is the incident ray, and the ray that bounces back is the reflected ray.

Important

Laws of Reflection:

  • First Law: The angle of incidence is always equal to the angle of reflection (∠i = ∠r).
  • Second Law: The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.

Regular and Diffused Reflection

  • Regular Reflection: When all parallel rays reflected from a smooth surface (like a plane mirror) remain parallel in one direction.
  • Diffused (Irregular) Reflection: When parallel rays are reflected from a rough surface (like cardboard or brick) and go in different directions. Note: The laws of reflection are valid for every surface, even rough ones!
Concept

Real vs. Virtual Images:

  • Real Image: Can be obtained on a screen. It is formed when light rays actually intersect after reflection/refraction. It is always inverted.
  • Virtual Image: Cannot be obtained on a screen. It is formed when light rays appear to meet when produced backward. It is always erect but laterally inverted.

Characteristics of Image Formed by a Plane Mirror

  • The image is virtual and cannot be taken on a screen.
  • The image is erect but undergoes lateral inversion (the left side of the object appears as the right side of the image, and vice versa).
  • The image is of the same size as the object.
  • The image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it.

2. Reflection at Curved Surfaces (Spherical Mirrors)

Highly polished curved surfaces can act as mirrors, known as spherical mirrors. They obey the laws of reflection just like plane mirrors.

  • Concave Mirror: A spherical mirror silvered on the outer surface, so its reflecting surface is towards the hollow side. It converges light rays.
  • Convex Mirror: A spherical mirror silvered on the hollow side, so its reflecting surface is towards the outer side. It diverges light rays.

General Terms Used in Spherical Mirrors

  • Pole (P): The geometric centre of the spherical mirror.
  • Centre of Curvature (C): The centre of the hollow sphere of which the mirror is a part.
  • Principal Axis: The imaginary line passing through the pole and the centre of curvature.
  • Principal Focus (F): A point on the principal axis where a beam of light travelling parallel to the principal axis actually meets (concave) or appears to meet (convex) after reflection.
Fact

Focal Length (f): The distance between the pole and the principal focus. The focal length is always half the radius of curvature. f = R / 2

Uses of Spherical Mirrors

  • Concave Mirrors: Used as shaving mirrors, reflectors in headlights/torches to create a powerful beam, and by dentists/ENT doctors to focus light and see enlarged images.
  • Convex Mirrors: Used as street light reflectors (to diverge light over a wide area) and as rear-view mirrors in vehicles because they always form an erect, diminished image, giving a wider field of view.

3. Refraction of Light

Light travels in straight lines within a single medium. However, when light travels obliquely from one optical medium to another, it deviates from its path. This phenomenon is called the refraction of light. It happens because the speed of light changes depending on the density of the medium.

Concept

Rules of Refraction:

  • When light travels from an optically rarer (less dense) medium to an optically denser medium (e.g., air to glass), it bends towards the normal.
  • When light travels from an optically denser medium to a rarer medium (e.g., water to air), it bends away from the normal.
  • A ray striking the surface perpendicularly (normally) does not deviate.

Laws of Refraction

  • First Law: The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  • Second Law: The ratio of the velocity of light in vacuum to the velocity of light in a given optical medium is a constant, known as the Refractive Index.

Examples of Refraction: A coin at the bottom of a water-filled beaker appears raised; a swimming pool appears shallower than it is; stars appear to twinkle.

4. Dispersion of Light by a Prism

A prism is a piece of transparent material having three rectangular surfaces and two triangular bases. The angle between its two refracting surfaces is called the angle of prism.

When a ray of light passes through an equilateral glass prism, the angle between the incident ray and the emergent ray is known as the angle of deviation.

Fact

Dispersion and the Spectrum:

The phenomenon due to which white light splits into seven colours on passing through a prism is called dispersion. The band of seven colours formed on a screen is called a spectrum.

The colours in order from the base of the prism are Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, and Red (VIBGYOR).

  • Why does dispersion happen? The speed of light for all colours is the same in a vacuum but differs in transparent media. Violet light travels the slowest and deviates the most. Red light travels the fastest and deviates the least.
