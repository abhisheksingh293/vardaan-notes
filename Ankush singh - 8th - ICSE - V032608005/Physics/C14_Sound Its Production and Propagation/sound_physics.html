Sound

1. Introduction to Sound

Sound is a form of energy that produces a sensation of hearing in our ears. It is produced when a body vibrates. Therefore, every source of sound is a vibrating body.

  • Sound requires a material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) for its propagation.
  • Sound cannot travel in a vacuum.
  • The speed of sound is maximum in solids, less in liquids, and least in gases.
Concept

How Sound Propagates: When a source of sound vibrates, it creates a periodic disturbance in the medium nearby. This disturbance travels in the form of waves. The particles of the medium vibrate about their mean positions, transferring energy from one place to another with a constant speed, without actually moving from their places permanently. This continuous transfer of kinetic and potential energy among particles constitutes sound energy.

2. Key Terms Related to Sound Waves

To understand sound better, we must define the characteristics of a vibrating body (like a tuning fork or a pendulum).

Amplitude

The maximum displacement of a vibrating body or a particle of the medium from its mean (resting) position is called its amplitude.

  • If a body is struck softly, it has a smaller amplitude.
  • If a body is struck hard, it vibrates with a larger amplitude.

Frequency ($f$)

The number of vibrations produced by a vibrating body in one second is called its frequency.

Fact

The SI unit of frequency is Hertz (Hz). If a tuning fork completes 256 vibrations in one second, its frequency is 256 Hz.

Bigger units include kilohertz (1 kHz = 1000 Hz) and megahertz (1 MHz = 1,000,000 Hz).

Time Period ($T$)

The time taken by a vibrating body to complete one full vibration (or oscillation) is called its time period.

Important

Relationship between Time Period and Frequency:

Frequency ($f$) and Time Period ($T$) are inversely proportional to each other.

$$T = \frac{1}{f}$$ or $$f = \frac{1}{T}$$

3. Types of Sound Vibrations

The human ear is not sensitive to vibrations of all frequencies. Based on frequency, vibrations are classified into three types:

  • Infrasonic (or Subsonic) Vibrations: Vibrations with a frequency less than 20 Hz. The human ear cannot perceive them.
  • Sonic (or Audible) Vibrations: Vibrations with a frequency between 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz. These can be perceived by the human ear.
  • Ultrasonic Vibrations: Vibrations with a frequency above 20,000 Hz. These are also inaudible to humans.
Concept

Uses of Ultrasonic Vibrations:

  • Animals: Dogs can hear up to 40,000 Hz (trained using a Galton whistle). Bats (50 Hz to 100,000 Hz) use ultrasonic waves for echolocation to catch prey, as do dolphins.
  • Industry & Medicine: Used for homogenizing milk, dishwashing machines, driving away pests (rats/cockroaches), imaging internal organs (ultrasound), and relieving joint pain.

4. Reflection of Sound and Echoes

When sound energy strikes a hard surface, it bounces back in a different direction. This phenomenon is called the reflection of sound. The laws of reflection for sound are similar to those for light.

  • Reflectors: Hard, polished surfaces like metals, stones, and buildings reflect sound well.
  • Absorbers: Soft materials with loose textures like curtains, gunny bags, carpets, and thermocol absorb sound. (This is why cinema halls use acoustic materials to prevent unwanted reflections).

Echo

The repetition of sound caused by the reflection of sound waves from a distant, hard obstacle (like a cliff or a high building) is called an echo.

Important

Conditions for Hearing an Echo:

  1. The human ear can distinguish two sounds only if they reach the ear at least 0.1 seconds apart.
  2. Assuming the speed of sound is ~332 m/s (or 340 m/s in some cases), the minimum distance between the source and the reflecting body must be around 17 meters.
  3. The sound must be loud enough, and the reflecting surface must be large.

SONAR

SONAR stands for Sound Navigation and Ranging. It is a device fitted on ships to find the depth of the sea or locate underwater objects using the reflection of ultrasonic waves.

Formula for calculating depth ($d$):

$$d = \frac{v \times t}{2}$$

Where $v$ is the speed of sound in water, and $t$ is the total time taken for the echo to return.

5. Characteristics of Sound

Sounds differ from one another based on three main characteristics: Loudness, Pitch, and Quality.

Loudness (Intensity)

Loudness is the characteristic that distinguishes a faint sound from a loud sound, even if they have the same frequency.

  • Dependence: Loudness depends primarily on the amplitude of the vibrating body. Greater amplitude produces a louder sound.
  • Other factors: Loudness increases with a larger surface area of the vibrating body (e.g., a large drum is louder than a small one) and decreases as the distance from the source increases.
Fact

Unit of Loudness: Loudness is measured in decibels (dB).

  • 10 dB - 25 dB: Just audible (whispering)
  • 50 dB - 60 dB: Normal conversation (comfortable)
  • Above 80 dB: Becomes noise (uncomfortable)
  • 120 dB - 130 dB: Very uncomfortable (can cause pain)

Pitch

Pitch is the effect produced in the brain that helps us distinguish between a shrill sound and a flat (or bass) sound.

  • Dependence: Pitch depends strictly on the frequency of the vibrating body.
  • A high frequency produces a high-pitched (shrill) sound (e.g., a woman's voice, a flute, a mosquito).
  • A low frequency produces a low-pitched (flat/bass) sound (e.g., a man's voice, a drum).
Concept

Pitch in Musical Instruments:

  • Wind Instruments (Flute): Smaller lengths of vibrating air columns produce higher-pitched notes.
  • String Instruments (Violin, Guitar): Thinner, tighter, or shorter strings vibrate faster, producing a higher frequency and thus a higher pitch.

Quality (Timbre)

Quality or Timbre is the characteristic that allows us to distinguish between two sounds of the same loudness and same pitch produced by different instruments (e.g., a piano and a flute playing the same note).

It depends on the waveform of the sound, which is determined by the presence of secondary tones (overtones) mixed with the principal note.

6. Monotone

A sound of a single, pure frequency is called a monotone. It never changes in pitch, loudness, or speed (it does not rise or fall).

  • A tuning fork is the primary source that produces a pure note or monotone.
  • Speaking in a monotone means the voice lacks expression and remains on one flat note.
