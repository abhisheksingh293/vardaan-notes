HEAT TRANSFER

1. Boiling and Evaporation

Boiling or Vaporisation

When a liquid is heated, its molecules absorb heat energy, causing their kinetic energy to increase. Because the average kinetic energy is a measure of the temperature of a body, the temperature of the liquid rises. This weakens the intermolecular forces and increases the intermolecular spaces.

This process continues until the kinetic energy exceeds the intermolecular forces. At this stage, energetic molecules rapidly leave the liquid, changing into the gaseous state. This agitation occurs throughout the whole liquid and is known as boiling.

Concept

Latent Heat of Vaporisation: During boiling, the heat energy supplied does not increase the temperature further. Instead, it is hidden in the molecules of the gas to overcome the attractive forces. This hidden energy is known as the latent heat of vaporisation.

Evaporation

In some cases, a liquid changes into a vapour state without external heating. For example, wet clothes dry in the sun, and water in a flat dish disappears over time. This phenomenon is called evaporation.

During their motion, molecules on the liquid's surface possess higher kinetic energy than those inside. These energetic surface molecules overcome the downward force of attraction and escape into space as vapour. This process causes cooling in the remaining liquid because the heat energy for this change is taken from the surroundings.

Important

Key Differences Between Boiling and Evaporation:

  • Temperature: Boiling occurs at a fixed temperature (boiling point). Evaporation occurs at all temperatures below the boiling point.
  • Process Type: Boiling is a bulk process occurring throughout the liquid. Evaporation is a surface phenomenon occurring only at the exposed surface.
  • Cooling Effect: Boiling does not cause cooling; evaporation causes cooling.
  • Energy Source: Boiling requires an external source of heat; evaporation absorbs heat from the surroundings.

2. Thermal Expansion of Matter

When matter (solid, liquid, or gas) is heated, it expands. This increase in dimensions due to heating is called thermal expansion.

Expansion in Solids

Because solids have a definite shape and size, they expand in all dimensions. For convenience, thermal expansion in solids is divided into three categories:

Fact
  • Linear Expansion: The expansion in the length of a solid upon heating.
  • Superficial Expansion: The expansion in the area (surface) of a solid upon heating.
  • Cubical Expansion: The expansion in the volume of a solid upon heating.

Gravesande's Ring and Ball Experiment

This classic experiment demonstrates that solids expand on heating and contract on cooling. A metal ball easily passes through a closely fitting metal ring at room temperature. However, when the ball is heated, it expands and can no longer pass through the ring. Upon cooling back to room temperature, it contracts and passes through the ring once again.

Bimetallic Strip

If equal lengths of two different solid metals (e.g., brass and iron) are heated through the same temperature range, they do not expand by the same amount. When a bimetallic strip made of brass and iron is heated, the strip bends such that the brass is on the outer side of the curve (expanding more) and the iron is on the inner side (expanding less).

3. Real World Applications of Thermal Expansion

The principles of thermal expansion have significant practical applications in engineering and daily life:

  • Mounting of Iron Rim on Wooden Cart Wheels: An iron rim is made slightly smaller than the wooden wheel. It is heated until red-hot to expand it, slipped over the wheel, and then cold water is poured over it. It contracts on cooling and tightly grips the wheel.
  • Riveting: Hot rivets are passed through holes in metal plates and hammered flat. As they cool, they contract and pull the plates tightly together.
  • Laying of Rail Tracks: Small gaps are intentionally left between rails. If laid without gaps, the rails would expand in summer and bend outwards, causing derailments.
  • Mounting of Bridges: Girders supporting bridges are fixed at one end but rest on rollers at the other end. This allows the bridge structure to expand and contract freely during temperature changes without breaking the supporting pillars.
  • Laying of Overhead Wires: Electric and telephone wires are left slightly loose (sagging) in summer. If they were stretched tightly, they would contract during winter and break due to tension.
Concept

Why do thick glass tumblers crack when hot water is poured in?

Glass is a bad conductor of heat. When boiling water is poured inside, the inner surface heats up and expands rapidly, while the outer surface remains cool. This uneven expansion creates immense strain, causing the glass to crack. Special glasses like Borosil or Pyrex do not crack because they have a very small expansion rate and are made very thin.

4. Thermal Expansion of Liquids and Gases

Thermal Expansion of Liquids

Liquids do not have a definite length or area, but they do have a definite volume. Therefore, liquids only undergo cubical (volume) expansion. When heated, the volume of a liquid increases. Interestingly, if different liquids (like water, kerosene, and coconut oil) are heated through the exact same range of temperature, they will expand by different amounts.

Thermal Expansion of Gases

Similar to liquids, gases only possess cubical expansion when confined in an enclosed vessel. A key characteristic is that gases expand significantly more than both solids and liquids when heated. This can be demonstrated by placing a partially inflated balloon on a bottle and heating the bottle; the air inside expands drastically, fully inflating the balloon.
