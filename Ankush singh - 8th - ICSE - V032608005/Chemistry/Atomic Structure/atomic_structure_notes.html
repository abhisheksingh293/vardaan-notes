ATOMIC STRUCTURE

1. Historical Background of Matter

Maharishi Kanad's Model of an Atom

According to the great Indian sage Maharishi Kanad, if a pure substance (Padarth) is broken down into smaller pieces, a stage is reached when no further subdivision is possible. He called this ultimate smallest particle "Paramanu".

  • He believed that each anu may be made of two or more still smaller particles.
  • According to him, a paramanu is the ultimate smallest unit of matter.

The Greek Model of an Atom

Greek philosophers (around 400-500 B.C.), such as Democritus, believed that matter is composed of tiny particles which cannot be destroyed. The Greek word for such particles was "atomos" (meaning uncut). However, there was no experimental evidence to support this model early on.

2. John Dalton's Atomic Model

In 1808, John Dalton, an English school teacher, proposed a model of an atom based on experimental results available at that time, known as Dalton's Atomic Theory.

Important Postulates of Dalton's Atomic Theory:
  • All matter is made up of very small particles called atoms.
  • Atoms can neither be subdivided nor changed into one another.
  • Atoms can neither be created nor destroyed during any chemical reaction (Law of Conservation of Mass).
  • All atoms of a specific element are identical in all their properties, and different from those of other elements.
  • Atoms combine in the ratio of small whole numbers (e.g., 1:1, 1:2) to form compound atoms (now known as molecules).

3. Discovery of Subatomic Particles

Later scientific discoveries revealed that the atom is not indivisible but consists of smaller particles: electrons, protons, and neutrons.

Discovery of the Electron (Cathode Rays)

In 1897, Sir J.J. Thomson discovered that cathode rays consist of streams of negatively charged particles. These negatively charged particles were named electrons by G.J. Stoney. Later, Thomson confirmed that the electron is an essential constituent of all matter.

Fact
  • Mass of an electron: $9.108 \times 10^{-31}$ kg
  • Charge on an electron: $-1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ Coulomb

Discovery of the Proton (Anode Rays)

In 1886, E. Goldstein discovered anode rays (positive rays) using a modified discharge tube. These rays were found to be made of positively charged particles. The mass of the positively charged particle in the discharge tube varied depending on the gas used. When hydrogen gas was used, the particle obtained was given the name proton.

Fact
  • Mass of a proton: $1.672 \times 10^{-27}$ kg
  • Charge on a proton: $+1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ Coulomb

Discovery of the Neutron

In 1932, James Chadwick discovered the presence of neutral particles inside the atom. These particles were called neutrons. A neutron has no electrical charge.

Fact
  • Mass of a neutron: $1.675 \times 10^{-27}$ kg
  • Charge on a neutron: 0 (Electrically neutral)

4. Modern Atomic Models

J.J. Thomson's Plum Pudding Model

In 1898, J.J. Thomson proposed that an atom is considered to be a sphere of uniform positive charge, and electrons are embedded into it, like plums in a pudding (or seeds in a watermelon). Total positive charge equals total negative charge, making the atom electrically neutral.

Rutherford's Alpha Particles Scattering Experiment

Ernest Rutherford bombarded a thin sheet of gold foil with fast-moving alpha ($\alpha$) particles in an evacuated chamber.

Important Conclusions from Rutherford's Experiment:
  1. Most of the $\alpha$-particles passed straight through, meaning most of the space inside the atom is empty.
  2. A few $\alpha$-particles were deflected, showing that a heavy, positively charged core (nucleus) exists at the center.
  3. The entire mass of an atom is concentrated inside its nucleus, which is very small compared to the size of the entire atom.

Rutherford's Nuclear Model of the Atom

  • An atom consists of a positively charged nucleus, which is surrounded by electrons moving rapidly around it in circular paths (like planets around the sun).
  • Electrons and the nucleus are held together by electrostatic forces of attraction.

Bohr's Model of an Atom (1913)

Niels Bohr proposed a more brilliant description to fix flaws in Rutherford's model:

  • Electrons revolve around the nucleus in certain definite circular paths called orbits or shells.
  • Each shell corresponds to a definite energy. Thus, they are also known as energy levels.
  • These orbits are designated as K, L, M, N... shells, or characterised by integers $n = 1, 2, 3, 4...$ starting from the nucleus side.
  • As long as an electron remains in a particular orbit, it does not radiate energy.

5. Atomic Number & Mass Number

Concept Atomic Number ($Z$): The number of protons ($P$) present inside the nucleus of an atom of that element. Since an atom is neutral, it is also equal to the number of electrons.
Formula: $Z = P$
Concept Mass Number ($A$): The sum of the number of protons ($P$) and the number of neutrons ($N$) present in its nucleus (collectively called nucleons).
Formula: $A = P + N$

Representation of an Atom: An atom of element $X$ is symbolically written as $_Z^A X$, where $A$ is the mass number (superscript) and $Z$ is the atomic number (subscript).

6. Distribution of Electrons in Orbits (Bohr-Bury Scheme)

The distribution of electrons in different orbits (or shells) of an atom is called its electronic configuration. It follows rules proposed by Bohr and Bury:

Important Rules for Electronic Configuration:
  1. The maximum number of electrons which can be accommodated in a given orbit is given by the formula $2n^2$, where $n$ is the shell number.
  2. K Shell ($n=1$): Maximum $2(1)^2 = 2$ electrons.
  3. L Shell ($n=2$): Maximum $2(2)^2 = 8$ electrons.
  4. M Shell ($n=3$): Maximum $2(3)^2 = 18$ electrons.
  5. N Shell ($n=4$): Maximum $2(4)^2 = 32$ electrons.
  6. The outermost orbit can have at most 8 electrons, and the penultimate (second last) can have at most 18 electrons.
