Elements and Compounds

1. Classification of Matter

Everything around us that has mass and occupies space is known as matter. Matter can be broadly classified into two main categories based on its chemical composition:

  • Pure Substances: Substances made up of only one kind of particles (atoms or molecules). They have a fixed chemical composition. Examples include iron, water, and gold.
  • Impure Substances (Mixtures): Substances made up of two or more different kinds of particles mixed physically in any proportion. Examples include air, soil, and saltwater.

Pure substances are further divided into two essential categories: Elements and Compounds.

2. Elements

An element is the simplest and most basic form of a pure substance. It serves as the building block for all other complex substances in the universe.

Concept

An element is a pure substance that consists of only one specific type of atom. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances by ordinary chemical or physical methods like heating, cooling, or electrolysis.

Characteristics of Elements

  • An element is composed of identical atoms. For example, a piece of pure copper contains only copper atoms.
  • Elements have specific physical and chemical properties (like boiling point, melting point, and reactivity).
  • There are 118 known elements today; 92 of these occur naturally on Earth, while the rest are artificially created in laboratories.

Classification of Elements

Elements are broadly classified into four major categories based on their properties:

  • Metals: Usually solid, hard, shiny (lustrous), malleable (can be beaten into sheets), ductile (can be drawn into wires), and good conductors of heat and electricity. Examples: Iron, Copper, Gold, Silver.
  • Non-metals: Can be solid, liquid, or gas. They are generally dull, brittle, and poor conductors of heat and electricity. Examples: Oxygen, Carbon, Sulphur.
  • Metalloids: Elements that exhibit properties intermediate between metals and non-metals. Examples: Silicon, Germanium, Arsenic.
  • Noble Gases: Unreactive (inert) gases found in trace amounts in the atmosphere. They do not ordinarily participate in chemical reactions. Examples: Helium, Neon, Argon.

3. Symbols of Elements

Writing the full name of an element every time is time-consuming. Therefore, scientists use abbreviations called symbols to represent elements.

A symbol represents one atom of that specific element. The modern system of using English letters for symbols was proposed by the Swedish chemist J.J. Berzelius.

Rules for writing symbols:

  1. The first letter of the element's English name is often used as a capital letter (e.g., C for Carbon, O for Oxygen).
  2. If two or more elements start with the same letter, a two-letter symbol is used. The first letter is capitalized, and the second is in lowercase (e.g., Ca for Calcium, Co for Cobalt).
Fact

Symbols derived from Latin names: Not all symbols come from English names. Many common elements derive their symbols from their historical Latin names!

  • Sodium (Natrium) ➔ Na
  • Iron (Ferrum) ➔ Fe
  • Gold (Aurum) ➔ Au
  • Potassium (Kalium) ➔ K
  • Silver (Argentum) ➔ Ag

4. Compounds

Most elements do not exist independently in nature because they are highly reactive. Instead, they combine with other elements to form more stable substances called compounds.

Concept

A compound is a pure substance formed when two or more elements combine chemically in a fixed proportion by mass. The smallest unit of a compound that retains its properties is called a molecule.

Characteristics of Compounds

  • Fixed Composition: A compound is always made up of the same elements combined in a fixed ratio. For example, pure water (H₂O) always contains 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.
  • Different Properties: The properties of a compound are entirely different from the properties of its constituent elements. (Example: Hydrogen is a combustible gas, and Oxygen supports burning, but Water (H₂O) puts out fire!)
  • Chemical Separation: The elements in a compound cannot be separated by physical methods (like filtration or evaporation). They can only be separated by chemical methods (like passing electricity).
Important

Mixture vs. Compound: Simply mixing iron filings and sulphur powder creates a mixture (they retain their original properties and can be separated by a magnet). However, if you heat them strongly, they react chemically to form Iron (II) Sulphide, a compound (which has entirely new properties and cannot be separated by a magnet).

5. Chemical Formulae

Just as a symbol represents an element, a chemical formula represents a molecule of an element or a compound. It tells us which elements are present and the exact number of atoms of each element in that molecule.

Examples of Chemical Formulae:

  • Water (H₂O): Contains 2 Hydrogen atoms and 1 Oxygen atom.
  • Carbon Dioxide (CO₂): Contains 1 Carbon atom and 2 Oxygen atoms.
  • Sodium Chloride (NaCl): Contains 1 Sodium atom and 1 Chlorine atom.
  • Sulphuric Acid (H₂SO₄): Contains 2 Hydrogen, 1 Sulphur, and 4 Oxygen atoms.

6. Atomicity

The total number of atoms present in one molecule of an element or a compound is known as its atomicity.

  • Monoatomic: Molecules containing only 1 atom. (e.g., Helium - He, Neon - Ne). Metals are also generally considered monoatomic.
  • Diatomic: Molecules containing 2 atoms. (e.g., Hydrogen gas - H₂, Oxygen gas - O₂, Nitrogen gas - N₂).
  • Triatomic: Molecules containing 3 atoms. (e.g., Ozone - O₃, Carbon Dioxide - CO₂).
  • Polyatomic: Molecules containing more than 3 atoms. (e.g., Phosphorus - P₄, Sulphur - S₈).
