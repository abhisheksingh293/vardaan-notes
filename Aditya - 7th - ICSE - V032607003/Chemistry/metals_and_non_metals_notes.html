Metals and Non-Metals

1. Physical Properties of Metals

Metals are elements that exhibit specific physical characteristics. Here are their key properties:

  • State: Generally solid at room temperature (Exception: Mercury and gallium are liquid).
  • Hardness: Generally hard (Exceptions: Sodium and potassium are soft and can be cut with a knife).
  • Lustre: Freshly cut surfaces have a brilliant shine known as metallic lustre.
  • Density: Generally high density (Exceptions: Sodium and potassium have density less than water).
  • Melting & Boiling Points: Generally high (Exceptions: Sodium, potassium, mercury, and gallium).
  • Malleability: Can be beaten into thin sheets (Exceptions: Zinc, arsenic, and antimony are not very malleable).
  • Dक्टीलिटी (Ductility): Can be drawn into thin wires (Exceptions: Zinc, arsenic, and antimony).
  • Tensile Strength: Can bear a lot of strain without breaking (Exceptions: Zinc, arsenic, and antimony).
  • Conductivity: Good conductors of heat and electricity. Silver is the best conductor. (Exceptions: Bismuth and tungsten are poor conductors).
  • Sonority: Produce a ringing sound when struck.

2. Uses of Common Metals

Gold & Silver

  • Gold: Used for jewelry, coins, dentistry, space satellite components, and Ayurvedic medicine. It's highly malleable and ductile.
  • Silver: Used for coins, ornaments, table cutlery, water purifiers, sensitive electrical circuits, and photographic films (silver salts). It is the best conductor of heat and electricity.
Important

Tarnishing of Silver

Silver turns black when exposed to air for a long time because it reacts with hydrogen sulphide gas in the air to form black silver sulphide (Ag₂S).

Copper & Iron

  • Copper: Used for electric transmission wires, utensils, statues, printed circuits in electronics, and making alloys (brass, bronze).
  • Iron: Extensively used (often as steel) in the construction of ships, buildings, automobiles, bridges, and machinery.

Other Important Metals

  • Tin: Used for tinning food cans, and making alloys like solder and bronze. Does not rust in moist air.
  • Lead: Used for making lead-acid batteries, sanitary pipes, bullet tips, and fusible alloys.
  • Zinc: Used in galvanization (coating iron to prevent rust) and as negative electrodes in dry cells.
  • Aluminium: Used for high-voltage wires, packaging foils, and light alloys (duralumin, magnalium) for aircraft.
  • Magnesium: Burns with a dazzling flame (used in fireworks), used in thermite welding, and in alloys like magnalium.
Concept

Amalgam

A solution of a metal dissolved in mercury is called an amalgam. For example, sodium dissolved in mercury forms sodium amalgam. Gold and silver amalgams are used in dentistry.

3. Physical Properties of Non-Metals

Non-metals generally have properties opposite to those of metals:

  • State: Mostly gases at room temperature. (Exceptions: Carbon, sulphur, phosphorus, iodine are solids; Bromine is a liquid).
  • Hardness: Solid non-metals are generally brittle. (Exception: Diamond, a form of carbon, is the hardest naturally occurring substance).
  • Lustre: Do not have lustre. (Exceptions: Graphite and iodine crystals are lustrous).
  • Density: Generally low.
  • Melting & Boiling Points: Generally low.
  • Malleability & Ductility: Non-malleable and non-ductile. (Exception: Carbon fibers are highly ductile).
  • Conductivity: Poor conductors of heat and electricity. (Exception: Graphite is a good conductor).

4. Uses of Some Common Non-Metals

  • Nitrogen: Dilutes oxygen's activity to control combustion. Its compounds are vital for plant proteins (fertilizers).
  • Oxygen: Essential for respiration by animals and plants, and necessary for the combustion of fuels.
  • Chlorine: Used for sterilizing drinking water, bleaching fabrics, and manufacturing pesticides (DDT) and HCl.
  • Iodine: Added to common salt (iodized salt) to prevent goitre. Used in photographic films (silver iodide) and as an antiseptic (tincture of iodine).
Fact

Versatility of Graphite

Graphite (carbon) is used as pencil lead (mixed with clay), as electrodes in batteries, as a dry lubricant in machinery, for making heat-resistant crucibles, and in nuclear reactors.

Noble Gases

Helium, Neon, Argon, Krypton, Xenon, and Radon are unreactive noble gases.

  • Helium: Used in weather balloons (light and non-flammable).
  • Argon: Used for filling electric bulbs to improve efficiency.

5. Alloys

Concept

Definition of an Alloy

An alloy is a homogeneous mixture of two or more molten metals (or a metal and a non-metal) cooled to room temperature. They are made to improve properties like tensile strength, hardness, and resistance to corrosion.

  • Stainless Steel: Iron (83%) + Carbon (1%) + Chromium (15%) + Nickel (1%). Resists rust; used for cutlery and surgical instruments.
  • Alloyed Gold: Pure gold is too soft; it's alloyed with silver or copper to make it harder and lower its melting point for jewelry making.
  • Duralumin: Aluminium (95%) + Copper (4%) + Mg (0.5%) + Mn (0.5%). Lightweight and strong; used for aircraft frames.
  • Brass: Copper (60-80%) + Zinc (20-40%). Lustrous yellow, malleable; used for electric switches, taps, and statues.
  • Bronze: Copper (80%) + Zinc (10%) + Tin (10%). Corrosion-resistant; used for statues and coins.

6. Rusting (Corrosion) of Iron

Rusting is a slow chemical change that destroys iron objects. It occurs when iron is exposed to both moisture (water) and oxygen (air).

Equation: 4Fe (Iron) + 3O₂ (Oxygen) + xH₂O (Water) → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (Rust)

Prevention of Rusting

  • Oiling and Greasing: Forms a protective layer blocking air and moisture.
  • Painting: Used for gates, car bodies, and ships to prevent contact with moist air.
  • Galvanization: Coating iron sheets with a layer of molten zinc. Used for roofs and pipes.
  • Electroplating: Coating with nickel or chromium (e.g., bicycle wheels).
  • Alloying: Converting iron into stainless steel.

7. Metalloids

Elements that exhibit some properties of metals and some properties of non-metals are called metalloids. They are often electrical semiconductors.

Examples: Silicon, Germanium, Arsenic, Antimony, Boron, Polonium, Tellurium.

  • Silicon (Si): Hard with a metallic lustre, but brittle. Used extensively in making microprocessor chips (semiconductors), carborundum (abrasives), and silicone (waterproof material).
  • Germanium (Ge): Used in transistors, alloys, and fluorescent lamps.
  • Antimony (Sb): Used in the electronics industry, as type metal in printing, and alloyed with lead to improve hardness.
