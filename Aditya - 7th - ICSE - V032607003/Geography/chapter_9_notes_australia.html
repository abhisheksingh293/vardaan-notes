Chapter 9: Australia - Location, Political and Physical Features

1. Location and Extent

Australia is the smallest continent and the sixth largest country in the world. It derives its name from the Latin word 'australis', which means 'southern'.

Concept

The Land Down Under: Because Australia is the only continent that lies entirely within the Southern Hemisphere, it is famously referred to as the 'Southern Continent' or 'The Land Down Under'.

Geographical Boundaries

  • West: Indian Ocean
  • South: Southern Ocean
  • East: Pacific Ocean
  • North & North-east: Separated from Papua New Guinea and Indonesia by the Arafura Sea and the Torres Strait.
  • South-east: Separated from New Zealand by the Tasman Sea, and the mainland is separated from the island of Tasmania by the Bass Strait.

Latitudinally, it lies between 10°S and 40°S. Longitudinally, it stretches from 114°E to 154°E. The Tropic of Capricorn passes directly through the middle of the continent.

2. Political Divisions

Fact

Australia gained independence from British rule on January 1, 1901. Today, it is divided into six states and two federal territories.

  • States: Western Australia, Queensland, New South Wales, South Australia, Victoria, and Tasmania.
  • Territories: Northern Territory and the Australian Capital Territory (ACT).

Important Cities

  • Canberra: The capital city and largest inland city. Home to the Parliament House and the High Court of Australia.
  • Sydney: The largest city (capital of New South Wales). World-famous for its natural harbour, the Sydney Harbour Bridge, and the Sydney Opera House.
  • Melbourne: Capital of Victoria, the second-largest city, and famous as Australia's largest seaport and automobile industry hub.
  • Brisbane: Capital of Queensland, an important center for petroleum, paper, and metal industries.
  • Adelaide & Perth: Adelaide is known for oil refining (South Australia), while Perth (Western Australia) is famous for its pristine beaches.

3. Physical Features

Geologically, Australia is not a true island but a massive landmass. It consists largely of deserts and semi-arid lands, divided into four broad physical regions:

i. The Western Plateau

Covering almost two-thirds of the continent, it is made of old hard rocks. Much of it is a sandy or stony desert (stony deserts are locally known as gibber).

  • Prominent Deserts: Great Australian Desert, Great Sandy Desert, and Gibson Desert.
  • Average elevation is about 365 meters above sea level.
Important

Uluru (Ayers Rock): Located in this plateau, it is the largest single rock in the world. It is considered sacred to the Aborigines, the original inhabitants of Australia.

ii. The Eastern Highlands

Running parallel to the eastern and south-eastern coast from the Cape York Peninsula down to Tasmania. These are also known as the Great Dividing Range.

  • Historically, they acted as a massive barrier preventing early settlers from crossing into the interior.
  • The highest peak is Mount Kosciuszko (2228 meters), located in the Australian Alps and often snow-capped.

iii. The Central Lowlands

Located between the Western Plateau and the Eastern Highlands. They are divided into three distinct drainage basins based on the flow of river water:

  • Carpentaria Lowland: Rivers here drain north into the Gulf of Carpentaria.
  • Murray-Darling Basin: Located in the south, this is the most fertile region of Australia.
  • Lake Eyre Basin: An area of inland drainage (water doesn't reach the sea). Lake Eyre is a saltwater lake sitting 15 meters below sea level.

iv. The Coastal Plains

A narrow strip of flat land along the eastern coast that receives high rainfall, making it ideal for large settlements like Sydney, Melbourne, and Brisbane. In the south lies the Nullarbor Plain, a vast treeless area.

4. Important Water Features

Concept

The Great Artesian Basin: Found in the Central Lowlands, it is one of the largest underground water reservoirs in the world. Water is trapped in porous rocks (an aquifer) sandwiched between non-porous rocks. When bored, water gushes out without a pump, forming an artesian well. The water is salty—good for cattle, but not for crop irrigation.

Rivers

  • Murray River: The most significant river (2,570 km long), fed by snow melt from the mountains so it never runs dry.
  • Darling River: The main tributary of the Murray River (2,739 km long) but has an erratic water flow.
Fact

The Great Barrier Reef: Located off the north-eastern coast, it is the largest coral reef in the world, stretching roughly 2,000 km. Made of tiny coral polyps, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site hosting whales, dolphins, and diverse marine life.

5. Case Study: Sheep Rearing in Australia

Australia is globally known as the "Land of the Golden Fleece", controlling about 30% of the world's wool production.

  • History: Introduced by British colonisers. Captain John Macarthur was pivotal in breeding the high-quality Merino sheep. When King George III received a suit made of Merino wool, he heavily promoted the enterprise.
  • Sheep Stations: These are massive sheep farms. They are cordoned off into fenced paddocks (fencing began during the 1851-1880 gold rush to protect sheep from gold-seekers).
  • Mechanisation: The industry relies heavily on machines. Sheep shearers travel from farm to farm using electric clippers. The sheared wool is cleaned, sorted, graded, and packed into bales for export.
  • Economic Impact: Australia exports nearly all its wool, earning approximately $2 billion annually.
Important

Favourable Factors for Sheep Rearing: The central and western parts of Australia (like New South Wales) have little rainfall and natural vegetation consisting of shrubs and thorny bushes. This semi-arid climate is perfectly suited for Merino sheep to thrive.
