Weathering and Soil Formation

1. Types of Rocks

The surface of the Earth is made up of solid rocks and soil. A rock is a solid material composed of grains of minerals. Rocks are broadly divided into three main categories based on their formation and composition:

Igneous Rocks

Derived from the Latin word ignis meaning 'fire'. These rocks are formed by the cooling and solidification of hot, liquid materials known as lava or magma. They are known as primary rocks. Types include:

  • Extrusive rocks: Formed by the cooling of lava on the Earth's surface. They are smooth and fine-grained (e.g., Basalt).
  • Intrusive rocks: Formed by the cooling of magma deep within the Earth's crust. They are coarse, hard, and have large crystals (e.g., Granite).

Sedimentary & Metamorphic Rocks

  • Sedimentary rocks: Formed by the accumulation and consolidation of weathered rock fragments.
  • Metamorphic rocks: Derived from the Greek words meta (change) and morph (form). Formed when existing rocks are subjected to extreme heat and pressure.
Concept

Primary Rocks: Igneous rocks are often referred to as primary rocks because they are the first rocks to be formed on Earth directly from the cooling of magma.

2. Weathering

Weathering is the process of breaking down of rocks into smaller fragments upon exposure to the elements on the Earth's surface.

Mechanical Weathering

The physical breakdown of rocks into smaller pieces without any change in their chemical composition. Common types include:

  • Block disintegration: Occurs due to repeated expansion during the hot day and contraction during the cold night, leading to the breaking off of rock blocks.
  • Frost shattering: Water enters cracks in rocks, freezes during the night (expanding in volume), and thaws during the day. This repeated cycle breaks the rocks apart, primarily in temperate latitudes.
  • Exfoliation: The peeling off of the outer layer of rocks, similar to peeling an onion. Caused by frequent wetting and drying or extreme temperature changes.

Chemical Weathering

The decomposition of rocks due to chemical reactions, often facilitated by air and moisture.

  • Oxidation: The reaction of minerals (like iron) present in the rocks with atmospheric oxygen, weakening the rock—similar to the rusting of iron.
  • Carbonation, Hydration, and Solution: Other chemical processes where water and atmospheric gases alter the rock's chemical makeup.

Biological Weathering

Weathering caused by the actions of living organisms:

  • Plants: Roots penetrate into cracks; as they grow thicker, they widen the cracks and break the rock.
  • Animals: Burrowing creatures like earthworms, ants, and rodents loosen the soil and expose rocks to further weathering.
  • Human Activities: Mining, blasting, construction of roads and dams cause large-scale disintegration of rocks.
Fact

Exfoliation is most commonly seen in hot desert areas where there is a massive difference between daytime and nighttime temperatures, causing the rock's outer surface to expand and contract rapidly.

3. Soil Formation and Profile

Soil is the loose top covering of the Earth's surface, essential for plant growth. It takes thousands of years to form just a thin layer of soil.

Factors Affecting Soil Formation

  • Topography: The elevation and slope of the land affect the accumulation of soil.
  • Climate: Temperature and rainfall are the two most important factors dictating the rate of weathering.
  • Plants and Animals: Provide humus (organic matter) that enriches the soil's fertility.

The Soil Profile

The vertical section showing the different horizontal layers (horizons) of soil:

  • Top soil: The uppermost layer, predominantly made of organic matter and humus. It is dark in color and highly fertile.
  • Subsoil: Found beneath the topsoil, containing sand, silt, and clay. It holds moisture.
  • Weathered rock: A transition layer containing fragments of weathered parent rocks.
  • Bedrock (Parent rock): The solid, unweathered layer of rock at the bottom. The mineral properties of the soil are determined by this layer.

4. Soil Erosion

Soil erosion is the wearing away or removal of the fertile top layer of soil by agents like running water and wind.

  • Leaching: Occurs when heavy rainfall washes away essential minerals deep into the subsoil, reducing topsoil fertility.
  • Sheet erosion: The washing away of the top layer of soil over a vast area due to heavy rainfall.
  • Gully erosion: When rainwater forms deep channels or gullies on slopes, making the land unfit for cultivation.
Important

Deforestation (cutting down trees) and overgrazing are the leading human-driven factors that accelerate soil erosion by leaving the topsoil loose and exposed to wind and water.

5. Soil Conservation

Soil conservation refers to the methods and techniques used to protect the soil from erosion and maintain its fertility.

Major Conservation Methods:

  • Afforestation: Planting trees on a large scale. Roots hold the soil particles firmly together.
  • Planting shelter belts: Growing rows of trees to check the speed of the wind, thereby reducing wind erosion.
  • Terrace and Contour farming: Cutting steps on hilly slopes (terracing) and ploughing along the contours to reduce the speed of running water.
  • Crop rotation: Growing different crops in successive years on the same land to replenish specific soil nutrients naturally.
  • Fallowing: Leaving the agricultural land empty for a few seasons to allow it to regain its fertility naturally.
  • Controlling overgrazing: Restricting cattle grazing in specific areas to protect the vegetation cover.
  • Adding fertilisers: Adding organic matter and minerals to maintain soil health.
