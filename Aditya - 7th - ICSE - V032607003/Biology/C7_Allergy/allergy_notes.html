ALLERGY

1. Introduction to Allergy

If someone develops a rash or sneezes because of certain things like dust, animal fur, or pollen grains, that person is said to have an allergy. An allergy is an over-reaction of the immune system to a substance that in most other people has no effect.

The word "allergy" was first used by Clemens von Pirquet in 1900.

Concept

Allergy: It is a highly sensitive reaction of the body to certain substances that come in contact with the body or enter the body from outside.

Allergen: Any substance that causes an allergy or allergic reaction is called an allergen.

2. Common Allergens

People can be allergic to all sorts of things. Many allergens are air-borne particles. Common allergens include:

  • Dust and Mites
  • Pollen grains
  • Animal fur from dogs, horses, rabbits, cats
  • Food substances such as seafood, eggs, soya, and milk
  • Drugs (Medicines) like aspirin and antibiotics (e.g., penicillin)
  • Mould (Fungus)
  • Insect stings/bites
  • Strong sunlight and Cosmetics
Fact

Many doctors and scientists believe that heredity plays a major role in deciding who will be allergic. Grandparents, parents, and children within the same family are often allergic to the same substances.

3. Entry Routes of Allergens

  • Mouth: Food allergies (milk, egg, fish allergy), dust allergy.
  • Nose: Dust allergy, pollen allergy, animal fur allergy.
  • Skin: Insect bite allergy, strong sunlight allergy, cosmetic allergy.

4. Types of Allergies

On the basis of the period of occurrence, allergies are classified into two main types:

  • Seasonal Allergy: Occurs only during a specific time of the year. For example, pollen allergy is common during spring when new blooms appear. Sun reactions on the skin and smog (containing sulphur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide) during winter are also seasonal.
  • Perennial Allergy: These persist throughout the entire year. Examples include dust allergy, food allergies (milk, soya, eggs), insect bite allergies, eczema, and asthma.

5. Mechanism of an Allergic Reaction

When a person is exposed to an allergen (e.g., eating chocolate for the first time), it is digested. The digested products cause special cells in the body tissues to produce a chemical called an antibody.

Upon the next exposure, the allergen combines with the antibodies present in the skin and lungs. This damages the small blood vessels, allowing fluid to escape (causing spots on the skin) or triggering heavy mucus production in the lungs (leading to difficulty in breathing).

Important

The specific antibody responsible for allergic reactions is the IgE antibody. When this antibody combines with an allergen, a chemical called histamine is released. Histamine is what actually causes the allergic symptoms. Doctors often prescribe antihistamine drugs to prevent the reaction, though they cannot cure the allergy permanently.

6. Diagnosis of Allergy

  • Scratch Test: Doctors make small scratches in a row along the patient's arm. Tiny amounts of suspected allergens (like pollen or dust) are placed in the scratches. If the skin swells and reddens around a scratch, the patient is allergic to that specific substance.
  • Blood Test: A safe, quick, and simple test that measures the concentration of specific IgE antibodies in the blood. A higher IgE antibody value indicates a greater likelihood of experiencing allergy symptoms.

7. Symptoms of Allergy

When sensitive people are exposed to allergens, histamine causes capillaries to release fluid, resulting in tissue swelling. Common signs and symptoms include:

  • Sneezing and coughing
  • Shortness of breath (breathlessness) and wheezing
  • Running nose and watery eyes
  • Skin rashes and itching (eyes, ears, lips, throat)
  • Nausea (feeling sick) and vomiting

8. Precautions and Care

The best preventive method is to identify the allergy and then take immediate steps to reduce exposure to that allergen. A high intake of antioxidants, zinc, and selenium during pregnancy may help prevent allergies in the baby, while early introduction of solid food after birth increases a child's risk.

9. Common Specific Allergies

Hay Fever

Usually developing in early summer, this allergy was originally thought to be caused by hay pollen, but pollen from any plant can trigger it. The pollen (a protein) affects the muscles of capillary cells in the lungs, nose, and eyes. Symptoms include running eyes, headaches, and sneezing. It can be treated temporarily with antihistamine pills or protein extract injections.

Eczema

A common skin complaint causing severe itching. It can be triggered by mental stress, worry, or sensitivity to substances like cosmetics, cheap clothing dyes, hard water, or household detergents.

Nettle Rash (Urticaria)

An allergic reaction to a drug or certain foods like shellfish, strawberries, or eggs. The skin quickly turns red and rashy, lasting from minutes to days before disappearing suddenly. Bathing the rash with warm water and soda provides relief.

Asthma

The Greek word "asthma" means panting for breath. Asthmatics have oversensitive cells in their air tubes (bronchi) that react to dust, feathers, animal dandruff, or chemical irritants. During an attack, the skin lining the air tubes swells and oozes thick sticky mucus, forcing the person to struggle and wheeze for breath. Asthma is treated using steroids and bronchodilators (like salbutamol, a modified adrenaline) typically administered via an inhaler.
