Nervous System

1. Introduction to Coordination

Inside the body, millions of cells, tissues, and organs do not work independently. They work in harmony, and their activities are highly coordinated.

Coordination means that body tissues and organs work together, performing their functions at the correct speed and sequence to fulfill the needs of the whole body.

Concept

Stimulus: Any condition or change in the internal or external environment of an organism intense enough to evoke a response from it.

  • External Stimuli: Environmental factors like light, heat, water, wind, or touch (e.g., pulling your hand away from a hot plate).
  • Internal Stimuli: Factors within the organism (e.g., secretion of enzymes or hormones).

In our body, there are receptors (sense organs like eyes, ears, nose, skin, tongue) which detect a stimulus. A response is the behavior caused by the stimulus. For example, when you see a coin on the floor (stimulus to the eye), your brain processes this and signals your muscles to pick it up (response).

Fact

Plants lack a nervous system. Coordination in plants takes place by chemicals only. Hormones are chemical messengers found in both plants and animals.

2. Basic Unit of Nervous System: The Neuron

The basic structural and functional unit of the nervous system is a special cell called a nerve cell or neuron. The function of a neuron is to conduct messages, called impulses, from one part of the body to another.

Parts of a Neuron

  • Cyton (Cell body): Contains the nucleus and cytoplasm.
  • Dendrites: Short, branching structures arising from the cell body that receive signals.
  • Axon: A long, tube-like tail with fine terminal nerve endings. It is enveloped in a protective covering called the myelin sheath.

Types of Neurons

  • Sensory (Afferent) Neurons: Carry impulses from the body parts/receptors to the brain or spinal cord. (e.g., Optic nerve of the eye).
  • Motor (Efferent) Neurons: Carry impulses or commands from the brain or spinal cord to the body parts/muscles.
  • Mixed Neurons: Carry impulses both ways.

3. Nervous System of Humans

The human nervous system coordinates muscles (for walking, writing), controls automatic actions (swallowing, blinking, breathing), and responds to external stimuli. It is broadly divided into two main parts:

A. Central Nervous System (CNS)

The CNS consists of the brain and the spinal cord, serving as the main control center.

  • Brain: Enclosed within the bony shell of the skull (cranium). It comprises three main parts:
    • Cerebrum: Uppermost and largest part. Its grooved surface accommodates millions of neurons. It controls mental activities (thinking, reasoning, intelligence, memory), perceives senses, and controls all voluntary actions.
    • Cerebellum: Located just under the cerebrum at the back of the head. It maintains the balance of the body and coordinates muscular activity.
    • Medulla Oblongata (Brain Stem): The lowest part joining the brain to the spinal cord. It controls internal organs (heartbeat, breathing, peristalsis).
  • Spinal Cord: Extends from the medulla and runs down through the vertebral column for protection. It contains a central canal filled with cerebrospinal fluid, surrounded by H-shaped grey matter (cell bodies) and an outer layer of white matter (nerve fibers). It conducts messages to/from the brain and controls reflexes below the neck.

B. Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)

The PNS consists of nerves that link the CNS to the various body organs.

Important

Nerves are bundles of neurons. The peripheral nervous system is made up of:

  • Cranial Nerves: Emerge directly from the brain. There are 12 pairs.
  • Spinal Nerves: Emerge from the spinal cord. There are 31 pairs.

4. Voluntary and Reflex Actions

Living things respond to stimuli through two main types of actions:

Voluntary Actions

These actions are under the direct control of the brain (specifically the cerebrum). Because they involve thinking, the speed of response is relatively slow. Examples include riding a bicycle, dancing, or writing.

Reflex Actions

A reflex action is a quick, automatic response to a stimulus without the direct involvement of the brain. These are strictly under the control of the spinal cord. Because the brain doesn't have to process the decision, the speed of response is very fast. Examples include immediately pulling your hand away from a hot pan or blinking when dust enters your eyes.

Concept

Reflex Arc: The specific path that an impulse takes in a reflex action.

The pathway is: Stimulus → Receptor Organ (e.g., skin) → Sensory Neuron → Spinal Cord (relay neuron) → Motor Neuron → Muscle Action (effector) → Response.
