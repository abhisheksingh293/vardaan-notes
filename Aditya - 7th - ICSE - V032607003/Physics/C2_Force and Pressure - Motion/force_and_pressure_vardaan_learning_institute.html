Force and Pressure

1. Turning Effect of Force (Moment of Force)

When a force is applied on a body pivoted at a point, the body tends to move about the pivoted point. The turning effect of a single force about a fixed point (pivot) is called the moment of force.

Concept

Moment of Force (M) = F × d

Where F is the applied force and d is the perpendicular distance from the line of action of the force to the pivot.

SI Unit: Newton-metre (Nm).

Factors Affecting the Turning Effect

  • The magnitude of the force applied.
  • The perpendicular distance from the fixed point to the line of action of the force.
Fact

A pair of equal-sized turning forces acting in different directions is called a couple. The turning moment caused by a couple is called a torque.

Examples from Daily Life

  • Doors: Less force is needed to open a door by pushing at the edge farthest from the hinges compared to near the hinges.
  • Hand flour grinders: Provided with a handle near its outer edge to maximize distance and minimize effort.
  • Steering Wheels: Force is applied tangentially to the rim to maximize distance from the center.

2. Pressure

A force acting normally (perpendicularly) on a surface is called a thrust.

Concept

Pressure is defined as the thrust (compressive force) acting per unit area.

Formula: Pressure (P) = Force (F) / Area (A)

SI Unit: N/m² or Pascal (Pa).

Important

1 Pascal (Pa) is the pressure exerted by a force of 1 N acting over an area of 1 m².

Pressure is directly proportional to the thrust and inversely proportional to the area of cross-section.

Applications Based on Pressure

  • Camel in Desert: A camel exerts less pressure on sand compared to horses due to broader feet, preventing them from sinking.
  • Heavy Vehicles: Rear wheels of buses/trucks are doubled, and army tanks use wide steel tracks to increase surface area, thereby reducing pressure on the ground.
  • Skiing: Flat and long skis increase the surface area to reduce pressure on the snow, preventing the skier from sinking.
  • Cutting Tools: Knives, axes, and pins are sharpened to reduce the area of cross-section, sharply increasing the pressure to penetrate surfaces easily.

3. Pressure Exerted by Liquids

Unlike solids, fluids exert pressure not only at the base but also on the sides of the vessel. This is known as lateral pressure.

Fact

Liquid Pressure Formula: P = h × d × g

  • h: Height (depth) of liquid.
  • d: Density of liquid.
  • g: Acceleration due to gravity.

Key Properties of Liquid Pressure

  • Pressure in a fluid is directly proportional to its depth and density.
  • Pressure in a fluid is independent of the area of cross-section or shape of the containing vessel.
  • Liquids always seek their own level.
  • At a given depth, a liquid exerts the same pressure in all directions.
Important

Pascal's Law: When pressure is applied to any part of an enclosed liquid, it is transmitted equally in all directions with undiminished force and acts at right angles to the surface of the vessel.

Consequences of Liquid Pressure

  • Dam Construction: Walls of dams are made very thick at the base to withstand the huge lateral pressure of deep water.
  • Deep-Sea Life: Deep-sea fishes burst when brought to the surface due to high internal blood pressure no longer balanced by external water pressure.
  • Deep-Sea Divers & Submarines: Divers wear specially designed pressure suits, and submarines have highly strengthened hulls to prevent crushing from the massive water pressure at great depths.

4. Atmospheric Pressure

Our earth is surrounded by an envelope of air called the atmosphere. The force exerted by the atmosphere per unit area on the surface of the earth is called atmospheric pressure.

Fact

The average atmospheric pressure at sea level is approximately 100,000 Pa. This is equivalent to the pressure exerted by a 76 cm vertical height of a mercury column or a 10.34 m vertical height of a water column.

Common Phenomena

  • Nosebleeds at high altitudes: Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude, but blood pressure remains high, causing capillaries in the nose to burst.
  • Leaking pens: Ink fountain pens leak on airplanes/hills because the air inside the pen is at a higher pressure than the outside atmosphere.
  • Suction pads: When pressed, air is forced out, and the larger external atmospheric pressure holds the pad firmly against the wall.
  • Drinking straws & syringes: Drawing fluid works due to a created pressure difference; atmospheric pressure forces the liquid up into the lower pressure zone.

Measurement of Atmospheric Pressure

The instrument used to measure atmospheric pressure is called a Barometer, originally constructed by Torricelli.

Concept

Torricellian Vacuum: The empty space created above the mercury column in a simple barometer tube.

Why is mercury used as a barometric liquid instead of water?

  • Mercury is the heaviest liquid (density 13.6 g/cm³), meaning a short column (76 cm) can counterbalance normal atmospheric pressure. A water barometer would require an impractical 10.34 m long tube.
  • Mercury does not vaporize under vacuum conditions, allowing for accurate readings. (Water would vaporize in the Torricellian vacuum).
  • Mercury does not wet the sides of the glass tube.
Important

The Lift Pump (Water Pump): Works on the principle that air exerts pressure and can force groundwater upwards when a pressure difference is created by a mechanical piston and valves.
