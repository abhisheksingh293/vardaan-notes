ENERGY

1. Energy, Work and Their Relation

All living beings perform basic activities to survive, which require energy derived from food. Heavy activities (like moving loads) require more energy than lighter activities (like reading). When animal or human energy is insufficient, machines utilize fuels like petrol and diesel to perform heavy work.

Concept Energy: The capacity (or ability) of a body to do work is called energy.

An object that does work loses energy, while the object on which work is done gains energy. In physics, work is distinctly defined and does not include mental activities like thinking or reading.

Concept Work: Work is said to be done only when the applied force causes some displacement of a body in its own direction.

Factors Determining Work

The amount of work done depends on two critical factors:

  • Magnitude of Force: More force applied results in more work done.
  • Displacement: More displacement of a body in the direction of the force results in more work done.
Fact Mathematically, work is the product of Force (F) and Displacement (S).

W = F × S
Important Zero Work Scenarios: Work is considered ZERO in physics if:
  • The force does not cause any displacement (e.g., pushing a solid wall).
  • The displacement is at right angles (90°) to the direction of the applied force (e.g., walking on a flat ground while holding a heavy load on your head).

2. Units of Work and Energy

Work and Energy share the same units of measurement because the total amount of work done by a body is numerically equal to its energy.

Fact Joule (J): The SI system unit of work and energy. 1 Joule of work is done when a force of 1 Newton causes a displacement of 1 metre in its own direction. (1 J = 1 N × 1 m)

Calorie is an older unit of energy, traditionally used for heat.

  • 1 Calorie is the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of 1 gram of water through 1°C.
  • The relationship between Joules and Calories is: 1 calorie ≈ 4.2 Joules (or exactly 4.18 J).
  • A larger unit, the Kilocalorie, is equal to 1000 calories.

3. Various Forms of Energy

Energy exists in multiple forms depending upon its source and nature:

Mechanical Energy

The energy possessed by a body due to a displacement caused by the application of force.

  • Potential Energy: Energy possessed by a body on account of its position or configuration (e.g., a stretched bow, water at a height).
  • Kinetic Energy: Energy possessed by a body by virtue of its motion (e.g., a speeding train, flowing water).

Other Forms of Energy

  • Heat Energy: Released when fuels like coal, oil, or gas burn. Used for cooking and running steam engines.
  • Light Energy: A form of energy that makes objects visible. Plants convert it into food via photosynthesis.
  • Sound Energy: Produced by vibrating bodies. It can cause movement, such as shaking windows during a loud thunderclap.
  • Electric Energy: Essential for modern life. Runs motors, heaters, and lighting devices.
  • Magnetic Energy: Possessed by magnets, capable of causing motion in magnetic substances (like iron).
  • Chemical Energy: The hidden energy in the atoms of elements or compounds. Found in food, batteries, and fuels.
  • Nuclear Energy: Energy released due to mass loss when a heavy nucleus splits or light nuclei combine (e.g., atomic bombs, nuclear reactors).

4. Interconversion of Energy

Energy continuously transforms from one form to another. Common examples include:

  • Electric to Light & Heat: An electric bulb turning on.
  • Chemical to Electric: A battery powering a device.
  • Potential to Kinetic: A stretched bow string released to fire an arrow.
  • Sound to Electric: Speaking into a microphone.
  • Light to Electric: Solar panels (photovoltaic cells) capturing sunlight.

5. Sun: The Ultimate Source of Energy

Almost all forms of energy on Earth originate directly or indirectly from the sun.

  • Solar energy drives the water cycle, creating flowing water (Hydroelectricity).
  • It causes wind currents (Wind energy).
  • Plants store solar energy as chemical energy during photosynthesis, which eventually forms fossil fuels (coal, petroleum) over millions of years.

6. Law of Conservation of Energy

Important The Law States: Energy is neither created nor destroyed in a system. The total energy remains the same. It can only be transformed from one form to another.

Example - Roller Coaster: A roller coaster at the top of a hill has maximum Potential Energy and zero Kinetic Energy. As it drops, its height decreases (losing Potential Energy) and speed increases (gaining Kinetic Energy). At any given point on the track, the sum total of its Kinetic and Potential energy remains perfectly constant.
