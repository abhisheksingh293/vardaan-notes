SOUND

1. Sound, its Production and Propagation

Sound is a fascinating form of energy that we experience daily. We recognize sounds produced by living beings and non-living things even without seeing them.

Concept

Longitudinal Wave: When the particles of a medium oscillate in the same direction in which the wave is being propagated, the wave formed is called a longitudinal wave.

Sound waves produced in air are an example of a longitudinal wave. As sound propagates, it forms alternating regions of compressions and rarefactions.

Vibrations are the Cause of Sound

When a particle (or body) moves to and fro about its mean position, it is said to vibrate. Every source of sound is a vibrating body.

2. Sources of Sound

  • Tuning Fork: A U-shaped fork of steel provided with a steel handle. The two arms are called prongs. It is a convenient source of producing pure sound in a laboratory.
  • Human Beings: Humans produce sound when vocal cords stretch across the voice box (larynx) and vibrate as air from the lungs passes through a narrow slit between them.

Musical Instruments

  • Wind Instruments: Flute, trumpet. Sound is produced by the vibration of an enclosed column of air.
  • Reed Instruments: Harmonium, mouth organ. Sound is produced by thin metal strips called reeds vibrating.
  • Stringed Instruments: Guitar, violin, sitar. Sound is produced by the vibrations of stretched strings on plucking or bowing.
  • Percussion Instruments: Drum, tabla. Sound is produced when a stretched leather membrane is made to vibrate by striking.

3. Propagation of Sound

For sound to be heard, three conditions are necessary:

  1. A vibrating body to produce sound.
  2. A material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to transmit the vibrations.
  3. A receiver (such as a human ear) to interpret the sound.
Important

Sound Needs a Medium: Sound cannot travel through a vacuum. The Bell Jar experiment proves that as air is withdrawn from a glass jar containing an electric bell, the sound fades away completely.

Speed of Sound in Different Mediums

Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids, and slowest in gases. For example:

  • Air (Gas): ~346 m/s at 25°C
  • Water (Liquid): ~1498 m/s
  • Steel/Iron (Solid): ~5100 to 5950 m/s
Fact

Lightning vs Thunder: Light travels extremely fast (3 × 10⁸ m/s), while sound travels slowly in air (~330 m/s). This is why a lightning flash is seen instantly, but the crack of thunder is heard a few seconds later.

4. Characteristics of a Sound Wave

The three most important characteristics of vibrations are:

Definitions
  • Amplitude: The maximum displacement of a vibrating body from its mean position. Its SI unit is the metre (m).
  • Frequency: The number of vibrations of the vibrating body in one second. Its SI unit is hertz (Hz).
  • Time Period: The time taken by the vibrating body to complete one vibration. Its SI unit is the second (s).

Relation: Frequency (v) is inversely proportional to Time Period (T): v = 1/T

5. Range of Hearing

Our ears are sensitive to a specific range of frequencies:

  • Audible Sound: Between 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.
  • Infrasonic (Subsonic) Sound: Less than 20 Hz (Inaudible to humans).
  • Ultrasonic Sound: Higher than 20,000 Hz (Inaudible to humans).
Applications

Uses of Ultrasonic Waves:

  • SONAR: (Sound Navigation and Ranging) Used in ships to detect submarines, icebergs, and map the depth of ocean beds.
  • Animals: Bats use echoes of ultrasonic sound to navigate in the dark. Dogs can hear up to 40,000 Hz (trained using a Galton's whistle). Dolphins use ultrasonic squeaks to find prey.

6. Reflection and Absorption of Sound

Sound energy bounces off hard surfaces, obeying the same laws of reflection as light. However, materials with a loose or porous texture (like wool, carpets, and gunny bags) absorb sound. This principle is used to design sound-proof rooms or cinema halls to prevent echoing.

Echo

The repetition of sound reflected from a high building or distant object is called an echo.

Because the sensation of sound persists in our ear for 1/10th of a second, an echo can only be heard if the reflecting body is at a minimum distance of 17 metres from the source.

7. Loudness, Pitch, and Quality

Important

Loudness vs Pitch

  • Loudness depends upon the amplitude of the vibrating body. Larger amplitude = Louder sound. Loudness decreases as distance increases. It is measured in decibels (dB).
  • Pitch depends upon the frequency. Higher frequency produces a shrill sound (high pitch, like a child's voice). Lower frequency produces a bass/flat sound (low pitch).

Quality (Timbre)

Timbre is the property that allows us to distinguish between two sounds of the same pitch and loudness coming from different instruments (e.g., a guitar and a piano). It depends on the waveform.

  • Tone: A pure sound of a single frequency.
  • Note: A sound produced by a mixture of multiple frequencies (pleasant to hear).
