THE VIJAYANAGARA AND THE BAHMANI KINGDOMS

The Delhi Sultanate reached its peak of territorial expansion during the reign of Muhammad-bin-Tughlaq. However, internal weaknesses and external invasions led to its decline, paving the way for the rise of powerful regional kingdoms, most notably in South India.

1. Decline of the Delhi Sultanate

The disintegration of the Turko-Afghan empire started during the reign of the Tughlaqs. The primary reasons for this decline include:

  • Absence of Popular Support: The empire relied on military strength, not goodwill. Weakening military power led generals to revolt.
  • Inherent Weaknesses: Built on the power of the sword, it lacked the foundation of public goodwill.
  • Absence of Strong Rulers: After Firoz Shah Tughlaq, a succession of weak and inefficient kings hastened the decline.
  • Unenlightened Policy: Orthodox and intolerant views alienated the Hindu majority, depriving the government of moral support.
  • Mistakes of Muhammad-bin-Tughlaq: His foolish projects plunged the country into chaos.
Important Invasion of Timur: The final blow that shattered the disintegrating Turko-Afghan empire was the devastating invasion by Timur.

2. Rise of Regional Kingdoms

As the Delhi Sultanate weakened, several independent regional kingdoms emerged across the subcontinent:

  • Malwa: Dilawar Khan Ghori declared independence in 1401-1402. Hoshang Shah was a powerful later ruler.
  • Jaunpur: Malik Sarwar established the Sharqi dynasty. It was eventually annexed by Bahlul Lodi in 1483-1484.
  • Bengal: Ruled by the Ilyas Shahi and Husain Shahi dynasties. Later conquered by Sher Shah Suri and Humayun.
  • Kashmir: Zain-ul-Abidin was a liberal, enlightened ruler, often called the "Akbar of Kashmir." Akbar annexed it in 1586.
  • Mewar (Rajput State): Rana Kumbha and Rana Sanga were powerful rulers. Rana Sanga was later defeated by Babur at the Battle of Khanua.
  • Marwar (Rajput State): Ruled by the Rathore dynasty. Rao Jodha founded Jodhpur; Maldeva was a significant ruler.
  • Gujarat: Zafar Khan established an independent kingdom (Muzaffar Shah). Notable rulers include Ahmad Shah and Mahmud Begarha. Annexed by Akbar.
  • Khandesh: Malik Raja declared independence from the Tughlaqs. It was later annexed by Akbar.

3. Rise of the Vijayanagara Kingdom

In the 14th century, two powerful kingdoms arose in South India: the Bahmani Sultanate and the Vijayanagara Empire.

The Vijayanagara kingdom was established in 1336 by two brothers, Harihara and Bukka, who had previously served in Muhammad-bin-Tughlaq's army. They founded their capital, Vijayanagara (City of Victory), on the banks of the river Tungabhadra. Their dynasty was known as the Sangama dynasty.

Harihara I took the title of chief rather than king initially to avoid angering Muslim Sultans. He was succeeded by Bukka. They constantly battled neighboring kingdoms like the Hoysalas and the Bahmani rulers.

Dynasties in Vijayanagara (1336 - 1565+)

  • Sangama: In power till 1485.
  • Saluva: In power till 1505.
  • Tuluva: In power till 1565.
  • Aravidu: Ruled till the seventeenth century.
Fact Many foreign travellers, including Nicolo Conti, Fernao Nuniz, Domingo Paes, Duarte Barbosa, and Abdur Razzaq, visited Vijayanagara and wrote extensively about its immense magnificence and wealth.

4. Krishnadeva Raya (1509-1530): A Case Study

Krishnadeva Raya was the greatest ruler of the Vijayanagara Empire, belonging to the Tuluva dynasty. He was a brilliant commander and efficient administrator.

  • Military Campaigns: He shattered the Adil Shahi forces of Bijapur, attacked Gulbarga, and defeated independent kingdoms that arose from the Bahmani ruins. He took the title Yavanarajyasthapanacharya.
  • Administration: Maintained strict law and order, promoted agriculture by building tanks and dams, and encouraged foreign trade with the Portuguese, English, and Dutch.
  • Literature: A great scholar, he wrote the Telugu poem Amuktamalyada and the Sanskrit play Jambavati Kalyana. His court featured eight eminent Telugu scholars known as the Ashtadiggajas (one of whom was Tenali Rama).
  • Architecture: Founded a township called Nagalapuram. Built the famous Vittalaswami temple and Hazara Rama temple in Hampi. Added impressive gopurams to many South Indian temples, including Tirupati.

5. Achievements of the Vijayanagara Empire

Administration & Revenue

  • Centralized System: The king was supreme, assisted by a council of ministers (mantriparishad) and a secretariat (sachivalaya).
  • Provincial Division: Empire divided into Mandalams (provinces ruled by royal princes), Rajyas, Nadu (districts), and Grama (villages).
  • Nayankar System: A feudal system where administrative posts became hereditary, and the king grew dependent on feudal lords.
  • Revenue: Land revenue was the main source (1/6 to 1/8 of produce). Taxes on trade, crafts, property, and even marriages were imposed. (Widow remarriage was tax-free to encourage it).

Art, Architecture & Literature

The rulers produced a new architectural style called the Vijayanagara style or Dravida style.

  • Features: Elaborately sculptured pillars (often depicting horses), the Kalyana Mandapam (open pavilion), and massive Raya Gopurams (towers).
  • Capital: Hampi was the magnificent capital, boasting temples where the central deity was installed in the Garbhagriha (sanctum).
  • Literature: Patronized works in Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada, and Tamil. Krishnadeva Raya was known as Andhra Bhoja.
Important Decline of Vijayanagara: After Krishnadeva Raya's death, the empire weakened. In 1565, at the Battle of Talikota, the combined forces of the Deccan Sultanates (Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda, Bidar) defeated and killed Rama Raya. Vijayanagara was pillaged and ruined.

6. The Bahmani Kingdom (1347-1527)

Conflicts between Deccan officers and the Delhi Sultanate led to the establishment of the independent Bahmani state in 1347, initially with its capital at Gulbarga.

Its founder was Hasan Gangu, who assumed the title Ala-ud-din Hasan Bahman Shah.

Important Rulers

  • Firoz Shah Bahmani (1397-1422): Defeated the Vijayanagara king Deva Raya I (though later lost to him). Built an astronomical observatory in Daulatabad. Made the Deccan a cultural center. Shifted capital to Bidar.
  • Mahmud Gawan (1463-1481): An Iranian trader who became the Prime Minister (wazir). He managed the kingdom for 18 years, expanding its borders. A scholar himself, he built a famous madrasa in Bidar.
Concept Deccani vs. Pardesi Conflict: The Bahmani nobility was divided into Deccanis (older immigrant Muslims settled in Deccan) and Pardesis (recent immigrants from Central Asia/Iran). This constant feud weakened the state. Mahmud Gawan (a Pardesi) was executed due to this internal conspiracy.

7. Achievements of the Bahmani Empire

  • Administration: Divided into provinces called Tarafs, governed by Tarafdars who had vast civil and military powers. Senior officials included the wazir (prime minister), amir-i-jumla (finance), and wazir-i-ashraf (foreign affairs).
  • Architecture: Built many forts (Gulbarga, Daulatabad, Bidar). Famous structures include the Jama Masjid, Bala Hisar, and Mahmud Gawan's madrasa. Their exquisite tombs featured flat domes and enameled tile work. The Gol Gumbaz in Bijapur and Charminar in Hyderabad are world-famous later examples of this architectural lineage.

8. Decline of the Bahmani Kingdom

After the unjust execution of Mahmud Gawan, the empire weakened under incompetent rulers. By 1527, it disintegrated into five independent Deccan Sultanates:

  1. Ahmadnagar (Nizam Shahis)
  2. Bijapur (Adil Shahis)
  3. Golconda (Qutb Shahis)
  4. Berar (Imad Shahis)
  5. Bidar (Barid Shahis)
