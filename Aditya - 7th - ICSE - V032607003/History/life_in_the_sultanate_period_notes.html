LIFE IN THE SULTANATE PERIOD

The Delhi Sultanate ruled over most parts of the Indian subcontinent from 1206 to 1526. Because of this, Islamic, Turkish, and Afghan traditions have left their mark on Indian life, language, art, and architecture. This fusion of Indian and Islamic culture gave birth to a unique Indo-Islamic culture.

1. Society in the Sultanate Period

The society during the Delhi Sultanate was broadly divided into four major groups: aristocracy, priests, townspeople, and peasants.

  • Aristocracy: Included the Sultan, his relatives, courtiers, iqta holders, and Hindu and Muslim chieftains. They held almost all the wealth and power, leading lives of immense luxury.
  • Priests: Comprised the Ulemas (Islamic scholars) and Brahmins. They were often granted tax-free land and became so powerful that they frequently influenced the policies of the Sultan.
  • Townspeople: Included wealthy merchants, traders, artisans, and brokers. They lived in flourishing towns that gradually became centers of administration.
  • Peasants: Lived in villages and paid taxes to the state as land revenue. They were often exploited, struggled hard to make ends meet, and saw virtually no change in their lifestyle regardless of which dynasty ruled.
Important Social Practices: The caste system and polygamy were prevalent. The practice of child marriage, sati, the purdah system, and the strict taboo on widow remarriage continued. Women could not inherit property and received education mostly at home.

2. Trade and Commerce

During the 13th and 14th centuries, many towns emerged, leading to a growing demand for raw materials and food. Both inland and overseas trade flourished. India traded with China, the Malaya Islands, West and Central Asia, and East Africa.

  • Imports: Horses and precious metals like gold and silver.
  • Exports: Luxury items like silk were in high demand.
  • Merchant Classes: Karwanis were merchants specializing in carrying grains. Multanis handled long-distance trade and were so wealthy they frequently lent money to nobles. Brokers formed another important class, acting as middlemen taking commissions.

3. Administration

The administration of the Sultanate dynasty was based on Shariyat laws (laws of Islam). The Sultan was the head of the state, vested with political, legal, and military authority.

Concept Key Administrative Departments:
• Diwan-i-Wizarat: The Finance Department, headed by the Wazir (Prime Minister).
• Diwan-i-Arz: The Defence Ministry, headed by the Ariz-i-mamalik (responsible for army maintenance).
• Diwan-i-Insha: The department of royal correspondence and records, headed by the Dabir-i-mamalik.
• Diwan-i-Risalat: The department of public charities and ecclesiastical affairs, headed by the Sadr-us-sudur.

4. Indo-Islamic Architecture

Architecture made tremendous progress, witnessing the growth of the Indo-Islamic style. Muslim rulers employed Indian craftsmen, resulting in a synthesis of both styles.

Key Features of Sultanate Architecture

  • The Turks introduced domes (large areas enclosed by hollow, semicircular roofs).
  • Use of the true arch (pointed arch supported by stones placed obliquely, not by a beam).
  • Construction of lofty towers and minarets (long, slender towers).
  • Decorations utilized geometric shapes (square, triangular, rectangular) and teachings of the Quran inscribed in Persian script, moving away from the Hindu tradition of sculpting images of deities.
Fact The Qutub Minar is the highest stone tower in India (72.5 meters), built originally by Qutb-ud-din Aibak and completed by Iltutmish. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site featuring intricate Arabic and Nagari inscriptions.

5. Language, Literature, and Arts

Under the patronage of the Turko-Afghan rulers, significant works were produced in both literature and fine arts.

Language & Literature

  • Persian became the court language.
  • Amir Khusrau: A famous Persian writer (1252-1325) who created a new style of Persian poetry called Sabaq-i-Hind (the Indian style).
  • Growth of Urdu: A major literary achievement was the evolution of Urdu, which emerged from the mingling of Persian, Arabic, and Turkish words with Sanskrit-origin languages.
  • Regional Languages: Supported by the Bhakti movement, languages like Hindi, Marathi, Bengali, and Gujarati flourished. Saints like Kabir and Tulsidas preached in Hindi.

Music, Dance, and Painting

  • Music: Amir Khusrau (known as the Father of Hindustani Classical Music) introduced new Persian-Arabic ragas and is credited with inventing the sitar. He evolved qawwali by blending Hindu and Persian styles. New instruments like the sarangi and rabab were also introduced.
  • Dance: Kathak gained prominence. It was a storytelling dance form combining Hindu themes with Persian customs.
  • Painting: The art of miniature painting developed, characterized by a fusion of newly introduced Persian and traditional Indian styles.

6. Cultural Changes

The mingling of Indian and Turkish populations led to a rich, composite culture affecting daily life:

  • Clothing: Hindus and Muslims alike adopted garments like the pyjama-kurta, kaftans, and salwar kameez.
  • Cuisine: New foods such as biryani, bread, wine, and the Central Asian snack samosa became staples of the Indian diet.
  • Medicine: The Turks introduced the Unani system of medicine to India.
