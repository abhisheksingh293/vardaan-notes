The Cell

1. Introduction to the Cell

Living things are made up of tiny living parts or compartments called cells. A cell is defined as the basic structural and functional unit of all living things. The cells are considered as the building blocks of life. Based on the number of cells, living organisms are classified into two types:

  • Unicellular Organisms: Living organisms made up of only a single cell. They are often microscopic (too small to be seen with the naked eye). Examples: Amoeba, Paramecium, Euglena, Bacteria, and Yeast.
  • Multicellular Organisms: Organisms made of many different types of cells adapted to perform particular functions. Examples: All higher plants and animals.

2. Discovery of the Cell & Cell Theory

The cell was discovered by an English scientist, Robert Hooke in 1665. He observed thin slices of dead cork under his crude microscope and found box-like compartments forming a honeycomb structure. He named these compartments "cells".

Important The Cell Theory was formulated by scientists Schleiden and Schwann. They stated that all organisms are made up of cells and their products. Later, Virchow proposed an important addition: "All cells arise by the division of pre-existing cells."

3. Cell Shape and Size

Cells exhibit a variety of shapes and sizes to perform specific functions in the body:

  • Red Blood Cells (RBC): Disc-shaped to easily carry oxygen throughout the human body.
  • White Blood Cells (WBC): Amoeboid (irregular) in shape so they can squeeze through blood vessels to reach sites of injury or infection.
  • Spirogyra: Multicellular, filamentous, and cylindrical algae.
  • Chlamydomonas: Unicellular and oval-shaped algae.

Size Differences

The size of a cell differs greatly across organisms. The largest known cell is the Ostrich egg. The longest cell in the human body is the nerve cell, and the smallest cell in the living world is found among bacteria (Mycoplasma).

4. Structure of a Cell

All the living plant and animal cells contain a living substance called protoplasm. Protoplasm has three main parts: the cell membrane, cytoplasm, and the nucleus.

Concept Protoplasm is the complete living substance of a cell. It includes the cell membrane, the nucleus, and the jelly-like cytoplasm filling the rest of the cell.

Cell (Plasma) Membrane

It is a very thin, living outer boundary of the cell. It allows only certain molecules to enter and prevents the entry of other molecules. Therefore, it is known as a selectively permeable membrane.

Cell Wall (Plants Only)

An extra protective covering that surrounds the cell membrane in plant cells. It is made of a stiff, non-living material called cellulose. It provides rigidity and shape to the plant cell. (Note: Cell wall is absent in animal cells).

Cytoplasm

The jelly-like substance occupying the space between the cell membrane and the nucleus. All life functions take place here. It contains various cell organelles.

The Nucleus

The nucleus is the most prominent part of the cell and acts as the control centre or brain of the cell, regulating all activities. Interestingly, mature human Red Blood Cells (RBCs) lack a nucleus. The nucleus consists of four parts:

  • Nuclear membrane: The outermost covering of the nucleus with tiny pores.
  • Nucleoplasm: The jelly-like fluid inside the nucleus.
  • Nucleolus: A small spherical body inside the nucleus.
  • Chromatin network: A network of thread-like structures. During cell division, chromatin condenses to form chromosomes, which play an important role in the inheritance of characters from parents to offspring.

5. Cell Organelles

Organelles are minute structures found in the cytoplasm that perform specific functions for the cell's survival.

  • Mitochondria: Small, rod-shaped organelles. They perform respiration and provide energy to the cell. Hence, they are called the Powerhouse of the cell.
  • Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER): A network of tube-like structures. It provides a large surface area for life functions and gives support to the cell. It can be rough (with ribosomes) or smooth.
  • Golgi Complex: Consists of stacks of flat membranes. It collects, synthesizes, and distributes substances made in the cell.
  • Lysosomes: Small spherical bodies containing digestive enzymes. They break down or destroy old, infected cells. Thus, they are called the 'suicide bags' of the cell.
  • Ribosomes: Membraneless tiny granules that act as the site of protein synthesis.
  • Centrosome: Located near the nucleus in animal cells only. It helps in cell division.

Plastids (Plant Cells Only)

Double-membrane bound dense bodies found only in plant cells. There are three types:

  • Chloroplasts: The most conspicuous plastids. They contain the green pigment chlorophyll which helps trap sunlight for photosynthesis (The Kitchen of the cell).
  • Chromoplasts: Contain coloured pigments (red, yellow, orange) that give colour to flowers and fruits.
  • Leucoplasts: Colourless plastids that store starch, proteins, and oils (mostly found in seeds).

Vacuoles

Clear spaces in the cytoplasm surrounded by a single membrane called the tonoplast. They store cell sap, food, and waste products. Vacuoles are generally absent or very small in animal cells, but plant cells possess a large central vacuole that provides structural support.

Fact In simple organisms like Amoeba, the vacuoles contain food particles and are appropriately termed food vacuoles.

6. Differences Between Animal and Plant Cells

While plant and animal cells share common features (like the plasma membrane, nucleus, mitochondria, ER, and Golgi complex), they have distinct differences:

  • Cell Wall: Absent in animal cells; Present (made of rigid cellulose) in plant cells.
  • Plastids/Chloroplasts: Absent in animal cells; Present in plant cells.
  • Centrosome: Present in animal cells (helps in cell division); Absent in plant cells.
  • Vacuoles: Absent or very small and numerous in animal cells; Large in size and fewer in number (usually one large central vacuole) in plant cells.
