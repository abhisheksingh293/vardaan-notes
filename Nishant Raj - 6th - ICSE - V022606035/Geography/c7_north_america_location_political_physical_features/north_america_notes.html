North America: Location, Political and Physical Features

1. Introduction

North America is the third-largest continent in the world. It was historically referred to as the "New World" by explorers.

Important

America was discovered by Christopher Columbus in 1492. In 1507, the New World came to be known as 'America', named after the Italian explorer and cartographer Amerigo Vespucci.

2. Location and Boundaries

North America is roughly triangular in shape and lies entirely in the Northern and Western Hemispheres.

  • North: Surrounded by the Arctic Ocean.
  • East: Bordered by the Atlantic Ocean.
  • West: Bordered by the Pacific Ocean.
  • South: Bounded by the Caribbean Sea.

The continent extends approximately from 7°N to 84°N latitude and from 20°W to 165°W longitude. The 100°W meridian passes right through the middle of the continent.

Concept

Connecting Links: The narrow Bering Strait separates North America from the continent of Asia in the northwest. In the south, the Isthmus of Panama joins North America to South America.

3. Political Divisions

North America consists of 23 independent countries. About 80 percent of the North American landmass is occupied by just two countries: Canada and the United States of America (USA).

  • Central America: The continent becomes narrower towards the south, forming Central America (includes countries like Nicaragua, Guatemala, Panama, Costa Rica, El Salvador, and Belize).
  • The Caribbean: A group of island nations south-east of the mainland, including Cuba, Dominican Republic, Haiti, and Jamaica, often referred to as the West Indies.
Fact

Greenland is the largest island in the world. While it is geographically a part of the North American continent, it politically belongs to Denmark.

4. Major Physical Features

North America can be divided into four major physical divisions:

A. The Canadian Shield

Also known as the Laurentian Plateau, it is located in the extreme North of the continent around the Hudson Bay. It is made of very old, hard rocks that are extremely rich in minerals. During the Ice Age, moving glaciers carved out large hollows here, which later filled with water to form lakes. The famous Five Great Lakes (Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie, and Ontario) form the southern boundary of the shield.

B. The Western Cordilleras

These are young, fold mountains that run parallel to the west coast from Alaska to Panama. They were formed around the same time as the Himalayas.

  • The highest mountain peak is Mount McKinley (6,194 m) located in the Alaska Range.
  • Other important ranges include the Rocky Mountains, Cascade Range, Sierra Nevada, and Sierra Madre.
  • This region features hot springs, geysers (like those in Yellowstone National Park), and active volcanoes that are part of the 'Pacific Ring of Fire'.

C. The Central Lowlands

Also known as the Prairies, these gently undulating plains are found between the Western Cordilleras and the Appalachian Highlands. They were formed by silt deposited by rivers, making the soil highly fertile. They are drained primarily by the Mackenzie River in the north and the Mississippi-Missouri river system in the south.

D. The Appalachian Highlands (Eastern Highlands)

These are old, fold mountains located in the east, stretching from Newfoundland to Atlanta. Because of their age, they have been eroded and are not very high.

They are famous for the "Fall Line"—a point where rivers drop sharply from the plateau to the coastal plain, creating waterfalls that are used to generate hydroelectricity.

5. Important Waterbodies

North America has an excellent network of rivers that aid in transportation, agriculture, and power generation:

  • North-Flowing: The Mackenzie (flows into the Arctic Ocean) and Nelson rivers.
  • East-Flowing: The St. Lawrence River, which combines with the Great Lakes to form the busiest inland waterway in the world.
  • South-Flowing: The Mississippi-Missouri system (the largest river system in North America, flowing into the Gulf of Mexico) and the Rio Grande.
  • West-Flowing: The Yukon, Columbia, Fraser, and the Colorado River (famous for carving the Grand Canyon and housing the Hoover Dam).

6. Case Study: The Lumberjacks of Canada

Canada has vast stretches of coniferous forests (taiga) extending from Newfoundland to British Columbia. These forests consist of pure stands (single species over large areas), making clear-felling possible. Lumbering is the commercial extraction of wood, and the people who do this tough, physical work are called Lumberjacks.

Lumbering is mostly done during the winter season. The frozen sap makes the trees easier to cut, and the slippery snow allows logs to be easily dragged to the frozen rivers. When the ice thaws in spring, the logs float down to sawmills.

Types of Lumberjacks

  • High-riggers: Highly skilled workers who climb tall trees to chop off branches and the top before the tree is felled.
  • Sawyers (Buckers): They cut the tree trunk and branches into appropriately sized logs before they are sent to mills. This process is called limbing.
  • Skidders: Their job is to drag the cut logs out of the forest to landing sites (like roads or frozen rivers) using tractors and chains.
  • Transporters (Haulers): They manage the logs as they float down the river. Armed with spiked poles (and sometimes dynamite), they jump from log to log to prevent logjams.
