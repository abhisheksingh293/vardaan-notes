Force

1. What is Force?

In simple terms, the word force is associated with either a pull or push which causes a change in the state of rest or motion of a body. Based on "PDF Gallery_20260726_215420.pdf", force is applied continuously in our daily activities.

Concept Force is an external agent which changes or tends to change the state of rest or uniform motion of a body, or changes its direction or shape.

Effects of Force

From the effects of force, we mean what force can do or what changes a force can bring about. A force can:

  • Cause motion in a stationary object: For example, pushing a book placed on a table makes it move.
  • Stop moving objects or slow them down: Applying brakes to a moving bicycle slows it down and eventually stops it. A cricket fielder stops a ball by applying force in the opposite direction.
  • Make a moving object move faster: Pressing the accelerator of a moving vehicle or hitting a moving football makes it travel faster.
  • Change the direction of moving objects: A cricket player hitting a ball with a bat changes its speed and direction.
  • Change the shape of objects: Pulling a spring stretches it. Crumpling a piece of paper or shaping plasticine changes their forms.
Important A force cannot cause any effect on the mass of an object. Mass is an inherent property of a matter and is a constant quantity.

2. Classification of Forces

Forces in nature basically act on a body directly, through a connector, or from a distance.

Contact Forces

All forces which act on a body directly or through a connector are called contact forces. A push can act through a rigid connector only, while a pull can act through both rigid (metal handle) and non-rigid (rope) connectors.

  • Muscular Forces: Also called biological force. It is the force produced by the muscles of living beings (e.g., pulling a cart, lifting a heavy weight, animals ploughing fields).
  • Mechanical Forces: Forces generated by a machine. Machines need energy (petrol, electrical, wind, flowing water) to produce this force.

Non-Contact Forces (Forces at a Distance)

The forces which do not make direct contact with a body but act through space are called non-contact forces.

  • Gravitational Force: The Earth pulls all objects towards its centre. This pull is called the force of gravity. It is always attractive and gives an object its weight.
  • Electrical Force (Electrostatic): The force with which an electrically charged object can pull or push other charged objects. It can be attractive as well as repulsive.
  • Magnetic Force: The strange property of a magnet attracting iron, steel, cobalt, or nickel towards itself. Magnetic force can easily pass through substances like wood or glass.

Consequential Forces

These forces do not exist on their own. They are called into play when a force is applied on a given body.

  • Example 1: When a cricket ball rolls on the ground, the force of friction is called into play, acting in the opposite direction to stop it.
  • Example 2: Stretching a spring calls into play the consequential force of tension, which tends to bring the spring back to its original shape.

3. Force of Friction

Concept Friction refers to the resistance to motion that occurs whenever two bodies are in contact with each other. A force which acts at the surface of contact, when one body moves upon another body, is called the force of friction.

Types of Friction

  • Static friction: The frictional force that comes into play when one body tends to move over another, but the actual motion has yet not started.
  • Sliding friction: The force that comes into play when one body is actually sliding over the surface of another. It is less than static friction.
  • Rolling friction: The force that comes into play when a wheel, sphere, or cylinder rolls over a surface without slipping.
Fact For the same weight, rolling friction is much smaller than static or sliding friction. This is why the discovery of the wheel has been a major milestone in human history!

Effects of Friction

  • Opposes motion: It always acts in the direction opposite to the applied force.
  • Produces heat: Rubbing palms together or a coin on a table generates heat energy.
  • Causes wear and tear: Soles of shoes and tyres of vehicles wear out over time due to friction.

4. Friction in Daily Life

Advantages of Friction

  • It helps us to walk without slipping by pushing the ground backwards.
  • Deep grooves in tyres offer large friction to prevent vehicles from skidding on wet roads.
  • It holds knots tight (e.g., jute thread offers more friction than silk).
  • It holds nails and screws in wooden furniture.
  • It saves us from meteors; the friction between the meteor and the earth's atmosphere generates enough heat to burn them up before they reach the ground.
  • It allows writing on paper with a pencil or pen.

Disadvantages of Friction

  • Wears out the soles of shoes and vehicle tyres.
  • Wears out the moving parts of machinery.
  • Generates heat, resulting in the wastage of energy (about 20% of an automobile's fuel is wasted overcoming friction).
  • Requires a lot of energy to move heavy objects.
Important Friction: A Necessary Evil. It brings wear, tear, and wasted energy (evil), but without it, bodies wouldn't move, stop, or even allow us to walk safely (necessary).

5. Methods of Modifying Friction

Reducing Friction

  • Streamlining: Shaping an object so that it experiences minimum friction in air or water (e.g., fish, birds, aeroplanes, ships).
  • Polishing: Smoothens surface irregularities (e.g., carrom boards).
  • Lubrication: Applying light oil (for light parts), grease (for heavy parts), graphite, or talc powder to moving parts.
  • Using Wheels: Attaching wheels to a heavy suitcase dramatically decreases friction.
  • Ball Bearings: Small steel balls placed between cylindrical moving surfaces in bicycles and motors to convert sliding friction into rolling friction.

Increasing Friction

  • Providing deep grooves on vehicle tyres.
  • Providing spikes on the soles of athletic and mountaineering shoes.
  • Making industrial belts and grinding stones with rough surfaces.

6. Units and Measurement of Force

A unit is required to describe whether a force is small or big. We measure force using a spring balance, which stretches based on gravitational pull to indicate weight/force on a scale.

Concept The Standard International (SI) unit of force is the newton (N). The force required to lift 100g of mass vertically upward is called one newton.
  • Kilogram force (kgf): The force required to lift a body of mass 1 kg vertically upward.
  • Gram force (gf): The force required to lift a mass of 1 g vertically upward.
Fact Since 1 kg mass is equal to 1000g, 1 kgf = 1000 gf. Additionally, one kilogram force is equal to ten newtons: 1 kgf = 10 N.

7. Magnitude, Direction, and Addition of Forces

A force has both magnitude (amount or strength, represented by the length of a straight line) and direction (represented by an arrow head).

  • Addition: When two forces are applied at the same point and in the same direction, the resultant force (net force) is equal to the sum of the separate forces.
  • Subtraction: When two forces are applied at the same point but in opposite directions, the resultant force is the difference of the forces and acts in the direction of the larger force.
  • Cancellation: If equal forces act in opposite directions, the net force is zero.
