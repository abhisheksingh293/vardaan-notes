Overpopulation and Underpopulation

1. The Concept of Carrying Capacity

Ecologists believe that the human population, currently over 7 billion, cannot continue to grow indefinitely. There are absolute limits to the life-sustaining resources Earth can provide.

Concept

Carrying Capacity: It is the maximum number of individuals of a species that an environment can support indefinitely without degrading the natural resources.

Simply put, there are not enough resources in the world to give every human a decent standard of living if the population expands beyond this carrying capacity (which is projected to reach 9 billion by 2100).

2. Overpopulation

Overpopulation implies a scenario where a region has too many people and not enough resources (food, jobs, technology, services, and energy) to maintain a reasonable standard of living. Countries like Bangladesh and Mali are prime examples of overpopulated nations.

Causes of Overpopulation

  • Birth and Death Rates: Due to improved technology and efficient medical facilities, global birth rates have risen while death rates have declined, leading to an overall population explosion.
  • Poverty: Economically poor nations often lack the capacity to support growing populations. Ironically, families living below the poverty line often increase their family size to gain more earning hands.
  • Illiteracy and Lack of Family Planning: Literacy and population growth rates are strongly interrelated. A lack of education prevents changes in attitude toward family size and limits the enforcement of effective family planning policies.

Impact of Overpopulation

  • Depletion of Natural Resources: An insufficient supply of food and water leads to malnutrition and a lower resistance to disease. It also increases a nation's reliance on imports, ballooning the national debt.
  • Environmental Hazards: Immense pressure on land causes widespread deforestation and loss of natural habitats. High industrialization and urbanization severely increase global pollution levels and contribute to global warming.
  • Unemployment and Poverty: A massive population burdens the economy, causing a severe scarcity of jobs, low income, and a very low standard of living.
  • Health and Sanitation: Haphazard land use leads to poor infrastructure, compromised hygiene, and poor medical access, negatively impacting demographic traits like the Infant Mortality Rate (IMR).
  • Imbalance in Trade: Developing countries spend disproportionate amounts on healthcare and housing rather than industrial growth, increasing dependency on developed nations.

3. Underpopulation

Underpopulation refers to a situation where there are not enough people to fully utilize the resources and technology available in a given area. In these areas, the resources can easily support a much larger population without reducing living standards.

Examples include equatorial Congo, the Amazon River Basin, and the rich Prairie region of North America.

Causes of Underpopulation

  • Low Fertility Rate: In many developed nations, the fertility rate has dropped to about two children per woman. Literacy, economic development, and urbanization make large families impractical.
  • Emigration: The permanent movement of individuals from one country to another. This severely drains the population of the host country.
  • Disease: The emergence of deadly illnesses and epidemics (such as HIV/AIDS) has historically caused sharp population declines.
  • Famine: A severe scarcity of food due to crop failure or conflict (e.g., the Somalia Famine of 2010-2012) actively pushes populations out or causes mass casualties.
  • War and Conflicts: Advanced modern warfare (including chemical and nuclear threats) forces people to flee combat zones, massively reducing a region's population (e.g., Syria).
Important

Migration Factors:

  • Push Factors (Negative): Lack of employment, oppressive politics, poor economy, disease, or war that force people to leave.
  • Pull Factors (Positive): Better employment, abundant land, political freedom, and economic stability that attract people to a new region.

Impact of Underpopulation

  • Unstable Economy: A shrinking consumer base can lead to deflation (a decrease in the overall price levels of goods and services).
  • Population Ageing: A declining birth rate increases the ratio of elderly people, placing intense social and economic pressure on the youth to support the economy.
  • Labour Shortage: Intensive labour sectors suffer greatly. However, a shortage of labour can sometimes increase demand for workers and temporarily reduce unemployment.
  • Environmental Relief: On a positive note, underpopulation significantly decreases pressure on natural resources and reduces pollution levels.

4. A Comparative Case Study: Canada and Tanzania

Both large and small countries can experience population imbalances. Let us compare an underpopulated developed nation with an overpopulated developing nation.

Canada (Underpopulated)

Canada is the second-largest country in the world by area, but vast stretches remain uninhabited. It is underpopulated because it possesses an advanced economy and tremendous natural resources (oil, gas, timber, wheat) that could easily support a much larger population with a high standard of living. It boasts state-funded healthcare, a 99% literacy rate, and is one of the world's top ten trading nations.

Tanzania (Overpopulated)

Tanzania is a relatively small country but possesses one of the fastest-growing populations in Africa. It is overpopulated because it severely lacks the natural and economic resources needed to provide basic needs for its people. Citizens struggle with access to safe drinking water, education, and medical care. High youth unemployment and heavy reliance on agriculture have resulted in widespread undernourishment and a low life expectancy.

Fact

Population Density & Economy: Population density is not directly linked to the level of economic development. For example, Japan has a high density of 335 persons per sq. km but is highly developed and not considered overpopulated. Conversely, Mali has a very low density (12 persons per sq. km) but is deeply impoverished and considered overpopulated relative to its available resources.
