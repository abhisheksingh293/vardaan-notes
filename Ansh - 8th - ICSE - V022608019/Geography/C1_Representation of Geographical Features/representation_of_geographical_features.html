Representation of Geographical Features

1. Topographical Maps (Toposheets)

A topographical map is a large-scale map that shows both natural and man-made features with the help of conventional signs, symbols, colours, and contour lines. These maps provide highly detailed information about a relatively small area.

Concept

Topography refers to the shape and features of the surface of the Earth. A topographical map is essentially a detailed two-dimensional representation of a three-dimensional land surface.

Fact

The Open Series Maps introduced by the Survey of India as per the National Map Policy of 2005 are drawn on a Representative Fraction (R.F.) of 1:50,000. They generally use a contour interval of 20 metres.

2. Contours and Relief Features

Relief features are expressed on topographical maps primarily through the use of contour lines.

Understanding Contours

  • Contour Lines: Imaginary lines drawn on a map joining places that have the exact same height above mean sea level. They are always drawn in brown colour.
  • Contour Interval (C.I.): The constant difference in height between two consecutive contour lines.
  • Vertical Interval (V.I.): The vertical difference in height between any two points or contour lines.
  • Horizontal Equivalent (H.E.): The actual horizontal distance between two points on two contour lines.
  • Gradient: This indicates the steepness of the slope. It is the ratio between the Vertical Interval and the Horizontal Equivalent (Gradient = V.I. / H.E.).
Important

Index Contours vs. Intermediate Contours: Thin brown lines are drawn at an interval of 20m (Intermediate Contours). After every four lines, a thick brown line is drawn to represent a 100m height difference. This thick line is known as the Index Contour.

Identifying Types of Relief Features

The spacing and alignment of contour lines help us identify the specific type of landform:

  • Steep Slope: Indicated by contour lines drawn very close together.
  • Gentle Slope: Indicated by contour lines drawn far apart.
  • Conical Hill: Represented by concentric circles with equally spaced contours, where heights increase towards the central innermost contour.
  • Plateau: A flat-topped highland with steep edges. The innermost contour is very wide, depicting a flat table-like top, while the outer edges have closely spaced contour lines.
  • Ridge: A long, narrow, sharp-edged upland with several peaks, resembling a range of hills.
  • Col: A shallow, low-lying narrow depression located between two hills.
  • Saddle: A shallow depression between two hills that is broader than a col.
  • Gap: A low depression close to the ground level.
  • Pass: A gap between two hills, but located at a higher elevation.

3. The National Grid Reference System

Since topographical maps provide detailed information of a small area, a network of intersecting red lines called the Grid Reference System is used to locate features accurately.

Eastings and Northings

  • Eastings: The vertical lines drawn on the map. Their value constantly increases as you move eastwards.
  • Northings: The horizontal lines drawn on the map. Their value constantly increases as you move northwards.
  • Grid Square: The intersecting network of eastings and northings forms squares that represent an area of 1 sq. km (2 cm x 2 cm on a 1:50,000 map).
Concept

Four-Figure vs. Six-Figure Grid Reference:
- Four-Figure: Used for locating features of larger dimensions (e.g., a forest, sand dunes, or a large village).
- Six-Figure: Used to state the precise and pinpoint location of a very specific feature (e.g., a temple, a specific lined well, or a church).

4. Settlement Patterns

Settlements are groups of human dwellings. On a toposheet, thick and dense settlements are generally found over fertile plains and wide river valleys, while steep slopes and marshes are avoided.

Types of Settlements on a Toposheet

  • Temporary Settlements: Represented as blank red squares. These are found in harsh climatic areas where activities like hunting, gathering, or shifting agriculture take place.
  • Permanent Settlements: Represented as solid red squares. These are permanent homes built in densely populated, established areas.

Common Settlement Patterns

  • Nucleated (Clustered) Settlements: Dwellings are clustered closely together, usually around a focal point like a well, temple, or road junction. It indicates a developed village with good facilities.
  • Dispersed (Scattered) Settlements: Dwellings are scattered over a large area, often near agricultural land. This pattern is associated with sparse population.
  • Linear (Ribbon) Settlements: Dwellings are strung out in a single line along a transport route, such as a road, canal, railway line, or along the foothills of a mountain.

5. Principles of Map Interpretation

Interpreting a topographical map involves bringing out the relationship between various natural and man-made features shown on the map.

  • Relief and Drainage: The flow direction of rivers is a clear indicator of the general slope of the land. The presence of waterfalls and rapids relates to steep slopes.
  • Climate Indicators: The presence of dry streams, dry tanks, or seasonal cart tracks indicates that the area receives seasonal rainfall (e.g., monsoon dependent).
  • Land Use and Occupations: The colours on the map directly indicate occupations. For example, large tracts of yellow wash (cultivated land) indicate agriculture as the primary occupation. Green areas (forests) indicate collection of forest products or forestry. Areas with mines or quarries indicate mining.
  • Transport and Communication: The network of metalled roads, unmetalled roads, and railway lines helps determine the connectivity and importance of various settlements in the map area.
