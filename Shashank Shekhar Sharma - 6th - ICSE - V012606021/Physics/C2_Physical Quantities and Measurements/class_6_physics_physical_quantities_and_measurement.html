PHYSICAL QUANTITIES AND MEASUREMENT

1. Measurement and its Importance

In our daily life, we make measurements constantly. For example, a tailor measures cloth length, a vegetable vendor measures mass, and a doctor measures body temperature. Our senses of touch or sight are often unreliable for exact measurement, which makes the scientific process of measurement essential.

Concept

Measurement is the comparison of an unknown quantity with a known fixed quantity of the same kind. The known fixed quantity is called a Unit.

Every measurement is expressed in two parts:

  1. The Unit: The standard used for measurement.
  2. The Number (Magnitude): Tells us how many times the unit is contained in the given quantity.

Example: If the length of a room is 5 metres, 'metre' is the unit and '5' is the magnitude.

2. Choice of Unit and Systems of Units

A good unit must be of a convenient size and universally accepted. Its value must remain the same at all places and times.

In the past, different systems were used in different countries:

  • C.G.S. system: Centimetre (Length), Gram (Mass), Second (Time).
  • F.P.S. system: Foot (Length), Pound (Mass), Second (Time).
  • M.K.S. system: Metre (Length), Kilogram (Mass), Second (Time).

Today, scientists worldwide use the Standard International Units (S.I. Units) to ensure uniformity.

Important

Rules for writing S.I. Units:
1. Symbols for units (not named after scientists) are written in small letters (e.g., m, kg, s).
2. Symbols for units named after scientists are written with a capital letter (e.g., K for Kelvin, N for Newton).
3. The symbol is never written in plural (e.g., 10 cm, NOT 10 cms).

3. Basic Physical Quantities

Quantities that can be measured are called physical quantities. The basic fundamental quantities are Length, Mass, Time, and Temperature.

A. Measurement of Length

Length is the distance between two points. Words like width, breadth, depth, thickness, and height are all basically measurements of length.

  • S.I. Unit: Metre (m).
  • Multiples: Kilometre (km) is used for long distances. 1 km = 1000 m.
  • Submultiples: Centimetre (cm) and Millimetre (mm) are used for shorter lengths. 1 m = 100 cm = 1000 mm.

Devices used: A metre ruler and a measuring tape. When using a ruler, keeping your eye exactly vertically above the mark is crucial to avoid a parallax error (incorrect reading due to angled viewing).

B. Measurement of Mass

Mass is the quantity of matter contained in a body. Unlike weight, mass does not change with location.

  • S.I. Unit: Kilogram (kg).
  • Multiples: Quintal (1 quintal = 100 kg) and Metric tonne (1 tonne = 1000 kg).
  • Submultiples: Gram (g) and Milligram (mg). 1 kg = 1000 g and 1 g = 1000 mg.

Devices used: A beam balance (commonly used by grocers and in physical labs) and an electronic balance (used for highly precise measurements).

C. Measurement of Time

Time is the interval between two events. We measure time using the rotation and revolution of the earth.

  • S.I. Unit: Second (s).
  • Multiples: Minute (min), Hour (h), Day, and Year.

A mean solar day is the time taken by the earth to complete one rotation on its axis (24 hours). A year is the time taken by the earth to complete one revolution around the sun (365 days).

Devices used: Pendulum clock, watches, and stopwatches (used for accurately measuring short time intervals like sports races).

D. Measurement of Temperature

Temperature is the measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of an object. Heat flows from an object at a higher temperature to one at a lower temperature.

  • S.I. Unit: Kelvin (K).
  • Common Units: Degree Celsius (°C) and Degree Fahrenheit (°F).
Fact

The normal temperature of a healthy human body is exactly 37°C or 98.6°F. If the body temperature rises above this, the person is suffering from a fever.

Devices used:

  • Laboratory Thermometer: Marked generally from -10°C to 110°C. Used for measuring the temperature of liquids like boiling water.
  • Clinical Thermometer: Designed specifically to measure human body temperature. It has markings from 35°C to 42°C (or 95°F to 110°F). It features a slight bend or constriction (kink) in the capillary tube, which prevents mercury from falling back down automatically, allowing the doctor to read the temperature accurately.

4. Measurement of Area

Area is the total surface occupied by an object. It is a derived quantity, meaning it is calculated by multiplying two fundamental lengths.

  • S.I. Unit: Square metre (m²).
  • Bigger Units: Are (100 m²), Hectare (10,000 m²), and Square Kilometre (1,000,000 m²).

Area of Regular Shapes

  • Square: Area = side × side
  • Rectangle: Area = length × breadth
  • Triangle: Area = ½ × base × height

Measuring Area of Irregular Shapes

The area of an irregular surface (like a leaf) can be measured using a graph paper. The outline of the object is drawn on graph paper with 1 cm squares. The area is estimated by adding the number of completely filled squares to the number of half (or more than half) filled squares.
