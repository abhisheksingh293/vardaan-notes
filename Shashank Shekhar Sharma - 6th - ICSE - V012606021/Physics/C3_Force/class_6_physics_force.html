FORCE

1. Force as a Push or Pull

In our everyday life, we often use words like push, pull, stretch, and squeeze to describe actions. Pushing or pulling tends to move a body, while stretching and squeezing tend to change the shape or size of a body.

  • Push: To open a door we push it, to move a stationary car we push it, and a bulldozer pushes rubble to move it.
  • Pull: To move a grass roller, a gardener pulls it. A rickshaw puller pulls a rickshaw to move it.
  • Stretch/Squeeze: Stretching a rubber string increases its length. Squeezing an inflated football changes its shape.
Concept

Force is a physical cause (a push or pull) which changes or tends to change the state of rest, the state of motion, or the size and shape of a body.

2. Effects of a Force

When a force is applied to a body, it can have multiple effects:

  • A force can move a body originally at rest (e.g., kicking a stationary football).
  • A force can stop a moving body (e.g., catching a moving cricket ball or applying brakes on a bicycle).
  • A force can make a moving body move faster (e.g., pedalling a bicycle harder).
  • A force can slow down a moving body.
  • A force can change the direction of motion of a moving body (e.g., hitting a moving tennis ball back to the opponent).
  • A force can change the shape or size of a body (e.g., squeezing a tube of toothpaste).
Fact

A force does not change the mass of the body it is applied to. The S.I. unit of force is the Newton (N). Two forces acting in the same direction add up, while forces in opposite directions subtract.

3. Kinds of Forces

Forces are broadly classified into two categories based on their application:

A. Contact Forces

The forces which act on bodies by making actual physical contact are called contact forces.

  • Muscular force: The force applied by our muscles (e.g., lifting a bag).
  • Force of friction: The force exerted by a surface on a body that opposes its motion.
  • Tension: The force in a string when a load is suspended from it.

B. Non-Contact Forces (Forces at a Distance)

The forces which act on bodies without any physical contact between them are called non-contact forces.

  • Gravitational force: The force of attraction between two masses, such as the Earth attracting objects towards itself (this is what gives objects weight).
  • Electrostatic force: The force of attraction or repulsion between charged bodies (e.g., a plastic comb attracting bits of paper after being rubbed on dry hair).
  • Magnetic force: The force of attraction or repulsion between magnetic poles.

4. Force of Friction

If we stop pedalling a moving bicycle, it gradually slows down and eventually stops. This happens because a force acts in the opposite direction of the bicycle's motion.

Friction is the force that opposes the relative motion between two surfaces in contact with each other.

Effects of Friction

  • Opposes motion: Friction always makes it harder to slide or roll an object.
  • Acts in opposite direction: Friction acts exactly opposite to the direction the object is moving or trying to move.
  • Produces heat: Rubbing your palms together makes them warm due to friction.
  • Causes wear and tear: The moving parts of machines and the tyres of vehicles gradually wear out due to continuous friction.

Factors Affecting Friction

  • Smoothness of surfaces: Friction is more on rough surfaces and less on smooth surfaces.
  • Nature of medium: Solids offer the most friction, liquids offer less, and gases offer the least.
  • Weight of the moving body: Greater weight increases the grip between surfaces, leading to higher friction.

5. Types of Friction

Friction is broadly divided into three types based on the state of motion:

  1. Static Friction: The opposing force between an object and a surface when the object is pushed but does not move. It is a self-adjusting force.
  2. Sliding Friction: The opposing force when an object actually slides over a surface. It is always less than static friction.
  3. Rolling Friction: The opposing force when an object rolls over a surface (like a wheel). Rolling friction is significantly less than sliding friction.
Important

Because rolling friction is much less than sliding friction, heavy luggage bags are fitted with small wheels (rollers), and machines use ball bearings to make movement easier.

6. Controlling Friction

Friction can be both a necessity and a hindrance. Depending on the situation, we either need to reduce it or increase it.

A. Ways to Reduce Friction (When it is a disadvantage)

  • Polishing/Smoothing: Smoothing rough surfaces reduces the grip between them.
  • Using Lubricants: Applying oil, grease, or fine powder (like on a carrom board) fills the tiny pores in surfaces, reducing friction.
  • Using Ball Bearings: Converting sliding motion into rolling motion using steel balls (e.g., in bicycle axles).
  • Streamlining: Giving a special pointed or sharp shape to objects (like airplanes, boats, and fish) to reduce friction offered by air or water.

B. Ways to Increase Friction (When it is advantageous)

Friction is highly useful; without it, we could not walk, write, or drive properly. We increase it by:

  • Making surfaces rough: Grooving tyres and adding spikes to athletes' shoes increases grip.
  • Keeping surfaces dry: Wet surfaces have less friction (causing slipping), so drying them increases safety.
