THE FLOWER

1. Introduction

A flower is usually the most beautiful and colourful part of a plant. It serves as the reproductive organ of the plant. A flower is attached to the shoot by means of a stalk or pedicel. The tip of the stalk is enlarged and slightly flattened, called the thalamus, from which the petals and other floral parts arise.

2. Parts of a Flower (The Four Whorls)

A typical fully opened flower consists of four concentric rings or whorls:

  1. Calyx (First Whorl): The outermost whorl made up of tiny green leaf-like structures called sepals. They protect the growing bud.
  2. Corolla (Second Whorl): Made up of large, fragrant, and brightly coloured structures called petals. They are never green and their main function is to attract insects for pollination.
  3. Androecium (Third Whorl): The male reproductive part of the flower, consisting of stamens.
    • Each stamen has a thin stalk called a filament and a swollen tip called an anther.
    • The anther produces yellow, powdery pollen grains which contain the male gametes.
  4. Gynoecium / Pistil (Fourth Whorl): The innermost whorl and the female reproductive part. It is made of the carpel/pistil, which has three parts:
    • Ovary: The swollen base containing ovules (which hold female gametes).
    • Style: A tube-like structure arising from the ovary.
    • Stigma: The expanded tip that receives pollen grains.
Concept

Classification based on Reproductive Parts:

  • Complete / Bisexual Flower: A flower containing all four whorls (both male androecium and female gynoecium are present).
  • Incomplete / Unisexual Flower: A flower in which either the male or female reproductive part is missing.

3. Pollination

The main function of a flower is to produce seeds and fruits. The first step towards this is pollination. Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anthers to the stigma of a flower.

  • Self Pollination: Occurs within a single flower or between two flowers on the same plant.
  • Cross Pollination: Occurs between flowers on different plants of the same kind.
Fact

Agents of Cross Pollination:

  • Insects: Butterflies and bees visit flowers for nectar. Pollen sticks to their bodies. Insect-pollinated flowers are large, bright, fragrant, and produce nectar (e.g., Mustard, Marigold, Dahlia).
  • Wind: Flowers produce dry pollen in large quantities which are blown away to land on feathery stigmas (e.g., Maize, Pine, Palm).
  • Water: Pollen is transferred via water (e.g., Vallisneria). Note: Lotus and Trapa are water plants but are pollinated by insects because their flowers are exposed to air.
  • Animals: Birds (like hummingbirds) and bats can also act as pollinating agents.

4. Fertilization and Formation of Fruit

After a pollen grain lands on the stigma, it produces a pollen tube that grows down through the style into the ovary. It carries the male gamete, which fuses with the female gamete inside the ovule. This process is called fertilization.

Fate of Floral Parts after Fertilization:

  • The ovary swells and develops into a fruit.
  • The ovules containing the fertilized cells develop into seeds.
  • The sepals, petals, and stamens usually dry up and fall off.
Important

True vs. False Fruits: Usually, a fruit is the ripened ovary. However, in fruits like apples and pears, the thalamus becomes the main fleshy, edible part while the ovary remains a small central core containing seeds. These are called False Fruits.

5. Structure of a Fruit

A typical fruit contains two main parts: the pericarp (fruit wall) and the seeds.

The Pericarp develops from the ovary wall and has three layers in fleshy fruits:

  • Epicarp: The outer, thin protective covering (skin).
  • Mesocarp: The sweet, fleshy, edible middle layer.
  • Endocarp: The inner hard part that encloses the seeds.

Fleshy vs. Dry Fruits:

  • Fleshy Fruits: The pericarp is soft and fleshy (e.g., tomato, grape, papaya). In mangoes, the epicarp and mesocarp are pulpy, but the endocarp is hard.
  • Dry Fruits: The pericarp is dry and not pulpy. It encloses the seed (e.g., pea pod, walnut).

6. The Seed

A seed is a fertilized and ripened ovule. Based on the number of seed leaves (cotyledons), seeds are classified as:

  1. Dicotyledonous (Dicot) Seeds: Have two cotyledons (e.g., bean, gram, pea).
  2. Monocotyledonous (Monocot) Seeds: Have only one cotyledon (e.g., maize, wheat).

Structure of a Bean Seed (Dicot)

  • Seed Coat: Protects the seed. Made of outer testa and inner tegmen.
  • Hilum: A scar where the seed was attached to the fruit wall.
  • Micropyle: A tiny pore above the hilum that absorbs water for germination.
  • Cotyledons: Two fleshy seed leaves containing stored food.
  • Embryo: The baby plant inside, consisting of a radicle (develops into the root) and a plumule (develops into the shoot).

Structure of a Maize Grain (Monocot)

  • The seed coat is fused with the pericarp (fruit wall), which is why it is called a "grain".
  • Endosperm: The large upper part that stores food as starch.
  • Aleurone Layer: A protein-rich outermost layer of the endosperm.
  • Cotyledon: Located in the lower portion, containing the small embryo (radicle and plumule).

7. Seed Germination

The process by which the embryo within the seed becomes active and grows into a young plant (seedling) is called germination.

Types of Germination

  • Epigeal Germination: The cotyledons are pushed above the surface of the soil (e.g., bean, tamarind, cucumber).
  • Hypogeal Germination: The cotyledons remain below the ground in the soil (e.g., pea, maize).
Concept

Conditions Necessary for Germination:

  1. Water (Moisture): Needed for chemical reactions and to activate enzymes.
  2. Air (Oxygen): Needed by the seed to respire, oxidise stored food, and release energy for growth.
  3. Suitable Temperature (Warmth): Enzymes act best at favourable temperatures (usually between $35^\circ C$ and $40^\circ C$). They are inactive at very low temperatures and destroyed at very high temperatures.
