THE LEAF

1. Introduction to Plant Parts

A variety of small and large flowering plants surround us, such as tulsi, rose, mango, neem, and peepal. Despite variations in height, shape of leaves, and colour of flowers, all flowering plants share distinct structural similarities: they all have roots, stems, leaves, and they bear flowers.

A. The Root System

The underground part of a plant is called the root. It grows downward into the soil, away from sunlight, and towards the force of gravity. The primary root with all its branches and root hairs forms the root system.

Concept

Types of Root Systems:

  • Tap Root System: Has a thick main root called the primary root, bearing many side branches (secondary roots). Found in dicot plants like gram and pea.
  • Fibrous Root System: A cluster of roots of the same thickness and size arising directly from the base of the stem. Found in monocot plants like maize and grass.

Functions of the Root System:

  • Fixes the plant firmly in the soil.
  • Absorbs water and minerals from the soil for the growth of the entire plant.
  • Binds the soil together to prevent it from getting washed away by rain or wind.

B. The Shoot System

The part of a plant that grows above the soil forms the shoot system. It is made up of the stem, buds, leaves, flowers, and fruits.

The Stem: The main aerial part of the shoot system.

  • Nodes: Points on the stem where leaves and branches originate.
  • Internode: The part of the stem between two successive nodes.
  • Apical/Terminal Buds: Located at the tip of the shoot; responsible for vertical growth.
  • Axillary Buds: Located in the axil (angle between the upper side of a leaf and the stem). They produce new branches with leaves or flowers.

2. Structure of a Leaf

Leaves are the flat, green parts of the shoot that grow laterally from the nodes of the stem. Unlike the stem, leaves stop growing after attaining their full size.

Fact

Parts of a typical leaf:

  • Petiole: The basal stalk of a leaf attached to the stem. Leaves directly attached without a petiole are called sessile leaves.
  • Lamina (Leaf Blade): The flat, green, and broad part of the leaf. Its outer edge is the leaf margin.
  • Midrib & Veins: The petiole continues into the lamina as the midrib, which branches into veins and veinlets to conduct water and food, and provide structural support.

3. Kinds and Classification of Leaves

Types of Leaves

  • Simple Leaf: The lamina is undivided and is a single piece (e.g., mango, banana, peepal).
  • Compound Leaf: The leaf blade or lamina is divided into smaller units called leaflets (e.g., rose).

Arrangement of Leaves (Phyllotaxy)

  • Alternate: Only one leaf arises from each node in an alternate direction (e.g., mint, peepal).
  • Opposite: Two leaves arise on each node opposite to each other (e.g., jasmine, guava).
  • Whorled: More than two leaves are attached at each node, arranged in a whorl (e.g., oleander).
Concept

Venation (Arrangement of Veins):

  • Reticulate Venation: Veins and veinlets are irregularly distributed, forming a network (e.g., peepal, mango). Common in dicots.
  • Parallel Venation: Veins run parallel to each other (e.g., banana, grass, wheat). Common in monocots.

4. Main Functions of the Leaf

Leaves are the food factories of the plant. Their two main functions are photosynthesis and transpiration.

A. Photosynthesis

The process by which a plant leaf prepares food from water and carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) in the presence of chlorophyll and sunlight. The reaction can be summarized as:

$Water + Carbon \ Dioxide \xrightarrow{Sunlight \ \& \ Chlorophyll} Glucose + Oxygen$

  • Food: Plants prepare glucose not only for themselves but also for animals and humans.
  • Oxygen: Leaves give out oxygen ($O_2$) as a by-product, which supports life on earth.

B. Transpiration

The process by which water is lost in the form of water vapour by evaporation from the surface of leaves and other aerial parts.

  • Cooling Effect: Evaporation cools the plant and surrounding air during hot summers.
  • Transpirational Pull: Creates a suction force that pulls more water and essential mineral salts up from the roots.

5. Modifications of Leaf

Sometimes, a complete leaf or part of it is modified to perform special functions:

Important
  • Leaf Tendrils: In weak-stemmed plants, leaves/leaflets are modified into wiry, coiled structures that are sensitive to touch, helping the plant climb (e.g., Sweet pea).
  • Spines: Leaves are modified into spines to reduce water loss (e.g., Cactus) or provide defense (e.g., Prickly poppy).
  • Scale Leaves: Thick/fleshy or thin/dry leaves designed to store food and protect buds (e.g., Onion, ginger).

6. Insectivorous Plants

Plants that trap insects to meet their nitrogen demands (because they grow in nitrate-deficient soils) are called insectivorous plants.

  • Pitcher Plant: The lamina is modified into a pitcher, and the leaf apex forms a lid. It secretes digestive juices to consume trapped insects (Found in Garo and Khasi Hills, Meghalaya).
  • Venus Flytrap: Leaves have long pointed hairs and are hinged in the middle. They snap shut when an insect lands on them.
  • Bladderwort: Highly segmented leaves that form small bladder-like traps for insects.

7. Vegetative Propagation in Leaves

While most plants propagate through seeds, some reproduce using vegetative parts (roots, stems, or leaves). Plant structures that reproduce vegetatively must have stored food and vegetative buds.

  • Leaves of plants like Bryophyllum and Begonia produce adventitious buds along their margins.
  • When these buds fall into moist soil, they grow roots and develop into young, tiny plants (plantlets).
