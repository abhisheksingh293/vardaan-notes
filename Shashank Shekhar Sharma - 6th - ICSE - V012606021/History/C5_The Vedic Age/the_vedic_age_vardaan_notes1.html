The Vedic Age

1. Introduction to the Vedic Age

The Vedic Age refers to the period in ancient Indian history when the Vedas and other Vedic Sanskrit texts were composed. This civilisation flourished between 1500 BCE and 600 BCE on the Indo-Gangetic plains of the Indian subcontinent.

Important Chronological Division: The era is divided into two distinct periods based on significant changes in society, religion, and politics:
  • Early Vedic Age (Rig Vedic Age): 1500 BCE – 1000 BCE
  • Later Vedic Age (Epic Age): 1000 BCE – 600 BCE

The Aryans and their Migration

Originally, the Aryans lived in the Steppes stretching from southern Russia to Central Asia. They migrated to north-west India and were referred to as Indo-Aryans. Upon arrival, they faced resistance from local inhabitants whom they called Dasas or Dasyus.

  • In the Early Vedic Age, they settled in a region called Sapta Sindhu (land of seven rivers), naming it Brahmavarta (land of the Gods).
  • In the Later Vedic Age, they moved into the Indo-Gangetic plains, which they named Aryavarta (Land of Aryans).

2. Sources of Information

The chief source of information about this era is the Vedas. The word Veda is derived from the Sanskrit word 'vid', which means wisdom or knowledge. The texts were composed in classical or Vedic Sanskrit and passed down orally for generations before being written.

Concept The Shruti Texts: The four Vedas are known as Shruti, meaning "that which was heard directly by the sages."

The Four Vedas

  • The Rig Veda: The oldest Veda. It contains 1,028 hymns (suktas) praising nature gods like Indra, Agni, and Varuna. It is divided into 10 mandalas (books) and contains the famous Gayatri Mantra.
  • The Sama Veda: The book of chants. It contains melodies sung by priests during soma ceremonies.
  • The Yajur Veda: The guidebook of rituals. It provides instructions for executing sacrificial acts.
  • The Atharva Veda: The book of spells and charms. It contains hymns written to ward off evil spirits and demons.

Other Literary and Archaeological Sources

Other vital texts belonging to the Vedic literature include the Brahmanas (prose explanations of mantras), the Upanishads and Aranyakas (philosophical discussions, also known as Vedanta), the Puranas, and the great epics (Mahabharata and Ramayana).

Archaeological evidence comes from excavations at sites like Hastinapur and Atranjikhera. A special type of pottery, the Painted Grey Ware (PGW), is closely associated with this period.

3. Life in the Early Vedic Age (1500 - 1000 BCE)

Political Life

The people lived in tribes called Jana. Each tribe chose a king called Rajan based on his bravery and strength; this position was not hereditary. The King ruled according to the wishes of his tribe and was assisted by the purohita (chief priest), senani (army head), and gramani (village headman).

There was no regular tax. Instead, people offered voluntary contributions called Bali. The King was advised by assemblies:

  • Sabha: A small, selected council of elders.
  • Samiti: A large assembly of all the people of the tribe.
  • Vidhatha: An assembly involving all people, including women.

Social and Economic Life

The primary unit of society was the joint family (kula), headed by the eldest male, known as the grihapati (patriarchal system). Society was divided into four varnas (Brahmans, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, Shudras) based entirely on occupation. It was highly flexible; inter-caste marriages were common.

Fact Status of Women: Women held a highly honorable place in the Early Vedic society. They received education (scholars like Gargi and Maitreyi), participated in the Samiti assemblies, and chose their husbands via swayamvara. Evils like purdah and sati did not exist.

Economically, the early Aryans were pastoralists and cattle farmers. Cows were deeply respected (called Aghnya) and served as a measure of a family's wealth. Agriculture was a secondary occupation.

Religion

They worshipped forces of nature such as Indra (God of rain/thunder), Surya (Sun God), Varuna (Sky God), and Agni (Fire God). There were no temples or idols; they performed open-air sacrifices (yajnas) accompanied by hymns.

4. Life in the Later Vedic Age (1000 - 600 BCE)

Political Changes

In this era, small tribes merged to form large, powerful kingdoms. Kingship became strictly hereditary. The Sabha and Samiti lost their significance, and the king assumed immense power, taking titles like Samrat (king of kings). Complex yajnas were performed to cement royal authority:

  • Ashvamedha Yajna (Horse Sacrifice): Used to prove imperial sovereignty and claim dominion over neighboring lands.
  • Rajasuya Yajna: Performed to grant supreme, divine power to the king.

Voluntary Bali was replaced by forced, regular taxes.

Social Evolution and the Ashrama System

The flexible Varna system transformed into a rigid, birth-based caste system. The position of women drastically declined; they lost inheritance rights, were barred from assemblies, and were confined to domestic duties.

Concept The Four Ashramas: The life of an upper-caste Aryan was divided into four stages to ensure discipline and spiritual growth:
  • Brahmacharya: Student stage, attaining knowledge in the Gurukul.
  • Grihastha: Leading a married, responsible family life.
  • Vanaprastha: Giving up family life to meditate in the forest.
  • Sanyasa: Complete renunciation of worldly pleasures.

Gurukul Education System

Education was imparted orally by a guru in a secluded Gurukul. It was restricted to the upper three varnas. Students lived disciplined lives, served the guru, and studied subjects ranging from the Vedas and ethics to astronomy and mathematics. Instead of fees, students offered guru dakshina upon completion.

Economic and Religious Shifts

Agriculture became the primary economic activity. Crucially, the Later Vedic people discovered and utilized iron (referred to as shyam ayas), leading to better ploughs and tools. Trade flourished, and occupational guilds emerged.

Religiously, the early nature gods were replaced by the Trinity: Brahma (the creator), Vishnu (the preserver), and Shiva (the destroyer). Sacrifices and complex rituals performed by Brahmans became central to religious life.
