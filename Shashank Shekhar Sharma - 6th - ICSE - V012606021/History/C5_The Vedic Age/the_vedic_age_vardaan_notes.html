The Vedic Age

1. Introduction

The Vedic Age refers to the foundational period in Indian history when the Vedas and other ancient scriptures were composed. It spanned a significant timeframe and laid the foundations for the emergence of Hinduism, encompassing profound social, political, and cultural shifts.

  • Time Period: The Vedic Age flourished between 1500 BCE and 600 BCE.
  • Geography: The civilization expanded across the Indo-Gangetic plains of the Indian subcontinent.

2. Sources of Information

Literary Sources

The primary source of information for this period derives from texts composed in Vedic Sanskrit. Collectively, these texts form the Vedic literature, which was passed down orally for generations before being written down.

Concept

The Word "Veda": Derived from the Sanskrit root word 'vid', which means "knowledge". The Vedas are considered Shrutis (meaning "that which is heard"), believed to have been imparted directly by the Gods to sages.

The four primary Vedas are:

  • Rig Veda: The oldest text containing 1028 hymns (suktas) composed in praise of deities like Indra, Agni, and Varuna. It includes the famous Gayatri Mantra.
  • Sama Veda: Known as the book of chants and melodies, containing instructions on how hymns should be sung.
  • Yajur Veda: The book of sacrificial formulas and guidelines for rituals.
  • Atharva Veda: Also called the wisdom of the brahmins, it includes spells and charms to ward off evil spirits.

Other essential texts include the Brahmanas (prose explanations of the Vedas), Upanishads (philosophical teachings, also known as Vedanta), the Puranas, and the two great epics: the Mahabharata and the Ramayana.

Archaeological Sources

Excavations at sites like Hastinapur and Atranjikhera in Uttar Pradesh provide material evidence. The distinct Painted Grey Ware (PGW) pottery is heavily associated with Vedic people.

3. The Aryans

The people of the Vedic Age are widely known as the Aryans.

  • Origin: Originally semi-nomadic pastoralists from the Steppes (stretching from southern Russia to Central Asia).
  • Settlement: A group migrated to north-west India (Indo-Aryans). They initially settled in a region called Sapta Sindhu (Land of Seven Rivers), which they proudly named Brahmavarta (Land of the Gods).
  • They faced resistance from local inhabitants whom they termed Dasas or Dasyus.

4. The Early Vedic Age (1500 BCE - 1000 BCE)

Also known as the Rig Vedic Age because our understanding is derived predominantly from the Rig Veda.

Political Life

Society was organized into tribes called Janas. The tribe was ruled by a king called the Rajan, whose primary duty was to protect the tribe during wars. Crucially, kingship was not hereditary at this stage.

The King was assisted by:

  • Purohita: The chief priest who advised on religious matters.
  • Senani: The commander of the army.
  • Gramani: The village headman.
Fact

Democratic Assemblies: The King's power was checked by tribal councils. The Sabha was a small, select group of elders (like an advisory council). The Samiti was a larger assembly consisting of all the people of the tribe, where anyone could speak.

Social and Family Life

The fundamental unit of society was the family (kula). Multiple families formed a village (grama), and several villages made a clan (vish).

  • Patriarchal System: Families lived jointly, ruled by the eldest male member called the Grihapati.
  • Position of Women: Women held a highly honorable place. They received education, attended assemblies, and chose their husbands (Swayamvara). Great women scholars of this era included Gargi, Maitreyi, and Lopamudra. Evils like child marriage and Sati were completely absent.
  • Social Division: Society was divided into four classes: Brahmans (priests), Kshatriyas (warriors), Vaishyas (merchants/farmers), and Shudras (laborers). This division was flexible and based purely on occupation, not birth.

Economic and Religious Life

Cattle-rearing and agriculture were primary occupations. The cow was considered a measure of wealth and prestige. The barter system facilitated trade.

Religiously, they worshipped the forces of nature (Sun, Rain, Thunder). There were no temples or idols. Indra (God of rain/storm) was the most prominent deity, alongside Agni (Fire) and Varuna (Sky).

5. The Later Vedic Age (1000 BCE - 600 BCE)

As the Aryans migrated further into the fertile Gangetic plains, they called their new home Aryavarta. This period is also called the Epic Age.

Political Life Transitions

Kingdoms grew larger and more powerful. The power of the King increased exponentially, and kingship became strictly hereditary. The assemblies (Sabha and Samiti) lost their significance.

Important

Rise of Sacrifices: To prove their supreme power, kings performed elaborate rituals. The Ashvamedha Yajna (Horse Sacrifice) expanded their territory, while the Rajasuya Yajna granted them supreme earthly power.

Social Rigidities and Ashramas

The social structure underwent drastic, restrictive changes:

  • Position of Women Declined: Women were restricted to domestic duties, barred from inheriting property, and forbidden from attending assemblies or studying Vedas.
  • Rigid Caste System: The four varnas became entirely hereditary, based strictly on birth. Inter-caste mobility ceased.
  • The Four Ashramas: An Aryan man's life was strictly divided into four stages:
    1. Brahmacharya: Student life in Gurukuls attaining knowledge.
    2. Grihastha: Householder phase (marriage and family).
    3. Vanaprastha: Retirement to the forest for meditation.
    4. Sanyasa: Complete renunciation of worldly ties.

Gurukul Education System

Education was imparted orally in secluded ashramas called Gurukuls. Students learned Vedas, mathematics, grammar, and medicine while serving their Guru. Upon completion, they offered voluntary tribute known as Guru Dakshina.

Economic and Religious Shifts

Agriculture replaced cattle rearing as the primary occupation, drastically improved by the discovery and use of Iron tools and ploughs. Elaborate guilds were formed for trade.

Nature gods lost their prominence. A new trinity emerged: Brahma (The Creator), Vishnu (The Preserver), and Shiva (The Destroyer). Rituals and sacrifices became complex, highly expensive, and strictly controlled by the priestly class, leading to a strong belief in the cycle of Karma (action) and the pursuit of Moksha (salvation).
