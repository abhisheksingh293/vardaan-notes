Mahavira and Buddha

1. Social Conditions Before Mahavira and Buddha

In the 6th century BCE, the Later Vedic society experienced profound religious and social unrest. The simple religion of the Early Vedic period had evolved into a complex, rigid system that caused dissatisfaction among the masses.

  • Elaborate Rituals: Religious practices and ceremonies had become highly complex, expensive, and out of reach for ordinary people.
  • Dominance of Brahmans: Priests established themselves as the supreme authority, insisting that expensive sacrifices (Yajnas) were essential for attaining salvation. They controlled religious knowledge by using Sanskrit, a language the common masses did not understand.
  • Rigid Caste System: The Varna system became strictly hereditary and oppressive. The lowest caste, the Shudras (regarded as untouchables), faced severe discrimination. They were banned from temples, reciting mantras, or drawing water from public wells.
  • Growth of Trade: As trade flourished, the Vaishyas (merchant class) gained economic power but were denied higher social status due to the rigid caste rules. Consequently, they heavily supported new, more egalitarian movements.

2. The Rise of New Ideas

The philosophical teachings of the Upanishads were too deep and difficult for the common person to comprehend. The masses yearned for a simple, direct, and accessible path to salvation. This pressing spiritual need led to the emergence of two major reform movements: Jainism and Buddhism.

Concept

Reform Movements: Both Jainism and Buddhism began as reform movements to rid Hinduism of its complex evils (like the caste system and animal sacrifices). Over time, they grew into distinct, independent religions with their own global followings.

3. Vardhamana Mahavira and Jainism

Vardhamana Mahavira was the 24th and last Tirthankara (enlightened teacher/spiritual guide) of Jainism. The first Tirthankara was Rishabhadeva, and the 23rd was Parshvanatha.

Early Life and Renunciation

  • Birth: Born in 599 BCE in Kundagrama (near Vaishali, Bihar).
  • Family: His father, Siddhartha, was a Kshatriya chief of the Jnatrika clan. His mother, Trishala, was a Lichchhavi princess.
  • Renunciation: At the age of 30, Mahavira left his royal comforts, gave up all worldly possessions, and became an ascetic in search of truth.
  • Enlightenment: After 12 years of intense penance and deep meditation, he attained Kevala Jnana (Supreme Knowledge). He became known as Mahavira (the Great Hero) and Jina (Conqueror of the self). His followers came to be known as Jains.

Core Teachings of Mahavira

Mahavira rejected the authority of the Vedas and the necessity of rituals. He preached completely in Prakrit, the language of the common people, which aided in the vast spread of his message.

Fact

The Five Pillars (Vows) of Jainism:
1. Ahimsa: Extreme non-violence; not causing harm to any living being (even insects and plants).
2. Satya: Always speaking the harmless truth.
3. Brahmacharya: Leading a life of strict celibacy.
4. Asteya: Non-stealing and avoiding dishonesty.
5. Tyaga (Aparigraha): Non-possession; renouncing property and attachments.

To break free from the continuous cycle of birth and rebirth and achieve Moksha (salvation), Mahavira instructed his followers to practice the Triratnas (Three Jewels):

  1. Right Belief
  2. Right Knowledge
  3. Right Conduct

Sects and Texts in Jainism

After Mahavira's passing in Pawapuri (BCE 527), Jainism eventually split into two main sects based on ascetic practices:

  • Svetambaras: Monks who wear white clothes.
  • Digambaras: Monks who remain naked (sky-clad), strictly following Mahavira's original teachings of owning absolutely nothing.

The religious texts of Jainism were written in Prakrit. Important texts include the 12 Angas and the Purvas.

4. Gautama Buddha and Buddhism

Buddhism was founded by Siddhartha Gautama. Like Jainism, it offered a simpler, philosophical way of life aimed at ending human suffering.

Early Life and Enlightenment

  • Birth: Born in 563 BCE in Lumbini (near Kapilavastu, Nepal).
  • Family: His father, Shuddhodana, was the king of the Shakya clan. His mother was Queen Maya.
  • The Four Sights: Despite being protected from worldly sorrows in his palace, Siddhartha witnessed four sights that changed him forever: an old man, a sick man, a dead body, and a peaceful ascetic.
  • The Great Renunciation (Mahabhinishkramana): Deeply troubled by human suffering, he left his home, wife (Yashodhara), and son (Rahul) at the age of 29 to seek the truth.
  • Enlightenment: After years of wandering and meditating under a Pipal tree (Bodhi tree) in Bodh Gaya, he attained enlightenment. He was henceforth called the Buddha (The Enlightened One).

Core Teachings of Buddha

Buddha delivered his first sermon at Sarnath, an event known as Dharmachakra Pravartana (Turning the Wheel of Law). He preached in Pali, avoiding extreme luxury as well as extreme self-torture, advocating instead for the Middle Path.

Important

The Four Noble Truths:
1. The world is full of suffering.
2. The main cause of suffering is human desire.
3. Suffering can be ended by overcoming these desires.
4. Desires can be overcome by following the Eightfold Path.

The Eightfold Path consists of Right Action, Right Thought, Right Belief, Right Living, Right Speech, Right Effort, Right Recollection, and Right Meditation.

Sects, Texts, and Spread

Buddhism spread rapidly across Asia due to royal patronage from kings like Ashoka, Kanishka, and Harsha. The organized monastic system (the Sangha) also played a massive role.

  • Sects: Buddhism eventually split into Hinayana (orthodox, worshipped Buddha as a teacher) and Mahayana (worshipped Buddha as a God with idols).
  • Texts: Written primarily in Pali, the most sacred texts are the Tripitakas (Vinaya, Sutta, and Abhidhamma Pitaka) and the Jataka Tales (stories of Buddha's previous lives).

5. Comparison: Jainism and Buddhism

Similarities

  • Both founders were Kshatriya princes who renounced the world.
  • Both opposed the rigid caste system and promoted social equality.
  • Both rejected the authority of the Vedas, animal sacrifices, and complex rituals.
  • Both emphasized Ahimsa (non-violence) and moral living.
  • Both believed in the law of Karma and the cycle of rebirth.

Differences

  • Severity: Jainism advocated severe penance, extreme fasting, and strict non-violence (even avoiding farming lest insects die). Buddhism followed a moderate Middle Path.
  • Spread: Jainism largely remained confined to India. Buddhism spread extensively outside India, becoming a major world religion in countries like China, Japan, and Sri Lanka.
  • Concept of God: Buddhism remained mostly silent on the existence of God, focusing on self-effort. Jainism recognized the existence of Gods but placed them lower than the Tirthankaras.
