Transportation in Plants and Animals

1. Introduction to Transportation

Organisms require food, water, and oxygen for their survival. The process of moving these essential substances from one location to another within an organism is known as transportation. Simultaneously, the wastes produced by the body are collected and removed.

  • Unicellular Organisms: Simple processes like diffusion and osmosis handle the movement of materials.
  • Multicellular Organisms: Due to their complexity, different interconnected body parts perform specific functions to distribute required substances and eliminate waste rapidly.

2. Transportation in Plants

In plants, leaves synthesize food, while roots extract water and minerals from the soil. They rely on specialized tissues for the distribution of these life-sustaining elements.

Concept Conducting Tissues (Vascular Bundles)
Plants contain a network of tube-like structures that function as pipelines:
  • Xylem: Transports water and minerals absorbed by the roots upwards to the leaves and other parts.
  • Phloem: Transports the prepared food from the leaves to all other parts of the plant.

Transpiration

Transpiration is the evaporation of water molecules in the form of water vapour from the plant surface, primarily through the stomata on the leaves.

  • Creates a suction pull which helps transport water to great heights in tall trees.
  • Helps to lower and maintain the plant's body temperature during summers.
Fact Transpiration is often called a "necessary evil" for plants. An astounding fact is that a single maize plant can lose about 245 litres of water through transpiration in one growing season!

3. Transportation in Animals (Humans)

Higher animals possess a highly developed circulatory system designed to transport digested food, oxygen, carbon dioxide, and excretory wastes throughout the body.

The Heart

The heart is a hollow muscular organ located slightly to the left in the middle of the chest. It functions as a continuous pump.

  • A muscular wall called the septum divides the heart into left and right sides to prevent the mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.
  • Each side is further divided into two chambers: an upper atrium and a lower ventricle.

Blood Vessels

Blood vessels form an intricate network of tubes throughout the body, consisting of three main types:

  • Arteries: Carry oxygen-rich blood away from the heart to all body parts. They possess thick, elastic walls to withstand high pressure.
  • Veins: Carry oxygen-deficient (carbon dioxide-rich) blood back to the heart. They have thinner walls and operate under lower pressure.
  • Capillaries: Tiny branching tubes connecting arteries to veins inside organs to exchange substances directly with cells.
Important Veins are unique because they contain special valves. These valves ensure that the blood flows strictly in one direction—towards the heart—stopping any dangerous backflow.

Components of Blood

Blood is a fluid medium serving as the primary transport system. It comprises a liquid component and cell components:

  • Blood Plasma: A straw-coloured liquid making up about 55% of the total blood volume.
  • Red Blood Cells (RBCs): Contain haemoglobin, a red protein molecule. Haemoglobin binds with oxygen and acts as the respiratory carrier.
  • White Blood Cells (WBCs): The body's defence forces. They destroy harmful bacteria and protect against infections.
  • Blood Platelets: Essential for the clotting of blood, preventing excessive blood loss during injuries.

Heartbeat and Pulse Rate

The rhythmic contraction and relaxation of heart muscles constitute a heartbeat.

  • Systole: Contraction of the heart muscles.
  • Diastole: Relaxation of the heart muscles.

This rhythmic flow creates regular jerks in the main arteries, known as the pulse. At rest, a human heart beats 60 to 80 times per minute, which can spike up to 200 beats per minute during rigorous physical exercise.

4. Excretion in Animals

The process of removing harmful metabolic wastes produced by living organisms is called excretion. As nutrients are broken down within cells, toxic wastes like urea are generated and must be eliminated.

Organs of Excretion

  • The Kidneys: The primary excretory organs. Blood transports waste to the kidneys, which filter it to produce urine. The urine travels down the ureters, stores in the urinary bladder, and exits via the urethra.
  • The Lungs: Responsible for removing carbon dioxide produced during cellular respiration through exhalation.
  • The Skin: Supplied with numerous blood capillaries and sweat glands, the skin regulates heat through evaporation. Small amounts of urea and salts are also expelled via sweat.
Important Do not confuse excretion with egestion. Excretion is the removal of metabolic cellular wastes (like urea and CO2). Egestion is simply the elimination of undigested food (faeces) from the anus.
