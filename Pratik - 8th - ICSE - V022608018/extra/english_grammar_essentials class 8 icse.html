Grammar Essentials

1. Sentences

Concept

A Sentence is a group of words arranged in a specific order that makes complete sense. Every complete sentence contains a Subject (the doer or topic) and a Predicate (what is said about the subject).

Kinds of Sentences

  • Assertive (Declarative): Makes a statement or assertion. Ends with a full stop.
    Example: The sun rises in the east.
  • Interrogative: Asks a question. Ends with a question mark.
    Example: Where are you going?
  • Imperative: Expresses a command, request, entreaty, or advice. The subject 'You' is usually understood.
    Example: Please pass the salt. (Request) / Shut the door! (Command)
  • Exclamatory: Expresses strong or sudden feelings like joy, sorrow, or surprise. Ends with an exclamation mark.
    Example: What a beautiful painting!
Important

Punctuation Check: Always identify the type of sentence to apply the correct end punctuation. Marks (., ?, !) are crucial in ICSE writing and grammar exercises.

2. Subject-Verb Agreement (Concord)

Concept

Subject-Verb Agreement means that a verb must agree with its subject in number and person. If the subject is singular, the verb must be singular. If the subject is plural, the verb must be plural.

Crucial Rules for ICSE

  • Rule 1 (The 'And' Rule): Two or more singular subjects joined by 'and' usually take a plural verb.
    Example: Raj and Rohan are studying.
  • Rule 2 (Single Idea): If two singular nouns refer to the same person or thing, or express a single idea, the verb must be singular.
    Example: Bread and butter is his only food.
  • Rule 3 ('Or' / 'Nor'): Two or more singular subjects connected by or, nor, either...or, neither...nor take a singular verb.
    Example: Neither Hari nor Ravi has come.
  • Rule 4 (Proximity Rule): When subjects connected by 'or' or 'nor' are of different persons, the verb agrees with the subject nearest to it.
    Example: Either he or I am mistaken.
  • Rule 5 (Indefinite Pronouns): Words like each, every, everyone, someone, anybody, nobody, either, neither are always followed by a singular verb.
    Example: Each of the boys was given a prize.
Fact

Exceptions and Tricky Phrasing: When words are joined to a singular subject by with, as well as, along with, besides, the verb remains singular.
Example: The Captain, along with his team, was playing well.

3. Adverbs

Concept

An Adverb is a word that modifies the meaning of a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. It answers questions like how, when, where, and to what degree.

Kinds of Adverbs

  • Adverbs of Manner: Show how an action is done. (bravely, quickly, fast, hard)
  • Adverbs of Place: Show where an action is done. (here, there, everywhere, inside)
  • Adverbs of Time: Show when an action is done. (now, then, today, tomorrow, already)
  • Adverbs of Frequency: Show how often an action happens. (always, never, often, sometimes)
  • Adverbs of Degree: Show how much or to what extent. (very, entirely, almost, too, completely)
Important

Adjective vs. Adverb Trap: Some words look like adjectives but function as adverbs.
• He works hard. (Adverb of manner - modifying 'works').
• He is a hard worker. (Adjective - modifying 'worker').
Do not confuse 'hard' with 'hardly', which means 'scarcely'.

4. Conjunctions

Concept

A Conjunction is a word used to join words, phrases, or clauses together. It is strictly a "joining word" and does not perform any other function (unlike relative pronouns or prepositions).

Types of Conjunctions

  • Coordinating Conjunctions: Join elements of equal rank or importance (independent clauses). Remember the acronym FANBOYS (For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So).
  • Subordinating Conjunctions: Join a dependent (subordinate) clause to a main (independent) clause. They introduce a clause that cannot stand alone. Common examples: because, although, if, unless, since, until, while.
  • Correlative Conjunctions: Conjunctions that are used in pairs to connect elements of equal weight.
Example

Examples of Correlative Pairs:
• Neither... nor: Neither he nor his brother is present.
• Either... or: You must either obey my instructions or quit.
• Not only... but also: She is not only smart but also hardworking.
• Both... and: Both the teacher and the student were happy.

Important

Common ICSE Error: Do not use two conjunctions to join two clauses when only one is needed.
Incorrect: Though he is poor, but he is honest.
Correct: Though he is poor, he is honest. (Or: He is poor, but he is honest).
