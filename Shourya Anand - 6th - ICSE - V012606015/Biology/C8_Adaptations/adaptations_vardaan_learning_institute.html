Adaptations

1. Habitat

A habitat is a place where an organism (plants and animals) or a group of organisms live. It provides food, water, air, light, shelter, and favorable environmental conditions for them to live, grow, and breed. Examples of habitats include a dense forest for an elephant, a pond for a frog, or a sandy desert for a camel or cactus.

Components of Habitat

A habitat has two main components:

  • Abiotic or Physical components: These include non-living features like water (humidity), temperature, light, soil, rock, etc.
  • Biotic or Living components: These are the biological components, mainly including plants, animals, and microorganisms.

Types of Habitat

The main habitats in which living organisms live are:

  • Aquatic habitat: Organisms living in water (e.g., algae, fishes, whales). It includes fresh water (river, pond, lake) and marine water (sea, ocean).
  • Terrestrial habitat: Organisms living on land (e.g., camel, tiger). Common examples are deserts, mountains, grasslands.
  • Aerial habitat: Organisms that use air to perform activities (e.g., birds, bats, insects).
  • Arboreal habitat: Tree-dwelling animals that do not fly and live mostly on trees (e.g., monkey, squirrels, tree frog).
Concept Ecological Niche: The position and activities of an organism in its habitat. Each animal species has its own niche.

2. Adaptation

Living conditions differ across habitats (e.g., some are hot, cold, dry, or wet). Organisms develop special features and behaviors to adjust to their environment for survival, growth, and reproduction. This ability to change for better suitability or adjust to the environment is called adaptation.

Characteristics of adaptation:

  • All organisms show adaptation to some extent.
  • Organisms can tolerate a wide range of environmental changes.
  • Adaptation may be acquired or genetic.

3. Adaptations in Plants

Based on their habitats, plants are classified into different types, each with specific adaptations.

(i) Hydrophytes

Plants living in water. Their roots and stems are generally immersed.

  • Free-floating hydrophytes: Float freely on the water surface, not rooted in soil (e.g., Wolffia, Salvinia, Eichhornia).
  • Rooted, free-floating hydrophytes: Rooted in soil, but leaves/flowers float on the surface (e.g., Nymphaea, Nelumbo/Lotus).
  • Submerged floating hydrophytes: Completely submerged and not rooted (e.g., Utricularia).
  • Rooted submerged hydrophytes: Completely submerged and rooted in mud (e.g., Hydrilla, Vallisneria).
Fact Adaptations in Hydrophytes: They usually lack root caps and have poorly developed roots/root hair. Their stems are long, slender, spongy, and flexible. Large air cavities (aerenchyma) aid in floating. Leaves are thin, ribbon-shaped, and often lack stomata. Vascular and mechanical tissues are poorly developed, and the cuticle is thin or absent.

(ii) Mesophytes

Plants growing with a balanced availability of water in soil with high humidity and optimum temperature (e.g., peepal, neem, rose, mango). Ideal for crops, orchards, and forests.

  • Adaptations: Well-developed roots with root hair, solid freely branched stems, leaves of various shapes with widely present stomata, well-developed mechanical tissues, and a thin cuticle on the leaf epidermis.

(iii) Xerophytes

Plants growing in dry habitats like deserts and sandy hills, adapted to high temperatures and scarcity of water (e.g., Opuntia, Aloe vera, Acacia, babool).

  • Adaptations: Dense, expanded root system. Shiny, glazed leaf surfaces to reflect heat. Leaves reduced to spines/scales (like in Cactus or Babool) to reduce transpiration. Fleshy, spongy stem to store water (e.g., phylloclade in Cactus). Fewer stomata, sometimes sunken or covered in hair. Modified vascular tissues.

Adaptations in Mountain Plants

Plants in mountains (e.g., fir, pine, spruce, cedar) face cold, wind, and snowfall.

  • Adaptations: Tall and straight growth with few branches for maximum sunlight. Adventitious roots for anchorage against winds. Leaves have a waxy coating to prevent decay and are needle-shaped to slide off snow and reduce moisture loss. Thick bark protects against cold, and cones protect seeds.

Aerial Plants

Plants with roots above ground (e.g., Banyan, Mangroves, Orchids, Ivy). Some develop aerial roots to absorb sunlight or moisture, common in dense rainforests.

4. Adaptations in Animals

(i) Adaptations in Aquatic Animals

Animals living in water (e.g., fishes) have specific features.

  • Body covered with scales, cuticle, or shells for waterproofing and protection.
  • Presence of gas-filled sacs (air bladders) for buoyancy, respiration, and sound production.
  • Streamlined body: Spindle-shaped to reduce water resistance during fast movement.
  • Respiration through gills.
  • Absence of skin glands (sweat/oil).
  • Movement using fins (fishes) or webbed toes (frogs, ducks).

(ii) Adaptations in Terrestrial Animals

Land animals breathe through lungs and adapt based on their specific land habitat.

Mesic Habitat Animals

  • Cursorial (runners): Streamlined body for fast movement (e.g., tiger, deer, horse).
  • Fossorial (burrowing/digging): Tapering head/snout, long claws on forelimbs, short tail, reduced external ears (e.g., moles, rats).
  • Digitigrade walking: Walking on digits/toes (e.g., dog, cat).
  • Plantigrade walking: Entire palm/sole rests on the ground (e.g., bear, human).
  • Unguligrade walking: Walking on modified nails called hooves (e.g., horse, camel).
  • Bipedal: Two-footed locomotion (e.g., human, kangaroo).

Mountain Animals

Animals like the snow leopard, yak, and mountain goat face extreme cold and rugged terrain.

  • Thick fur and a thick layer of fat beneath the skin for insulation.
  • Small ears (e.g., snow leopard) to reduce heat loss.
  • Tall legs and flexible hooves (e.g., mountain goat) for climbing rocky slopes.
  • Well-developed large heart and lungs to breathe in lower oxygen levels.

Xeric (Desert) Habitat Animals

Animals face severe heat and water scarcity.

  • Protective body covering to prevent water loss via evaporation.
  • Many are nocturnal (e.g., rats, snakes), burrowing during the day and active at night.
Important Camel - "Ship of the Desert": Adapted with a two-toed foot and fleshy pads for walking on sand. Long eyelashes and closable nostrils protect against blowing sand. Excretes little urine, produces dry dung, and doesn't sweat to conserve water. Can drink ~50 liters of water at once. Hump stores fat as reserved food.

(iii) Adaptations in Aerial Animals

Birds are adapted for flight.

  • Streamlined body: Decreases air resistance.
  • Pneumatic bones: Hollow bones filled with air cavities make them lightweight.
  • Forelimbs modified into wings with strong flight muscles attached to the breastbone.
  • Body covered in feathers for flight and insulation.
  • Lungs are supplemented with air sacs to keep the body light.

(iv) Adaptations in Arboreal Animals

Tree-dwelling animals (e.g., monkeys, squirrels, tree frogs).

  • Well-developed rotating shoulder bones for support.
  • Long, hairy tail in monkeys for grasping branches and balance.
  • Broad hip bones in monkeys to support body weight.
