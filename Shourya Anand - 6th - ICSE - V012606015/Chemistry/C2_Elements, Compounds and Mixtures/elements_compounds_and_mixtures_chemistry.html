CHAPTER 2: ELEMENTS, COMPOUNDS AND MIXTURES

1. Pure and Impure Substances

Matter is classified into two main groups: pure substances and impure substances.

Concept

Pure Substance: A kind of matter which consists of the same type of particles and cannot be separated into its components by any physical means. Elements and compounds are pure substances.

2. Elements

Elements are pure substances which can neither be broken down into further simpler substances nor formed from two or more simpler substances by any physical or chemical means.

Characteristics of Elements

  • An element is a pure substance.
  • It is made up of only one kind of particle.
  • Elements may occur naturally or may be prepared artificially.
  • Elements exist in all three physical states (solid, liquid, gas).
Fact

There are currently 118 elements known. Out of these, 92 are naturally occurring in the earth's crust, while the rest are prepared artificially by scientists (radioactive elements). The most abundant elements in the earth's crust are oxygen, silicon, aluminium, and iron.

Classification of Elements

Elements are classified into four main categories based on their physical and chemical properties:

A. Metals

Metals are generally hard solids at room temperature and have a lustrous (shiny) surface. They are malleable and ductile. They have high boiling and melting points, and are good conductors of heat and electricity.

  • Malleability: The property by which substances can be hammered or rolled into thin sheets without breaking (e.g., gold, aluminium, silver).
  • Ductility: The property by which substances can be drawn into thin wires without breaking (e.g., gold, silver, copper).
  • Conductivity: Metals allow heat and electricity to pass through them easily.
  • Exception: Mercury is a metal but exists as a liquid at room temperature.

B. Non-metals

Non-metals have a dull surface, are non-malleable, and non-ductile. Their boiling and melting points are generally low. They are bad conductors of heat and electricity. They usually exist as solids or gases.

  • Exceptions: Graphite (a form of carbon) and iodine have shiny surfaces. Graphite is also a good conductor of heat and electricity. Bromine is a non-metal that exists as a liquid.
  • Hydrogen is the lightest element.

C. Metalloids

Metalloids are a group of elements that lie between metals and non-metals in terms of their properties. They exhibit some metallic and some non-metallic characteristics.

Examples: Boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony.

D. Noble or Inert Gases

These are gaseous elements that do not react chemically with other elements and are highly stable.

Examples: Helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon.

Comparison: Metals vs. Non-metals

Properties Metals Non-metals
Appearance Shining surface (lustrous) Dull surface (except graphite and iodine)
Density High density Low density
Melting Point High melting point Low melting point
Boiling Point High boiling point Low boiling point
Ductility Ductile (can be drawn into wires) Non-ductile
Malleability Malleable (can be beaten into sheets) Non-malleable
Conductivity Good conductors of heat and electricity Bad conductors (except graphite)

3. Symbols of Elements

A chemical symbol is a shorthand method used to represent an element. The system used today was developed by the Swedish Chemist J.J. Berzelius.

Important

Rules for Determining Symbols:
1. The first letter of the English name is used (e.g., H for Hydrogen, O for Oxygen).
2. If names share the first letter, the first and second letters are used (e.g., He for Helium, Ca for Calcium).
3. If the first two letters are shared, the first letter and a prominent consonant are used (e.g., Mg for Magnesium, Mn for Manganese).
4. Some symbols are derived from Latin names (e.g., Na for Sodium from Natrium, Fe for Iron from Ferrum).

4. Compounds

Compounds are pure substances formed when two or more elements combine chemically together in a fixed proportion by weight.

When elements form a compound, the compound exhibits properties entirely different from its constituent elements.

Examples of Compounds

  • Sugar: Formed by carbon (solid), hydrogen (gas), and oxygen (gas). The resulting sugar is a solid with different properties.
  • Salt (Sodium Chloride): Formed by sodium (a highly reactive solid metal) and chlorine (a poisonous gas). The resulting salt is essential for life.
  • Water: Formed by hydrogen (a combustible gas) and oxygen (a gas that supports combustion). Water is a liquid that extinguishes fire.

Characteristics of Compounds

  • A compound is a pure substance.
  • It is homogeneous (has the same composition and properties throughout).
  • The original components lose their individual properties.
  • Elements in a compound cannot be separated by physical methods (e.g., a magnet cannot separate iron from iron sulphide).
  • Energy is either absorbed or released during the formation of a compound.

5. Atoms and Molecules

Atoms

An atom is the smallest particle of an element. It possesses all the properties of that element and may or may not have an independent existence.

  • The atoms of most elements are very reactive and do not exist freely.
  • Atoms of one element are alike but differ in properties (shape, size, mass) from atoms of other elements.
  • An atom maintains its properties through all physical and chemical changes.
Concept

Structure of an Atom: An atom consists of a central core called the nucleus containing positively charged protons and neutral neutrons. Negatively charged electrons orbit the nucleus.

Molecules

A molecule is the smallest particle of an element or a compound that has an independent existence and shows all the properties of that substance.

  • Atoms in a molecule are held together by attractive forces called chemical bonds.
  • A molecule can be represented by a molecular formula.
  • Molecules can be formed by atoms of the same element (e.g., O2) or different elements (e.g., H2O).

Comparison: Atoms vs. Molecules

Feature Atom Molecule
Definition Smallest particle of an element. Group of two or more atoms chemically combined.
Composition Consists of a nucleus (protons, neutrons) and electrons. Consists of like or different atoms chemically bound.
Visibility Cannot be seen, even with a magnifying microscope. Cannot be seen with the naked eye, but some can be seen with highly magnifying microscopes.
Divisibility Cannot be further divided (chemically). Can be further divided to give individual atoms.
Existence May or may not have independent existence (e.g., single O atom). Capable of independent existence (e.g., O2 molecule).

6. Chemical Formulae and Atomicity

Atomicity

Atomicity is the number of atoms present in a single molecule of an element.

  • Monoatomic elements: Exist as single atoms (e.g., Inert gases like Helium, Neon, and metals like Sodium, Potassium). Atomicity = 1.
  • Diatomic molecules: Contain two atoms (e.g., Hydrogen H₂, Oxygen O₂, Nitrogen N₂, Chlorine Cl₂). Atomicity = 2.
  • Triatomic molecules: Contain three atoms (e.g., Ozone O₃). Atomicity = 3.
  • Polyatomic molecules: Contain more than two atoms (e.g., Phosphorus P₄). Atomicity = 4.

Chemical Formulae

The chemical formula represents one molecule of an element or a compound.

  • Formula for an element represents the number of atoms in one molecule (e.g., O₂).
  • Formula for a compound represents the ratio of different elements in the compound. For example, the formula for water is H₂O, meaning two atoms of hydrogen combine with one atom of oxygen.

7. Mixtures

A mixture is an impure substance formed when particles of two or more elements or compounds are mixed together mechanically in any proportion, and they retain their original properties.

Examples: Blood, tea, cold drinks, milk, air, soil, seawater.

Characteristics of Mixtures

  • Components are combined mechanically, not chemically.
  • Components are mixed in any ratio or proportion.
  • Individual components retain their original properties.
  • Components can be separated by simple physical processes (e.g., winnowing, sieving, filtration, evaporation).
  • Formation of mixtures does not involve energy exchange.

Types of Mixtures

Mixtures can be homogeneous or heterogeneous.

A. Homogeneous Mixture

A mixture in which the components are distributed uniformly throughout the mixture. You cannot distinguish the components visually.

Examples: Sugar solution, salt solution, milk, air, brass (alloy of copper and zinc).

B. Heterogeneous Mixture

A mixture in which the components are not distributed uniformly throughout the mixture. The components can often be distinguished.

Examples: A mixture of iron filings and sulphur powder, sand and water, soil, gun powder.
