Water

Water is a crucial natural resource. It covers about 70% of the Earth's surface and is absolutely essential for the survival of all forms of life. Its chemical formula is H₂O.

Fact

About 70% of the human body weight is water. Green vegetables contain about 80-90% water, and milk contains about 80% water by weight!

1. Water Resources

Water is found naturally in several forms, which are broadly classified into:

  • Surface Water: Found in rivers, lakes, seas, and oceans. River water carries dissolved impurities and suspended particles. Sea water is the largest resource but is highly impure and salty.
  • Rain Water: The primary source of water and the purest form of naturally occurring water. However, the first showers may contain dissolved atmospheric gases (like carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide) and dust particles.
  • Underground Water: Rainwater that soaks into the ground and gets trapped beneath the earth. The upper limit of this depth is called the Water Table. Water from wells and springs is mostly pure.

2. Three States of Water

Water is a unique substance that exists naturally in all three physical states. These states are highly interchangeable based on temperature (heating or cooling).

  • Solid State: Found as ice, snow, and frost.
  • Liquid State: Found as water in seas, rivers, lakes, and rain.
  • Gaseous State: Found as water vapour or steam in the atmosphere.

3. Importance of Water

Water is essential for sustaining life and everyday activities:

  • Biological Processes: Plays an important role in blood circulation, digestion, and excretion in animals. It regulates body temperature through sweating.
  • Photosynthesis: Plants use water, carbon dioxide, and sunlight to prepare their food. Water also transports minerals within the plant.
  • Habitat: Provides a living environment for numerous aquatic plants and animals.
  • Daily & Industrial Use: Used for drinking, cooking, washing, generating electricity (hydroelectricity), and as a coolant or solvent in industries.

4. Purification and Potable Water

Water from natural sources must be purified before consumption. The water fit for drinking is known as Potable Water.

Characteristics of Potable Water

  • It should be colourless and odourless.
  • It should be clean, transparent, and free from suspended impurities.
  • It should be completely free from harmful bacteria and germs.

Methods of Purification

  • Filtration: Passing water through layers of gravel and sand to remove suspended solid impurities.
  • Chlorination: Adding chlorine to water to kill harmful bacteria and germs.
  • Aeration: Exposing water to air under pressure. Oxygen dissolves in water, killing bacteria and removing bad taste and odour.
  • Boiling: Boiling water at home is a simple and effective way to kill germs. Adding a few crystals of potassium permanganate into wells also acts as a germicide.
Important

Waterborne Diseases: Consuming contaminated water can lead to serious diseases caused by pathogenic microorganisms. Common waterborne diseases include Diarrhoea, Gastroenteritis, Cholera, and Typhoid.

5. Water as a Universal Solvent

Water has the remarkable ability to dissolve a vast number of substances, which is why it is called the Universal Solvent.

Concept
  • Solvent: The liquid that dissolves a substance (e.g., Water).
  • Solute: The substance that dissolves in the solvent (e.g., Sugar or Salt).
  • Solution: The homogeneous mixture formed when a solute dissolves in a solvent (e.g., Saltwater).

Solubility & Solutions

The maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in 100 ml of water at a given temperature is called its solubility. Solubility generally increases with an increase in temperature. A solution that cannot dissolve any more solute at a specific temperature is called a saturated solution.

Hardness of Water

Water becomes "hard" when it contains dissolved salts of magnesium and calcium (like bicarbonates, chlorides, or sulphates). Hard water does not lather easily with soap. Hardness can be temporary (removable by boiling) or permanent.

6. The Water Cycle

The continuous cyclic movement of water from the earth to the atmosphere and back is called the Water Cycle or Hydrologic Cycle. The key steps include:

  • Evaporation: Sun's heat changes liquid water from oceans and lakes into water vapour.
  • Transpiration: Plants release excess water vapour into the air through their leaves.
  • Condensation: Water vapour cools down as it rises, forming tiny water droplets that gather to make clouds.
  • Precipitation: When clouds become heavy, water falls back to earth as rain, snow, or hail.
  • Surface Run-off: Rainwater flows down hills to form streams and rivers, eventually returning to the sea.

7. Water Pollution and Conservation

Water is polluted when its physical, chemical, or biological properties are altered by harmful substances, making it unfit for use.

Causes of Water Pollution

  • Domestic Sewage: Discharge of human waste and washing chemicals into rivers.
  • Industrial Wastes: Toxic chemicals (lead, mercury, arsenic) released directly into water bodies.
  • Agricultural Wastes: Run-off containing excess fertilizers and pesticides.
  • Oil Spillage & Acid Rain: Oil leaks from ships and toxic gases mixing with rainwater.

Conservation of Water

To sustainably manage our fresh water and meet future demands, we must conserve it by:

  • Practicing Rainwater Harvesting.
  • Preventing leakage in taps and avoiding unnecessary wastage (like leaving the tap running while brushing).
  • Planting more trees (afforestation) to improve groundwater retention.
  • Treating domestic and industrial sewage before discharging it.
