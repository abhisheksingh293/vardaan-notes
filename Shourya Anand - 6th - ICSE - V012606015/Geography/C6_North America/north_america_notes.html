North America

1. Discovery and Historical Context

North America is the world's third-largest continent. Both the North and South Americas get their name from Amerigo Vespucci, an Italian explorer who reached the region in 1507 CE.

Fact Christopher Columbus was the first to reach the Caribbean Islands in 1492 CE when he sailed westward in search of India. Because he mistook the islands for India, he called the region the West Indies and its inhabitants Red Indians.

Because they were discovered much later than the other known continents of the world, North and South America together came to be known as the New World.

2. Location and Extent

North America lies entirely within the northern hemisphere. The continent is intersected by important latitudes, notably the Arctic Circle in the north and the Tropic of Cancer in the south.

  • Latitudinal Extent: 7°N to 85°N
  • Longitudinal Extent: 20°W to 179°W

Surrounding Water Bodies & Land Connections

The continent is bordered by several massive bodies of water:

  • North: Arctic Ocean
  • East: North Atlantic Ocean
  • South: Caribbean Sea
  • West: North Pacific Ocean

The Bering Strait separates North America from Asia. Geographically, it is joined to South America by the Isthmus of Panama, though it is physically separated from it for naval transport by the man-made Panama Canal.

Coastline and Islands

Unlike Europe, North America does not have a deeply indented continuous coastline. Instead, it features a few large, wide indentations such as the Hudson Bay, Gulf of Mexico, Gulf of California, Gulf of St. Lawrence, and the Beaufort Sea.

Notable islands include Greenland (the largest island in the world) and the Arctic Archipelago.

3. Political Divisions

North America encompasses a wide variety of independent nations and island territories.

Concept Geographers often categorize the continent into two cultural and geographic sub-regions:
  • Anglo-America: Consists of Canada and the United States of America (USA).
  • Middle America: Refers to Mexico, the countries of Central America (like Belize, El Salvador), and the Caribbean Islands.

Canada is the largest country by area on the continent, occupying almost half of the total landmass of North America.

4. Major Physical Divisions

The geography of North America is highly diverse and can be classified into four primary physical divisions:

  • The Canadian Shield: A massive geological core covering much of eastern and central Canada.
  • The Appalachian Highlands: Also known as the Eastern Highlands, featuring older, rounded mountain ranges.
  • The Central Lowlands: The Great Central Plains that stretch through the interior of the continent.
  • The Western Cordilleras: A vast series of high, rugged, young fold mountain ranges along the western coast.

5. Lumbering Industry in Canada

Canada is home to the world's largest coniferous forests, known as the Taiga forests. This makes lumbering the main economic activity for many people in the region. The forests form a broad belt stretching from the Atlantic coast in the east to the Pacific coast in the west.

The main varieties of trees found in these forests include Douglas fir, hemlock, cedar, pine, larch, spruce, and maple.

Fact Canada leads the world in the export of lumber and wood products. Coniferous trees yield softwood, which is easy to saw and pulp. Products include plywood, newsprint, cellulose, rayon, timber, cardboard, and paper.

Lumberjacks: Workers of the Industry

The skilled workers involved in the logging industry are known as Lumberjacks. The process is divided into specialized stages handled by different groups:

  • High Riggers: They climb to the top of the tree, cutting away branches and removing the top to prevent damage when the tree falls.
  • Fallers (Sawyers): They use powerful chainsaws to cut down the main trunk of the tree.
  • Skidders: They drag the heavy fallen logs out of the forest to nearby roads, railways, or frozen rivers.
  • Haulers: They transport the collected logs to the pulp mills or sawmills.
Important Historically, lumbering was primarily a winter activity. During winter, the sap stops flowing inside the trees, and the frozen, snow-covered ground makes hauling heavy logs much easier. Logs were piled near frozen rivers and floated downstream to mills when the ice melted in the spring. Today, heavy mechanization has changed this scenario, making logging a year-round industry.
