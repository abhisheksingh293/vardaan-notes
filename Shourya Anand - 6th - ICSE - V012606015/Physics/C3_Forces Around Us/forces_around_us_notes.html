Forces Around Us

1. What is Force?

Concept

A pull or push acting on a body which tends to change its state of rest or motion, or the speed or direction of motion, or its shape or size is called force.

  • To make force effective, an interaction between two objects is necessary (e.g., pushing a door, pulling a rubber band).
  • SI Unit: The standard unit of force is Newton (N). It is also expressed in gram-force (gf), kilogram-force (kgf), and dyne.
  • Measurement: Force applied on a body can be measured using a device called a spring balance.
Fact

Resultant Force: When multiple forces act on a body, their combined effect is the resultant force. If forces are applied in the same direction, they are added. If forces are applied in the opposite direction, they are subtracted.

2. Effects of Force

When an unbalanced force acts on a body, it can cause various changes. A force can:

  • Produce Motion: A force can move a stationary object (e.g., pushing a heavy box).
  • Stop Motion: A force can stop a moving object by acting in the opposite direction (e.g., catching a moving ball).
  • Change Speed: By applying force in the direction of motion, speed increases. Applying force in the opposite direction reduces speed.
  • Change Direction: A force can alter the path of a moving object (e.g., hitting a cricket ball with a bat).
  • Change Shape and Size: Squeezing a sponge or stretching a rubber band changes its physical dimensions.
Important

Force can change the state of motion or shape of a body, but it never changes the mass of an object. The amount of matter inside an object remains constant regardless of the force applied.

3. Types of Forces

Forces are broadly classified into two categories based on how objects interact:

3.1. Contact Forces

Forces in which the two interacting bodies are in physical contact with each other.

  • Applied Force: Force applied by a person, animal, or object to change a body's state (e.g., pushing a chair).
  • Normal Force: The upward support force exerted by a stable body on an object in contact with it (e.g., a table supporting a glass).
  • Tension: The force exerted by a stretched string or rope.
  • Muscular Force: Force exerted by the muscles of our body (e.g., kicking a football, lifting a bag).
  • Collision Force: The force that comes into play when two bodies collide (e.g., two cars crashing).
  • Frictional Force: The force that opposes the relative motion of two bodies in contact (e.g., a rolling ball slowing down).
  • Mechanical Force: Force produced by a machine using external energy like fuel or electricity (e.g., a car engine).

3.2. Non-contact Forces

Forces which do not require physical contact and act across a distance.

  • Magnetic Force: The force of attraction or repulsion exerted by a magnet on magnetic materials (iron, nickel, cobalt) or other magnets.
  • Electrostatic Force: The force exerted by a charged body on another charged or uncharged body (e.g., a rubbed plastic comb attracting bits of paper).
  • Gravitational Force (Gravity): The attractive pull of the Earth (or any massive body) on all objects. It keeps us bound to the ground.

4. Friction

Concept

Friction is the force exerted by a surface or body that opposes the motion of a body in contact with it. It acts in the direction opposite to the direction of motion.

Causes: Friction is caused by microscopic irregularities (ridges and grooves) on the surfaces in contact. When surfaces rub, these irregularities interlock and oppose motion.

  • Properties of Friction:
    • It always opposes motion.
    • It produces heat (e.g., rubbing hands together).
    • It causes wear and tear of surfaces (e.g., shoe soles wearing out).
  • Factors Affecting Friction:
    • Nature of surfaces: Rough surfaces create more friction; smooth surfaces create less.
    • Weight of the body: A heavier body exerts more normal force on the ground, resulting in greater friction.
    • Note: Friction is independent of the area of contact.

4.1. Types of Friction

  • Static Friction: Friction between objects at rest. The maximum opposing force before motion begins is called static (limiting) friction.
  • Kinetic Friction: Friction that exists when objects are in motion. It is of two types:
    • Sliding Friction: When an object slides over a surface.
    • Rolling Friction: When an object rolls over a surface (rolling friction is always less than sliding friction).
Important

Friction is often called a necessary evil because it has both significant advantages and disadvantages in our daily lives.

4.2. Advantages of Friction

  • Helps us walk without slipping on the ground.
  • Allows treaded tyres to grip the road, enabling safe driving.
  • Enables brakes to stop a moving vehicle.
  • Allows us to write with a pen on paper or chalk on a board.
  • Enables us to light a matchstick, tie knots, and hold objects firmly.

4.3. Disadvantages of Friction

  • Causes wear and tear on machine parts, vehicle tyres, and footwear.
  • Produces unnecessary heat in machines, requiring cooling mechanisms and increasing maintenance costs.
  • Decreases the efficiency of machines because extra energy is wasted overcoming friction.

4.4. Methods of Controlling Friction

Increasing Friction:

  • Grooving: Adding treads to tyres, grooves to footwear soles, and spikes to athletes' shoes for better grip.
  • Making surfaces rough: Spreading sand/gravel on wet roads, or using spiked covers on vehicle handles.

Reducing Friction:

  • Polishing: Smoothing out surface irregularities.
  • Lubricating: Applying oil, grease, or graphite to fill depressions between surfaces and form a slippery layer.
  • Streamlining: Giving objects (aeroplanes, boats, fish) a special shape (broad in middle, narrow at ends) to reduce fluid friction.
  • Using Ball Bearings or Rollers: Converting sliding friction into much weaker rolling friction inside machines.
