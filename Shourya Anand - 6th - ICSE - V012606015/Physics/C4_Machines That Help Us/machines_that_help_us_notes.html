Machines That Help Us

1. Work and Energy

Concept

Work is said to be done if a force acting on a body moves it or changes its position or shape in the direction of the force acting on the body.

  • Formula for Work: Work = Force × Distance.
  • SI Unit: The standard unit of work is Joule (J). Calorie is another common unit used.
  • Zero Work: Work done is zero when a body does not move even if force is applied on it (e.g., a girl standing with her bag waiting for a bus does no work as she is not moving).
Fact

Energy is the ability of a body to do work. Energy is the cause, and work is the effect. When we do work, the energy in our body gets used up.

2. Understanding Machines

An object that allows us to perform a task with less effort is called a machine.

  • Simple Machines: Basic mechanical devices used in everyday life, such as scissors, doorknobs, bottle openers, hammers, and sewing needles. They neither provide energy by themselves nor do work on their own. They work only when we apply force.
  • Complex Machines: Machines made up of a combination of two or more simple machines. For instance, a bicycle (which has wheels, levers, and pulleys).

3. Mechanical Advantage (MA)

In reference to simple machines, a vital factor is the Mechanical Advantage. It is the ratio of the load lifted by a machine to the effort applied on it.

  • Effort (E): The force applied on the machine to do work.
  • Load (L): The resulting force exerted by the machine to lift or move the object.
  • Formula: MA = Load (L) / Effort (E)
Important

Mechanical Advantage has no unit since it is a ratio.

  • If MA > 1: The machine is a force multiplier (Load is more than Effort).
  • If MA < 1: The machine is a speed multiplier (Effort is more than Load).
  • If MA = 1: The effort and load are equal.

4. Types of Simple Machines

There are six basic types of simple machines:

4.1. Lever

A rigid bar or rod that can move about a fixed support or point is called a lever. The fixed support about which the lever rotates is called the fulcrum. Levers are divided into three classes:

  • Class I Lever: The fulcrum is between the effort and the load. MA can be greater than, equal to, or less than 1. (Examples: scissors, pliers, see-saw).
  • Class II Lever: The load is between the fulcrum and the effort. MA is always greater than 1. Used as a force multiplier. (Examples: bottle opener, wheelbarrow, nutcracker).
  • Class III Lever: The effort is between the fulcrum and the load. MA is always less than 1. Used as a speed multiplier. (Examples: tongs, fishing rod, broom).

4.2. Inclined Plane

A slanting surface which connects two points at different heights so that heavier objects can be moved up or down easily. It reduces the effort needed to lift objects.

  • Formula: MA = Length of the inclined plane / Vertical height.
  • The longer the distance of the ramp, the less effort is required to push the load.
  • Examples: Children's slides, ramps for wheelchairs, sloping roads.

4.3. Pulley

A simple machine consisting of a wheel with a grooved rim in which a rope or chain can pass. It is used to lift heavy objects by changing the direction of force.

  • Fixed Pulley: Attached to a hook or wall. It does not amplify force but changes its direction (MA = 1).
  • Movable Pulley: Moves with the load. It reduces the effort used to pull the load (MA = 2).
  • Block and Tackle System: Several pulleys used together to drastically reduce the force needed to lift very heavy loads.

4.4. Screw

An inclined plane wound around a rod spirally, making a number of turns. It is commonly used for holding things together.

  • Key Terms: Threads (windings), Lead (linear distance covered in one turn), Pitch (distance between adjacent threads).
  • Examples: Bolts, bottle caps, screw jacks (used to lift cars).

4.5. Wheel and Axle

A wheel attached to a cylindrical rod or shaft (the axle). They must move together. It can be used as a speed multiplier or a force multiplier.

  • Examples: Steering wheel, doorknob, bicycle tyres, screwdriver.

4.6. Wedge

Made of wood or metal with two slanting sides ending in a sharp or pointed edge. Generally a combination of two inclined planes used to split or hold objects.

  • Examples: Blade, knife, axe, door stopper.

5. Efficiency of a Machine

Concept

Efficiency refers to the ratio of the work done by a machine (output energy) to the work done on the machine (input energy).

Formula: Efficiency = (Output energy / Input energy) × 100

Important

No machine is 100% efficient! An ideal machine would have 100% efficiency, but in reality, some input energy is always lost in overcoming friction and generating heat. Therefore, output energy is always less than input energy.

6. Care of Machines

To ensure a long life and better performance, machines require proper maintenance:

  • Covering: Always cover a machine when it is not in use to protect it from dust.
  • Avoid Moisture: Keep machines in clean places away from dirt and moisture to prevent rusting. Rust weakens metal parts and increases friction.
  • Painting: Paint metal surfaces to protect them from moisture and rust.
  • Lubrication: Oil or grease the moving parts of the machine regularly to reduce friction and avoid wear and tear.
