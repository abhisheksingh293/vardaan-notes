Buddhism and Jainism

1. The Rise of New Beliefs

The 6th century BCE was a period of great social turmoil and growing inequalities in India. The Vedic religion had become overly complicated due to the rigidities of the caste system and an increasing focus on expensive, elaborate rituals and sacrifices. This social inequality created deep dissatisfaction among the common people and the lower classes.

In response to this discontent, new belief systems emerged around 500 BCE. These new paths challenged existing superstitions and inequalities, profoundly influencing Indian philosophy, art, architecture, and culture.

2. Buddhism

The founder of Buddhism was a Sakya prince named Gautama Siddhartha. He was born around 563 BCE in Lumbini, near Kapilavastu in Nepal. Raised in luxury, it was predicted at his birth that he would become either a universal monarch or an enlightened soul.

The Four Great Sights

Married at 16 to Princess Yasodhara and father to a son named Rahul, Gautama lived a sheltered life until a pivotal chariot ride. He witnessed four sights that deeply disturbed him and changed his life forever:

  • An old man (revealing the reality of aging).
  • A sick man (showing the pain of illness).
  • A dead body (demonstrating the certainty of death).
  • An ascetic (a holy man with a calm, peaceful expression amidst the suffering).

Realizing that aging, sickness, and death are inescapable, he renounced his princely life, leaving his family behind to seek the truth about overcoming suffering.

Fact

The Enlightenment: After six years of severe and unsuccessful penance, Gautama meditated under a peepal tree at Bodh Gaya for 49 days. It was here that he attained enlightenment and became known as the Buddha (the enlightened one) or Shakyamuni.

Teachings of Buddha

Buddha delivered his first sermon, known as Dharmachakra Pravartana (Turning the Wheel of Dharma), at the Deer Park in Sarnath. He preached in Pali, the simple language spoken by the common masses.

Concept

The Four Noble Truths: The core foundation of Buddhist philosophy states that: (1) The world is full of suffering. (2) Suffering arises from desire. (3) Giving up desire ends suffering. (4) Salvation (nirvana) is attained by following the Eight-Fold Path.

The Eight-Fold Path (Ashtangika Marga) consists of: Right belief, Right thought, Right speech, Right action, Right livelihood, Right effort, Right memory, and Right meditation.

Furthermore, Buddha strongly emphasized ahimsa (non-violence) against all people and animals. He did not mention a supreme God and firmly rejected the caste system, preaching that a person's deeds, not their birth, defined their worth.

Spread, Divisions, and Literature

Buddhism spread rapidly across India and to countries like Sri Lanka, Myanmar, and China, aided by dedicated monks, trade routes, and royal patronage from kings like Ashoka and Kanishka.

  • Divisions: During Kanishka's reign (1st century CE), Buddhism split into two major sects: Mahayana (Greater Vehicle) and Hinayana (Lesser Vehicle).
  • Literature: Key Buddhist texts include the Tripitakas (rules for monks and nuns) and the Jataka Tales (moral stories written in Pali about the previous lives of Buddha).

3. Jainism

Jainism was popularized by Mahavira, the 24th and last Tirthankara (spiritual teacher). He was born in 540 BCE in Kundagrama, near Vaishali, as the son of Siddhartha, a tribal chief.

At the age of 30, Mahavira renounced his family life to become an ascetic. After 12 years of deep meditation and severe penance, he attained enlightenment.

Important

Upon attaining supreme knowledge, Mahavira was called Jina, meaning "the conqueror" (one who has conquered his inner desires). His followers subsequently became known as Jains. He passed away at the age of 72, attaining complete liberation (parinirvana).

Teachings of Mahavira

Like Buddha, Mahavira rejected the authority of the Vedas and meaningless rituals. He believed in Karma (actions of past lives determine present conditions) and Moksha (liberation from the cycle of birth and death).

His philosophy was centered around the Three Jewels (Triratnas):

  • Right faith
  • Right knowledge
  • Right conduct

Mahavira was a strict promoter of ahimsa, believing that absolutely every living being—including plants and tiny insects—possessed a soul and must never be harmed.

Divisions and Literature

Over time, Jainism largely split into two main sects based on their strictness regarding physical possessions:

  • Digambaras ("sky-clad"): Monks who renounced all clothes.
  • Svetambaras ("white-clad"): Monks who wore simple white robes.

The teachings of Mahavira were initially passed down orally in texts called Purvas and Angas, and were written in Prakrit, another language of the common people.

4. Why Buddhism and Jainism Became Popular

Both religions gained massive followings quickly because they shared several highly appealing similarities that contrasted with the Later Vedic religion:

  • Equality: They entirely rejected the rigid caste system imposed by the brahmins, allowing anyone to join regardless of birth.
  • Language of the Masses: They preached in Pali and Prakrit, making their teachings easily understandable to commoners, unlike Sanskrit which was restricted to priests.
  • Simplicity: They did away with costly, complex rituals and animal sacrifices, offering a clear, simple code of conduct for a better life.
  • Non-Violence (Ahimsa): Both placed a heavy emphasis on respecting all forms of life.

Due to the emphasis on equality, merchants and traders were heavily attracted to these religions, providing immense wealth and donations to build monasteries and stupas. Powerful emperors acting as patrons secured the survival and spread of these great teachings across the world.
