Early States

1. From Tribes to Territories

In the Later Vedic period, the focus of power shifted from the northwest to the Gangetic plains. The society evolved from nomadic tribal organizations (called janas) to settled agricultural communities holding fixed territories. This transition marked the beginning of the earliest states in ancient India.

Concept

Janapada: The word literally means the land where the jana (the people or tribe) set its foot and settled down permanently. Archaeologists have excavated many janapada settlements, such as Purana Qila in Delhi, Hastinapur near Meerut, and Atranjikhera near Etah.

2. The Rise of Mahajanapadas

Around 2500 years ago (circa 500 BCE), some janapadas became larger, wealthier, and more powerful than others. These expanded and highly powerful states came to be known as Mahajanapadas.

Buddhist and Jain texts clearly mention a list of 16 Mahajanapadas that existed in the Indian subcontinent. Most of these states had a distinct capital city, and many of these capitals were fortified. Huge walls of wood, brick, or stone were built around them to provide protection from attacks, demonstrate the wealth of the king, and allow easier control over the people living inside.

Key Features of Mahajanapadas:

  • Standing Armies: Kings maintained permanent, regular armies for defense and expansion. Soldiers were paid regular salaries throughout the year (often using punch-marked coins).
  • Fortifications: Massive stone and brick walls were constructed requiring immense labor and resources.
  • Bureaucracy: Ministers (amatyas) and officials were appointed to assist the king in administration and justice.

3. Changes in Agriculture

The rise of the Mahajanapadas was heavily supported by massive advancements in agriculture, which produced the extra food needed to sustain large armies and urban populations.

  • Iron Ploughshares: People began replacing wooden ploughshares with iron ones. This made it possible to turn over heavy, clayey soil more effectively, yielding more grain.
  • Transplantation of Paddy: Instead of randomly scattering seeds, farmers started growing saplings first and then planting them in flooded fields. This drastically increased agricultural production, though it required back-breaking labor (usually done by dasa, dasis, and landless laborers called kammakaras).

4. The System of Taxation

To build huge forts and maintain massive armies, kings needed vast resources. They could no longer depend on occasional, voluntary gifts (bhaga) brought by the people. They started collecting regular taxes.

  • Taxes on Crops: This was the most important tax since most people were farmers. The tax was usually fixed at 1/6th of what was produced. This share was known as bhaga (a share).
  • Taxes on Crafts persons: Artisans paid taxes in the form of labor. For example, a weaver or a smith might have to work for the king for one day every month for free.
  • Taxes on Herders: Herders paid taxes in the form of animals and animal produce.
  • Taxes on Trade: Goods brought and sold through trade were taxed.
  • Taxes on Hunters and Gatherers: They had to provide forest produce (like timber, honey, or ivory) to the king.

5. Case Study: Magadha (A Powerful Monarchy)

Magadha emerged as the most powerful Mahajanapada in about two hundred years. Its geographical location gave it distinct advantages:

  • Rivers: The Ganga and Son rivers flowed through Magadha. This provided cheap water transport, steady water supplies, and incredibly fertile land for farming.
  • Forests & Elephants: Parts of Magadha were forested. Elephants, which were a crucial part of ancient armies, could be captured and trained. Forests also provided timber for building houses, carts, and chariots.
  • Iron Ore Mines: Iron mines in the region could be tapped to make strong tools for agriculture and powerful weapons for the army.
Fact

Magadha had two deeply ambitious and powerful rulers, Bimbisara and Ajatashatru. They used all means possible to conquer other janapadas. Mahapadma Nanda was another important ruler who extended Magadha's control to the northwest.

Important

The Fear of Magadha: When Alexander the Great reached the Beas River, he wanted to march further east. However, his soldiers mutinied. They were terrified because they had heard that the rulers of India (specifically the Nanda Dynasty of Magadha) possessed vast, unbeatable armies of foot soldiers, chariots, and elephants.

6. Case Study: Vajji (A Republic / Gana-Sangha)

While Magadha became a powerful monarchy (ruled by a single king), Vajji, with its capital at Vaishali (Bihar), was under a completely different form of government known as a gana or a sangha.

Understanding Ganas or Sanghas:

In a gana or a sangha, there was not one, but many rulers. Sometimes, even when thousands of men ruled together, each one was recognized as a raja (king). They performed rituals together and made decisions through public discussion and debate in vast assemblies.

  • If attacked by an enemy, they met to discuss exactly what should be done to defend the state.
  • Restrictions: However, women, dasas (slaves), and kammakaras (landless laborers) were strictly not allowed to participate in these democratic assemblies.
  • Both Buddha and Mahavira belonged to ganas/sanghas.

The rajas of powerful kingdoms persistently tried to conquer the sanghas. Nevertheless, these republics managed to survive for a very long time, until the last of the ganas were finally conquered by the Gupta rulers around 1500 years ago.
