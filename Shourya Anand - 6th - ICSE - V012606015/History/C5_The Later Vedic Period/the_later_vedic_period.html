The Later Vedic Period

1. Introduction

Between 1000 BCE and 600 BCE, significant changes took place in the lifestyle, beliefs, and political structures of the Aryans. This era is known as the Later Vedic Period.

  • The Aryans expanded eastwards from their earlier settlements and moved deep into the Gangetic plain.
  • Some groups even crossed the Vindhya mountains and ventured into South India.
Important

During this period, vital historical and religious texts were composed, providing historians with archaeological evidence of the time. These include the remaining three Vedas (Samaveda, Yajurveda, and Atharvaveda), the Upanishads, the Puranas, and the great epics—the Ramayana and the Mahabharata.

2. Political Structure and Expansion

As the Aryans expanded, tribal units grew and consolidated their power, leading to major changes in administration.

  • Janapadas to Mahajanapadas: Small kingdoms known as janapadas combined and conquered adjacent territories to form larger, independent states called mahajanapadas. By the 6th century BCE, there were sixteen prominent mahajanapadas.
  • Powerful Kingdoms: The four most powerful kingdoms of this era were Kosala, Vatsa, Avanti, and Magadha. Eventually, Magadha established dominance over the Gangetic plain.

Rise of the Absolute Ruler

The position of the King (Rajan) transitioned into an absolute ruler. Kings assumed grand titles such as maharajadhiraja and samrat, signifying supreme authority.

Concept

Ashvamedha Sacrifice: Kings demonstrated their dominance by performing grand sacrifices like the rajasuya and the ashvamedha (horse sacrifice). A royal horse was allowed to roam freely for a year; territories traversed by the horse were claimed by the king. If rival leaders captured the horse, a war would break out.

Administration and Assemblies

The King was assisted by a network of officials to manage the expanding territories:

  • Rajpurohita: Advisor and ritual specialist.
  • Senani: The military commander responsible for the kingdom's expansion.
  • Sangrihitri: The tax collector, managing the kingdom's revenue.

The earlier democratic assemblies—the sabha and samiti—lost their inclusive nature. They became dominated by upper classes, brahmins, and nobles, entirely excluding women and common people.

3. Social Life and the Caste System

The society during the Later Vedic Period grew much more rigid and structured.

  • The Varna (caste) system transformed from being occupation-based to strictly hereditary. Social status and profession were decided solely by birth, ignoring individual skill or merit.
  • Inter-caste marriages were strictly forbidden.
  • Brahmins held the absolute highest position due to their knowledge, learning, and exclusive ability to perform rituals. They wielded immense power, advising kings and demanding respect from the commoners.

4. The Position of Women

A notable shift in this era was the severe decline in the status of women.

  • Women were barred from studying Vedic literature and participating in public assemblies.
  • They were largely confined to the home and expected only to look after the family.
  • Society preferred sons, and women were stripped of the right to inherit property.
  • Polygamy (having more than one wife) became a common practice among princes and the wealthy class.

5. Religion and Beliefs

The religious focus shifted away from the Early Vedic Nature gods (like Indra and Agni) toward a new holy trinity:

  • Prajapati (Brahma): The Creator.
  • Vishnu: The Preserver.
  • Shiva: The Destroyer.

Religion became highly ritualistic. Lavish gifts to brahmins and elaborate community sacrifices organized for births, marriages, and funerals increased the dominance of the priests. However, by the end of this period, people began to criticize these complex and expensive rituals.

6. Economic Conditions

The Later Vedic economy was prosperous and rapidly evolving.

  • Agriculture was the primary occupation. Key crops included wheat, rice, vegetables, cotton, and oilseeds. Taxes levied on these crops provided revenue to the king.
  • The discovery and widespread use of iron revolutionized agriculture and warfare, leading to stronger tools and weapons.
  • Crafts such as weaving, pottery, carpentry, and leatherwork thrived. Artisans formed guilds to fix prices and regulate standards. Over time, these professions also became hereditary, leading to the creation of sub-castes.
Fact

Economic complexity increased as the ancient barter system was slowly replaced. Indians invented their earliest coinage between the 6th and 5th century BCE. These were pieces of silver of a specific weight, known as punch-marked coins, which bore symbols of animals, trees, and hills.

7. Education and the Ashrama System

The Aryans followed the Gurukul system of education. The student (brahmachari) lived in the house of the teacher (guru) and assisted with household tasks in exchange for knowledge. Education consisted of transmitting Vedic literature orally. It was restricted only to the three higher castes, entirely excluding the shudras.

The Four Stages of Life (Ashramas)

An individual's lifespan was ideally divided into four phases of 25 years each:

  • Brahmacharya Ashrama: The first 25 years dedicated to education, discipline, and living with the guru.
  • Grihastha Ashrama: The next 25 years meant for matrimony, family life, and fulfilling the duties of a householder.
  • Vanaprastha Ashrama: The third stage meant for meditation, detachment, and living the life of a hermit in the forest.
  • Sanyasa Ashrama: The final stage representing complete renunciation (giving up) of all worldly ties and earthly pleasures to seek salvation.
