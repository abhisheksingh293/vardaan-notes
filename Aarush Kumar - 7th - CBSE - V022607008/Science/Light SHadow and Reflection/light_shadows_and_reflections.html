Light: Shadows and Reflections

1. Sources of Light

Light is an invisible form of energy that is essential to see objects around us. Any object that gives out light is called a source of light. They are broadly classified into two categories:

  • Natural Sources: Sources of light that occur naturally in the environment (e.g., Sun, stars, fireflies, glow-worms).
  • Man-made Sources: Artificial sources created by humans (e.g., electric bulbs, candles, torches).

Luminous and Non-Luminous Objects

Based on their ability to emit light, objects can be classified into:

  • Luminous Objects: Objects that emit their own light (e.g., the Sun, stars, burning candles).
  • Non-luminous Objects: Objects that do not emit their own light. They become visible only when light from a luminous source falls on them and reflects into our eyes (e.g., tables, chairs, the Moon).

2. Transparent, Translucent, and Opaque Objects

Objects are classified into three types based on the amount of light that can pass through them:

  • Transparent Objects: Allow light to pass through them completely, allowing us to see through them clearly (e.g., clear water, clean glass, air).
  • Translucent Objects: Allow only a partial amount of light to pass through them. We can see through them, but not clearly (e.g., muddy water, butter paper, frosted glass, tissue paper).
  • Opaque Objects: Do not allow any light to pass through them at all. We cannot see through them (e.g., wood, metals, brick walls).
Concept

Rectilinear Propagation of Light: The property of light to travel in a straight line is called rectilinear propagation of light. This property explains the formation of shadows and how images are formed in a pinhole camera.

3. Shadows

A shadow is a dark patch formed when the path of light is obstructed by an opaque object.

Important

Three essential conditions for a shadow to be formed:

  • A source of light.
  • An opaque object to obstruct the light.
  • A screen (like a wall or the ground) on which the shadow is formed.

Characteristics of Shadows

  • A shadow is always black in colour, regardless of the colour of the object or the colour of the light used.
  • A shadow is always formed on the opposite side (behind) the opaque object.
  • A shadow only shows the shape or outline of the object and provides no inner details.
  • The size of the shadow varies depending on the distance between the object, the light source, and the screen. The shadow becomes larger when the object is moved closer to the source of light.
Fact

Shadow Puppetry has been a part of Indian cultural heritage for centuries. Puppeteers use flat cut-out opaque figures placed between a light source and a screen to create lifelike movements. Styles include Tholu Bommalata from Andhra Pradesh.

4. Mirrors and Reflections

We see our image in a mirror due to the reflection of light. Reflection of light is the process of bouncing back of the light rays that fall on the surface of an object.

Characteristics of the Image formed by a Plane Mirror

  • The image is always of the same size as the object.
  • The image is of the same colour as the object.
  • The image is erect (upright) and not upside down.
  • The image is laterally inverted.
Concept

Lateral Inversion: The phenomenon in which the left of the object appears as the right of the image, and the right of the object appears as the left of the image (a left-right reversal).

Important

Differences between a Shadow and an Image:

  • A shadow is always black, while an image has the same colours as the original object.
  • A shadow gives no details of the object (only the outline), while an image gives all the details.
  • A shadow is not laterally inverted, whereas an image formed by a plane mirror is laterally inverted.

5. Optical Instruments

Pinhole Camera

A pinhole camera is a simple device that demonstrates how light travels in straight lines. When light from an object passes through a tiny hole in a box, it goes straight and forms an image on the opposite side. Because light travels in straight lines, rays from the top and bottom of the object cross at the pinhole, resulting in an inverted image.

Periscope

A periscope is an optical instrument that uses mirrors placed in a Z-shaped box. It operates on the principle of multiple reflections of light, allowing an observer to see objects that are not in direct line of sight (e.g., used in submarines or bunkers).

Kaleidoscope

A kaleidoscope is a cylindrical optical instrument containing two or more mirrors angled toward each other. It works on the principle of multiple reflections. When loose, coloured objects inside reflect off the mirrors, it creates beautiful symmetrical patterns.
