MEASUREMENT OF TIME AND MOTION

1. The Measurement of Time

In our daily lives, accurate measurement of time is essential. We use clocks and watches to know exact times for school, meals, and other activities.

Tracking Time in Olden Days

Before clocks were invented, humans observed natural events that repeated at regular intervals to track time. These events included:

  • The interval between sunrise and sunset, or the next sunrise.
  • The time from one full moon to the next full moon.
  • The transition from one season to another season.
Fact These natural time intervals could not be used as standard SI units because they vary from place to place and season to season.

2. Ancient Time-Measuring Devices

Over time, early humans developed various devices to measure smaller intervals of time more accurately.

  • Sundial: Works on the principle that the position and length of a shadow cast by an object change with the position of the sun in the sky (e.g., the Jantar Mantar in New Delhi).
  • Water Clock (Ghatika yantra): Measures time by the steady flow of water either into or out of a vessel that has markings on it.
  • Sand Clock (Hourglass): Operates on the principle that a specific amount of sand takes a constant, exact time to fall from an upper chamber to a lower chamber.
  • Candle Clock: Uses the gradual and uniform burning of a marked candle to indicate the passage of time.
Concept A Pendulum Clock uses a swinging pendulum to measure time accurately. Galileo Galilei discovered that a swinging pendulum takes a fixed amount of time to complete one oscillation.

3. The Simple Pendulum

A simple pendulum consists of a small metallic ball called a bob, suspended from a rigid stand by a thread.

Important Terms Related to a Pendulum:

  • Mean (Rest) Position: The central, resting position of the pendulum when it is not moving.
  • Extreme Positions: The points where the bob reaches its maximum distance on either side from the mean position.
  • Oscillation: One complete to-and-fro movement of the pendulum (e.g., from one extreme position to the other and back again).
  • Amplitude: The maximum displacement of the bob from its mean position.
  • Time Period (T): The time taken by the bob to complete one full oscillation.
Important The Time period of a simple pendulum of a given length remains constant at a specific place. Measuring short intervals of time is often done using a stopwatch.

4. Units of Time

Different units of time are used depending on the requirement (e.g., age is in years, daily activities in hours or minutes).

  • The standard SI Unit of Time is the second (s).
  • Larger units include minutes (min) and hours (h).
  • 1 minute = 60 seconds.
  • 1 hour = 60 minutes.
  • 1 day = 24 hours (or 86,400 seconds).

5. Rest and Motion are Relative

An object is in motion if it changes its position with time relative to its surroundings. It is at rest if it does not change its position.

Rest and motion are relative concepts. For example, a girl sitting in a moving car is at rest with respect to the car and its passengers, but she is in motion with respect to the trees and buildings outside.

6. Speed and its Measurement

To determine if an object is moving fast or slow, we must consider the distance travelled and the time taken.

  • Speed: The total distance covered by an object in a unit of time. Fast objects have a higher speed.
  • Formula: Speed = Total distance covered ÷ Total time taken.
  • SI Unit of Speed: metre per second (m/s). Other units include km/h (for vehicles) or cm/s.
Concept Because vehicles speed up and slow down during a journey, we calculate the Average Speed (Total Distance ÷ Total Time).

Devices in Vehicles:

  • Speedometer: An instrument on a vehicle's dashboard that indicates the current speed in km/h.
  • Odometer: A device that measures and displays the total distance travelled by the vehicle in kilometres.

7. Uniform and Non-Uniform Linear Motion

  • Uniform Linear Motion: When a body moves in a straight line and covers equal distances in equal intervals of time (constant speed).
  • Non-Uniform Linear Motion: When a body moves in a straight line but covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time (speed keeps changing).

8. Distance-Time Graph

A distance-time graph visually represents how the distance travelled by an object changes with time. Time is plotted on the horizontal (X-axis) and distance on the vertical (Y-axis).

  • At Rest: The graph is a straight line perfectly parallel to the X-axis (time axis).
  • Uniform Motion: The graph is a straight, inclined line starting from the origin.
  • Non-Uniform Motion: The graph appears as a rising curve, showing speed changes over time.
Important Always ensure symbols for units are written in lowercase (e.g., 's' not 'sec', 'h' not 'hrs') and without a full stop unless at the end of a sentence.
