The Colonial Era in India

1. Understanding Colonialism

Concept

Colonialism is a process where one powerful group, kingdom, state, or country dominates and subjugates another. It involves establishing control over land, labour, and resources, often displacing indigenous communities.

Scholars distinguish between two primary forms of colonialism:

  • Settler Colonialism: The colonizing power seizes land and settles permanently, often displacing or marginalizing the original inhabitants. Control over land is central.
  • Resource Colonialism: Settlement is not the primary goal. The colonizing power dominates the region primarily to extract wealth, raw materials, and labour to send back to their homeland (the metropole).

2. Europeans in India

From the fifteenth century onwards, European nations sought new sea routes to Asia for profitable trade, especially in spices, cotton, and silk. After Constantinople fell to the Ottoman Turks, overland routes were blocked, prompting maritime exploration.

The Portuguese

  • Vasco da Gama reached Calicut (now Kozhikode) in May 1498.
  • To assert dominance, the Portuguese introduced the cartaz system, requiring all ships in the Indian Ocean to carry a written permit issued by them.
  • They established fortified trading posts in Calicut, Cannanore, Cochin, and captured Goa in 1510.

The Dutch and the French

  • The Dutch: Arrived in the early 17th century. They formed the East India Company of the Netherlands, capturing territories from the Portuguese. They suffered a major defeat at the Battle of Colachel (1741) by the kingdom of Travancore.
  • The French: The French East India Company was created in 1664 by finance minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert. Pondicherry (acquired 1673-74) became their headquarters. Governor-General Joseph Francois Dupleix played a key role in expanding French influence.

The English

  • In 1600, Queen Elizabeth I granted a charter to a group of merchants (the English East India Company), giving them exclusive rights to trade with the East.
  • In 1615, Sir Thomas Roe visited Emperor Jahangir's court and secured vital trading concessions.
Fact

The intense rivalry for trade monopolies between the English and French led to a series of military conflicts known as the Carnatic Wars (1744-1763). The English eventually emerged victorious.

3. British Expansion and Consolidation

Taking advantage of the weakening Mughal Empire after Aurangzeb's death, the English East India Company transitioned from traders to rulers.

Key Battles for Bengal

  • Battle of Plassey (1757): British forces led by Robert Clive defeated Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah. This marked the first major step in British political control.
  • Battle of Buxar (1764): The British defeated the combined armies of Mir Qasim, the Nawab of Awadh, and Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II. The Company was granted the Diwani (right to collect revenue) of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa.

Policies of Expansion

Important
  • Subsidiary Alliance (Lord Wellesley): Indian rulers had to disband their armies, maintain a British subsidiary force at their own cost, and accept a British Resident at their court. This severely compromised their independence.
  • Doctrine of Lapse (Lord Dalhousie): If a ruler of a subsidiary state died without a natural male heir, the territory would "lapse" or be annexed by the British (e.g., Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur).

4. Economic Impact of Colonial Rule

The British transformed India into a supplier of cheap raw materials and a vast market for British manufactured goods. This caused a massive drain of wealth.

Ruin of Indian Textiles

  • The Industrial Revolution allowed Britain to mass-produce cheap machine-made cloth, devastating India's hand-woven textile industry.
  • The British imposed a policy of one-way free trade: British goods entered India duty-free, while Indian exports to England faced exorbitant tariffs.

Impact on Rural Economy

  • New land revenue policies like the Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari, and Mahalwari systems forced peasants to pay high taxes in cash, leading to widespread indebtedness to moneylenders.
  • Farmers were forced to cultivate cash crops (indigo, jute, cotton) instead of food grains, directly contributing to devastating man-made famines, such as the Great Bengal Famine (1770-1772).

5. Education Under the British

Initially, education in India was flexible with no fixed curriculum (Pathshalas and Madrassas). The Charter Act of 1813 allocated funds for education, sparking a debate between Orientalists (favouring traditional Indian learning) and Anglicists (led by Lord Macaulay, favouring Western education in English).

Wood's Despatch (1854)

  • Emphasized Western learning to create a loyal workforce of English-speaking clerks (often called "babus") to serve the administration at lower wages.
  • Flaws: It severely neglected mass primary education, girls' education, and scientific/technical training.

6. Resistance Movements

Exploitative British policies sparked numerous uprisings long before 1857.

  • Sannyasi-Fakir Rebellion: Wandering ascetics and peasants in Bengal rebelled against restrictions on movement and alms collection, exacerbated by the 1770 famine.
  • Tribal Revolts: Tribals lost their lands and forest rights to new laws. Notable uprisings included the Bhil, Kol, and the Santhal Rebellion (1855-56) led by Sidhu and Kanhu.
  • Indigo Rebellion (1859-60): Bengal peasants refused to grow indigo under coercive contracts, forcing the government to set up the Indigo Commission.

7. The Great Rebellion of 1857

The biggest challenge to British rule culminated in 1857, driven by grievances across all sections of society.

Causes of the Rebellion

  • Economic: High revenue demands ruining peasants; artisans destroyed by cheap British goods.
  • Political: Resentment over the Doctrine of Lapse and the unjust annexation of Awadh (1856).
  • Military: Indian sepoys were paid less, denied promotions above subedar, and forced to travel overseas (which offended caste beliefs).
  • Immediate Cause: The introduction of the Enfield rifle. Rumors spread that the greased cartridge wrappers, which had to be bitten off, were made from cow and pig fat, deeply offending Hindu and Muslim sepoys.

Spread and Suppression

  • Mangal Pandey was the first to protest at Barrackpore. The revolt officially broke out in Meerut on May 10, 1857.
  • Key Leaders: Bahadur Shah Zafar (Delhi), Rani Lakshmi Bai (Jhansi), Nana Sahib and Tatya Tope (Kanpur), Begum Hazrat Mahal (Lucknow), and Kunwar Singh (Arrah).
  • The British ultimately suppressed the rebellion using immense military force and terror tactics. The revolt failed primarily because it did not spread across the entire country and lacked unified leadership.
Important

Aftermath (Act of 1858): The British Parliament ended the rule of the East India Company, transferring power directly to the British Crown. The Governor-General was given the title of Viceroy, and a policy of 'divide and rule' was actively promoted to prevent future unity.
