The Rise of the Marathas

1. Medieval Deccan and Maharashtra

The Deccan Plateau served as a strategic bridge between northern and southern India. In the medieval period, Maharashtra referred to the region where Marathi was the dominant language. It was divided into three distinct subregions:

  • The Konkan: A narrow coastal strip with important ports linking Maharashtra to Indian Ocean trade networks.
  • The Ghats: Steep hills rising from the Konkan containing numerous highly fortified hilltops crucial for military defense.
  • The Desh: A broad, fertile plateau to the east, rich in agriculture and densely populated, containing major pilgrimage sites.

The Bhakti Movement

The Bhakti Movement played a vital role in creating a shared cultural identity that cut across rigid caste boundaries, laying the ideological groundwork for the rise of the Marathas.

  • Sant Jnaneshwar: Composed a commentary on the Bhagavad Gita in Marathi (the language of the common people) rather than Sanskrit.
  • Sant Tukaram: Composed devotional poems known as abhangs, heavily criticizing social inequalities.
  • Warkaris: Devotees who undertook the annual pilgrimage to Pandharpur to worship Vithoba.

2. The Rise of Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj was born in 1630 at the Shivneri Fort to Shahji Bhonsle and Jijabai. Growing up under the guidance of his mother and his guardian, Dadoji Konddeo, he received training in administration and warfare, and absorbed the values of duty and justice from the epics and Bhakti saints.

Early Campaigns and Expansion

Realizing the strategic importance of hill forts, Shivaji captured forts like Torna, Rajgad, and Kondana using brilliant guerrilla warfare tactics between 1645 and 1647.

Fact The Afzal Khan Incident (1659): The Sultan of Bijapur sent general Afzal Khan to crush Shivaji. Suspecting a trap during their peace meeting, Shivaji wore hidden armor and concealed a wagh nakh (tiger claw) in his hand. When Afzal Khan tried to stab him, Shivaji counter-attacked and killed the general, routing the Bijapuri army.

Conflict with the Mughals

Emperor Aurangzeb viewed Shivaji as a massive threat and sent military campaigns to subdue him:

  • Shaista Khan (1663): Shivaji launched a daring night raid into the Mughal camp in Pune, wounding Shaista Khan and damaging Mughal prestige.
  • Attack on Surat (1664): Shivaji attacked the wealthy Mughal port of Surat to fund his growing kingdom.
  • Escape from Agra: Following the Treaty of Purandhar, Shivaji visited Aurangzeb's court in Agra. Humiliated and placed under house arrest, he famously escaped by hiding in baskets of sweets and fruits.
Important Treaty of Purandhar (1665): Aurangzeb sent his best general, Raja Jai Singh, who successfully besieged Purandhar Fort. Forced to negotiate, Shivaji signed this treaty, surrendering 23 forts to the Mughals and accepting Aurangzeb as his overlord.

Coronation and Later Expeditions

In 1674, Shivaji formally crowned himself king in a grand Vedic ceremony at the Raigad Fort, adopting the title Chhatrapati. This asserted full Maratha independence. In 1676, he launched a massive southern expedition into Karnataka, capturing strategic forts like Gingee and securing the region. Shivaji died in 1680, leaving behind a visionary and highly organized empire.

3. The Maratha Administration

Shivaji was heavily influenced by the Bhakti saint Samarth Ramdas, who believed political power was necessary to uphold dharma (righteous order). Shivaji governed not just as a conqueror, but for the welfare of his people.

Concept The Ashtapradhan: This was a specialized council of eight ministers created by Shivaji to advise him on various administrative, military, and religious matters, ensuring efficient and just governance.

Economy and Trade: Maratha territories generated revenue through taxes like Chauth and Sardeshmukhi. The state protected coastal trade at ports like Kalyan and Chaul. They constructed powerful coastal forts (e.g., Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg) and maintained a strong navy to combat piracy and secure maritime trade.

4. The Marathas After Shivaji

  • Chhatrapati Sambhaji (1680-1689): Shivaji's elder son faced continuous wars against the Siddis, Mysore, and the Mughals. He was eventually captured and executed by Aurangzeb in 1689.
  • Chhatrapati Rajaram I: Escaped to the southern fort of Gingee to continue the resistance. After his death in 1700, his wife Tarabai assumed leadership as regent, defending territories and expanding raids into Malwa and Gujarat.

5. The Rise of the Peshwas

Following Aurangzeb's death in 1707, Chhatrapati Shahuji was released. He appointed Balaji Vishwanath as his Peshwa (Prime Minister). Over time, the office of the Peshwa became hereditary, and they became the real decision-makers of the Maratha Empire.

  • Balaji Vishwanath (1713-1720): Forced the Mughals to return Shivaji's original territories and secured the right to collect taxes in six Deccan provinces.
  • Baji Rao I (1720-1740): Known as the "Fighting Peshwa," he rapidly expanded Maratha influence into Malwa, Gujarat, and parts of Rajasthan.
  • Balaji Baji Rao (1740-1761): Shifted the Maratha capital to Pune. Under his rule, the Maratha Empire reached its peak geographical extent.

The Maratha Confederacy

The vast empire evolved into a confederacy managed by powerful regional families loyal to the Peshwa:

  • Scindias: Dominated Malwa (Gwalior).
  • Holkars: Ruled Central India (Indore).
  • Gaekwads: Controlled Gujarat (Baroda).
  • Bhosles: Governed the region of Nagpur.

6. Cultural Developments

The Maratha era sparked a cultural renaissance in Maharashtra and beyond. The Marathi language flourished, and historical chronicles called Bakhars (like the Sabhasad Bakhar detailing Shivaji's achievements) were written.

Thanjavur - A Case Study

In 1676, Ekoji I (Shivaji's half-brother) established Maratha rule in Thanjavur (Tamil Nadu), which lasted for 150 years. This era is renowned for its cultural fusion:

  • Architecture: The Saraswati Mahal library and the imposing Manora Fort built by King Serfoji II.
  • Music: Became a hub for Carnatic music, hosting the famous "Trinity" (Syama Sastri, Tyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar).
  • Dance: Patronized Dasiattam, heavily shaping the modern classical dance form Bharatanatyam.
  • Painting: Evolved the intricate Thanjavur Paintings, known for depictions of deities (especially Lord Krishna) adorned with real pearls and gold borders.
