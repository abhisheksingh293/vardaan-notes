World Geography: Some Glimpses

1. Map Projections

A map is a representation of the Earth drawn on a flat surface. Because the Earth is spherical, drawing it perfectly on a flat surface is impossible without some distortion. To solve this problem, different methods using latitudes and longitudes were developed.

Concept

Map Projection: The method of drawing maps from a curved surface onto a flat surface using latitudes and longitudes. The study of map making is called cartography.

Three major types of map projections are used to minimize distortion:

  • Cylindrical Projection: The surface of the globe is transferred onto a paper cylinder that touches the Equator. It is best for mapping the equatorial region (e.g., Mercator's projection).
  • Conical Projection: A paper cone rests on top of the globe, touching it at a standard parallel. It is used to map mid-latitude regions.
  • Azimuthal Projection: The surface of the globe is transferred onto a flat plane touching a central point (usually a pole). It is primarily used to map polar regions.

2. Oceans and Submarine Landforms

The Earth is known as the blue planet or watery planet because about 71% of its surface is covered by water (the hydrosphere). About 97% of this water is stored in oceans.

Submarine Landforms

The ocean floor is not flat; it contains various landforms submerged beneath the water, known as submarine landforms.

  • Continental Shelf: A gently sloping surface extending from the coasts towards the open sea. It provides most of the world's marine food.
  • Continental Slope: A zone of steep slope extending from the continental shelf to the deep-sea plains. Submarine canyons and trenches are found here.
  • Deep-Sea Plains (Abyssal Plains): Vast, mostly featureless plains that sometimes contain underwater ridges and volcanic peaks. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is an example of an underwater mountain range.
  • Ocean Deeps and Trenches: Deep depressions on the ocean floor.
Fact

The Mariana Trench in the Pacific Ocean is the deepest oceanic trench on Earth. Its deepest point, the Challenger Deep, is more than 11,000 meters below sea level. If Mount Everest were placed inside it, its peak would still be submerged by over 1.6 km!

Coral Reefs

Coral reefs are formed by the secretions (calcium carbonate) of billions of tiny marine animals called coral polyps in warm, shallow tropical waters.

Important

The Great Barrier Reef, located off the north-east coast of Australia, is the world's largest coral reef. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site but faces severe threats from overfishing, pollution, tourism, and climate change.

3. Ocean Currents

An ocean current is the movement of ocean water in the form of a stream, in a definite direction, on the ocean surface. Broad and slow-moving currents are called drifts.

Ocean currents develop due to:

  • Variations in the temperature and density of ocean water.
  • The direction of prevailing winds (which largely controls their movement).
  • The rotation of the Earth.
  • The shape and position of the coastlines.

Ocean currents are classified as warm currents (flowing from the equator to the poles) and cold currents (flowing from the poles to the equator). The general circulation is clockwise in the northern hemisphere and anti-clockwise in the southern hemisphere.

4. Smaller Water Bodies and Waterways

Water bodies smaller than oceans play crucial roles in transport, trade, and travel.

  • Seas: Large bodies of salt water that are parts of an ocean. They can be open (Arabian Sea), partly enclosed (Mediterranean Sea), or completely landlocked (Caspian Sea).
  • Gulfs & Bays: Gulfs are highly indented inlets (e.g., Persian Gulf), while bays are curved indentations with wide openings (e.g., Bay of Bengal).
Concept

Straits are narrow stretches of water that join two large water bodies or separate two large landmasses (e.g., the Strait of Gibraltar). Canals are human-made waterways used for irrigation or shipping (e.g., the Suez Canal and Panama Canal).

5. The Continents

The solid outer layer of the Earth consisting of soil, rocks, and minerals is called the lithosphere. The Earth has seven continents.

Asia

  • The Tibetan Plateau: The largest and highest intermontane plateau, known as the "Roof of the World".
  • The Gobi Desert: A cold desert spreading across Mongolia and northern China, experiencing extreme temperature variations.
  • The Eurasian Steppes: Temperate grasslands found in Mongolia and eastern Europe/Asia.
  • The Ural Mountains: One of the oldest mountain ranges, serving as the northern boundary between Asia and Europe.

Europe

Often called the "peninsula of peninsulas." Key features include the Alps (highest mountain range in Europe; highest peak is Mont Blanc) and the Great European Plain, a vast, fertile plain drained by navigable rivers like the Rhine and Danube.

Africa

The second-largest continent, characterized by extensive hot deserts and a huge plateau.

  • The Sahara Desert: The largest hot desert in the world.
  • The Savanna: Tropical grasslands with scattered short trees, home to diverse wildlife.
  • The Nile: The longest river in the world, formed by the merger of the White Nile and Blue Nile.

South America

  • The Andes Mountains: The second-highest and longest mountain range on land.
  • The Amazon River & Rainforest: The Amazon is the largest river by water volume. It flows through the dense equatorial rainforests known as the selvas.
  • The Atacama Desert: A cold desert in Chile, known as one of the driest places on Earth.

North America

  • The Rocky Mountains: The highest mountain range in the western cordillera.
  • The Colorado Plateau: Known for deep valleys like the Grand Canyon.
  • The Great Lakes: A chain of five lakes (Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie, and Ontario). Lake Superior is the world's largest freshwater lake.
  • The Appalachian Mountains: Old, eroded eastern highlands.

Australia and Antarctica

  • Australia: The smallest continent. The Great Australian Desert covers a massive area, made up of smaller deserts like the Great Victoria Desert.
  • Antarctica: The "white continent" covered in a vast ice sheet. The Transantarctic Mountains divide the continent into East and West Antarctica.
