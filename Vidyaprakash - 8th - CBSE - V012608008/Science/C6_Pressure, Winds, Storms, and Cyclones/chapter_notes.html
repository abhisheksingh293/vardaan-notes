PRESSURE, WINDS, STORMS, AND CYCLONES

1. Pressure and Its Factors

The effect of a force depends on the area over which it acts. For example, pushing a thumb tack into a board is easier than pushing a flat bottle cap because the tack has a smaller area, concentrating the force.

Concept

Pressure is defined as the physical quantity used to measure the effect of force on a surface. It is the force applied per unit area.

  • Formula: Pressure (P) = Force (F) / Area (A)
  • SI Unit: Newton per square metre (N/m²), also called Pascal (Pa).
  • Meteorology Units: Millibar (mb) and hectopascal (hPa).

Factors Affecting Pressure

  • Magnitude of Force: Greater force applied on a given area increases the pressure.
  • Area of Contact: If the same force is applied over a larger area, pressure decreases. If applied over a smaller area, pressure increases.
Real-life Examples
  • School Bags: Broad straps spread the weight over a larger area, reducing pressure on shoulders.
  • Cutting Tools: Knives have sharp edges (small area) to exert high pressure and cut easily.
  • Camels: Broad feet spread their weight over the sand, preventing them from sinking.
  • Tractors: Broad tyres distribute heavy loads over soft fields, preventing sinking.

2. Pressure Exerted by Liquids

Liquids also exert pressure on the walls and bottom of their container due to their weight. This pressure acts in all directions.

Factors Affecting Liquid Pressure

  • Depth: Pressure increases with depth. The deeper you go, the greater the pressure because of the weight of the liquid above.
  • Density: Denser liquids exert more pressure than lighter ones at the same depth.
Fact

Dams are built thicker at the bottom because water pressure is vastly higher at greater depths. Overhead tanks are placed at a height so that gravity creates sufficient downward pressure to supply water to all taps.

3. Atmospheric Pressure

The Earth is surrounded by a blanket of air called the atmosphere. The pressure exerted by the weight of this air on the Earth's surface is known as Atmospheric Pressure.

Factors Affecting Atmospheric Pressure

  • Altitude: Pressure decreases as altitude increases (air gets thinner).
  • Temperature: Warm air expands and rises, creating low pressure. Cold air contracts and sinks, creating high pressure.
  • Humidity: Moist air is lighter than dry air, so increased humidity lowers pressure.
Concept

Measuring Instruments:

  • Manometer: A U-shaped tube used to measure the pressure of liquids or confined gases.
  • Barometer: An instrument used to measure atmospheric pressure, crucial for weather forecasting.
Important

The internal pressure of fluids and gases inside the human body perfectly balances the external atmospheric pressure. However, at high altitudes where external pressure drops suddenly, blood vessels can burst, leading to high altitude nosebleeds.

4. Wind and Its Formation

Moving air is called wind. Wind is always formed due to the uneven heating of the Earth's surface by the Sun. Air always moves from a region of high pressure to a region of low pressure.

Types of Coastal Winds

  • Sea Breeze (Daytime): Land heats up faster than water. Air above land rises (low pressure), and cooler, heavier air from the sea rushes in to replace it.
  • Land Breeze (Nighttime): Land cools down faster than water. Air above the sea is warmer and rises, causing cooler air from the land to blow towards the sea.
  • Monsoon Winds: Large-scale seasonal winds driven by uneven heating of land and ocean, bringing heavy rainfall crucial for agriculture.
Key Principle

High-speed winds result in lowering of air pressure. If powerful winds blow over a poorly built roof, the low pressure created above the roof allows the higher pressure inside the house to push the roof upward, sometimes blowing it completely off.

5. Storms, Lightning, and Thunderstorms

A storm is a violent atmospheric disturbance marked by strong winds, heavy precipitation, and sometimes thunder and lightning.

  • Formation: Warm, moist air rises rapidly (updrafts). As it cools, water vapor condenses into clouds. Falling rain creates downward currents (downdrafts). This constant, violent motion creates static electrical charges.
  • Lightning: The massive discharge of electrical energy between positively and negatively charged regions in clouds, or between clouds and the ground.
  • Thunder: The extreme heat from lightning expands the surrounding air instantly, creating a loud booming sound.
Safety

Lightning Conductor: A device featuring a metal rod and copper plate installed on tall buildings to provide a safe path for electrical discharges straight into the ground.

During a storm: Never stand under isolated trees, stay away from water, disconnect electrical appliances, and avoid open fields.

6. Cyclones and Tornadoes

Cyclones

A cyclone is a huge rotating weather system that forms over warm oceans, featuring a very low-pressure center.

  • The Eye: The calm, clear, cloud-free center of the cyclone.
  • The Eye Wall: The ring of towering thunderstorms surrounding the eye, containing the most violent winds and heaviest rainfall.
  • Effects: Cyclones cause immense destruction through storm surges (flooding coastal areas), torrential rains, and winds that destroy infrastructure and crops.

Tornadoes

A tornado is a violent, dark, funnel-shaped cloud that stretches from a thunderstorm down to the ground. Though smaller in area than a cyclone, its rotating winds are incredibly fast and destructive, capable of lifting vehicles and tearing apart houses.

Emergency Preparedness

During a tornado warning, immediately take shelter in an underground bunker or an inner, windowless room (like a closet or bathroom) on the lowest floor. Shield your head and neck with your arms.
