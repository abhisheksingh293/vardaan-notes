MATTER

1. Introduction to Matter

Concept

Matter is defined as anything that occupies space and has mass. All materials around us, living and non-living, are made of matter.

Matter can be classified based on its physical state into three main forms:

  • Solids (e.g., wood, metal, chalk)
  • Liquids (e.g., water, oil, milk)
  • Gases (e.g., oxygen, carbon dioxide, air)
Fact

Air and Water are Matter: We can prove air is matter because an inflated balloon occupies more space and has more mass than a deflated one. Similarly, a bottle filled with water feels heavier than an empty one, proving water has mass and occupies space.

2. Properties of Particles of Matter

All matter is composed of very tiny particles called molecules. These particles possess specific characteristics:

  • Continuous Movement: Particles are never at rest; they move continuously. This means they possess kinetic energy (energy of motion).
  • Intermolecular Force of Attraction: The particles of matter attract each other with a force that holds them together.
  • Intermolecular Space: There is always empty space between the particles of matter. When salt dissolves in water, the salt particles distribute into these intermolecular spaces.
Important

Temperature Effect: The kinetic energy of particles increases with an increase in temperature. For example, when you light an incense stick, the rising temperature increases the particles' kinetic energy, allowing the smell to mix rapidly with the air and travel far.

3. Physical States & Molecular Arrangement

The differences in the properties of solids, liquids, and gases arise from how their molecules are arranged:

A. Solids

  • Molecules are very closely packed.
  • They have fixed positions and can only vibrate about their fixed positions.
  • Intermolecular forces of attraction are very strong.
  • Intermolecular space is negligible.
  • Possess very little kinetic energy.

B. Liquids

  • Molecules are not very closely packed.
  • Their position is not fixed; they can move around easily.
  • Intermolecular forces are weaker than in solids.
  • Intermolecular space is greater than in solids.
  • Possess more kinetic energy compared to solids.

C. Gases

  • Molecules are very loosely packed.
  • They move around freely in all directions.
  • Intermolecular forces of attraction are negligible.
  • Intermolecular space is at its maximum.
  • Possess maximum kinetic energy.

4. Comparative Properties of Matter

Based on their molecular arrangements, the three states show highly distinct physical properties:

  • Shape: Solids have a definite shape. Liquids have no fixed shape and take the shape of their container. Gases also have no definite shape.
  • Volume: Solids and liquids both have a definite volume. Gases do not have a fixed volume and fill any container completely.
  • Ability to Flow: Solids are rigid and cannot flow. Liquids and gases can flow easily.
  • Compressibility: Solids generally cannot be compressed. Liquids can be compressed slightly. Gases can be compressed easily due to maximum intermolecular space.

5. Diffusion

Concept

Diffusion is the process of intermixing of particles of two different types of matter on their own. For example, when a drop of ink is added to water, the particles of ink get into the spaces between the water molecules and spread evenly.

6. Change of State

Matter can be changed from one physical state to another. This change can be brought about by two main factors:

A. By Changing Temperature

  • Heating: Heating a solid (like ice) gives its particles energy. They vibrate faster, overcome the forces of attraction, and the solid changes into a liquid (water). Further heating changes the liquid into a gas (steam).
  • Cooling: Lowering temperature causes particles to lose kinetic energy, move slower, and come closer together, changing a gas to a liquid, or a liquid to a solid.

B. By Applying Pressure

By applying high pressure and lowering the temperature, a gas gets compressed. Its molecules are pushed closer together, intermolecular attraction increases, and the gas liquefies.

7. Physical vs. Chemical Changes

Matter constantly undergoes changes around us. These are categorized into two types:

Physical Changes

  • No new substance is formed.
  • Usually accompanied by a change in shape, size, or physical state.
  • Generally temporary and reversible.
  • Examples: Melting of ice, dissolving sugar in water, stretching a rubber band, tearing paper.

Chemical Changes

  • A new substance with entirely different properties is formed.
  • Often accompanied by color change, release/absorption of heat or light, or evolution of a gas.
  • Generally permanent and irreversible.
  • Examples: Burning of an incense stick (produces ash and smoke), rusting of iron, cooking of food, digestion.
Important

Burning is Always Chemical: Any process involving burning (like burning a candle or a magnesium ribbon) is a chemical change because it inevitably produces new substances such as gases and ash that cannot be reversed into the original material.
