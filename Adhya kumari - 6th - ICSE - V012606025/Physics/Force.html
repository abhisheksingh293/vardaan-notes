FORCE

1. Force: As a Push or a Pull

In our daily life, we constantly push or pull things to make them move or stop. For example, opening a door requires pulling, while kicking a football requires pushing. If you stand in front of a jammed door, it will not open by itself until you apply effort.

Concept

Force is a push or a pull acting on an object which changes or tends to change the state of rest or of uniform motion of an object, or changes its direction, speed, size, or shape. The SI Unit of force is newton (N).

2. Effects of a Force

A force cannot be seen, but its effects can be observed and felt. A force applied on an object may affect it in the following ways:

  • Can cause motion in a stationary object: For example, pushing a stationary heavy box with the help of others to make it move, or pedaling a bicycle.
  • Can stop a moving object: A goalkeeper catching a moving football, or applying brakes to a moving bicycle.
  • Can change the direction of motion: A batsman hitting a cricket ball, or striking a carrom coin where it rebounds off the edge.
  • Can change the speed of a moving object: Pushing a moving bicycle from behind increases its speed, while pulling it from the front decreases its speed.
  • Can change the shape or size of a body: Blowing air into a balloon or compressing a spring.
Fact

All motions are produced by force, but not all forces produce motion. (e.g., You can push a solid wall with all your strength, but it will not move).

Important

When two or more forces act simultaneously on an object, the net force acting on the object is called the Resultant Force. If they act in the same direction, they add up. If they act in opposite directions, the resultant is the difference between the two forces.

3. Types of Forces

Forces are broadly classified into two main groups based on whether the objects are in physical contact or not.

3.1 Contact Forces

A force that acts when two objects are in actual physical contact with each other is called a contact force. Examples include:

  • Muscular force: Force applied by our body muscles (e.g., a horse pulling a cart, lifting a book).
  • Mechanical force: Force generated by a machine.
  • Frictional force: The force that opposes the motion of objects in contact.

3.2 Non-Contact Forces

A force that acts between two objects that are not in actual physical contact is called a non-contact force. They do not require a material medium. Examples include:

  • Gravitational force: The pull exerted by the earth on objects.
  • Magnetic force: The push or pull exerted by a magnet.
  • Electrostatic force: The force exerted by electrically charged bodies.

4. Force of Friction

A ball moving on the ground eventually slows down and stops. This means a force is being exerted by the ground on the ball, opposing its motion. This opposing force is known as Frictional Force or Friction.

Concept

Friction is a natural contact force that comes into action whenever a body moves or tries to move over the surface of another body. It always opposes the relative motion between two surfaces.

Properties of Friction

  • Friction always acts in a direction opposite to the direction of motion.
  • The amount of friction depends on the roughness (or smoothness) of the two surfaces in contact. Rough surfaces have more friction.
  • Friction depends on the mass of the object. The higher the mass, the greater the magnitude of the frictional force.
  • Friction produces heat (e.g., rubbing your palms together).
  • Friction causes wear and tear of the surfaces in contact.

5. Kinds of Friction

Friction is classified into three main kinds based on the state of the objects in contact:

  • Static Friction: The frictional force that comes into play when an object tends to move over a surface but does not actually move (it remains at rest despite the applied force).
  • Sliding Friction: The frictional force exerted by a surface when an object actually slides over it (e.g., a flat block of wood sliding on a table).
  • Rolling Friction: The frictional force exerted by a surface when an object rolls over it without slipping (e.g., wheels rolling on the ground).
Comparison

Rolling friction is much smaller than sliding friction, which in turn is smaller than static friction. This is why heavy bags are fitted with wheels—it transforms sliding friction into rolling friction, making them easier to pull!

6. Friction is a Necessary Evil

Friction brings about wear and tear, wastes energy, and slows things down. However, without it, we could not perform basic daily tasks. Therefore, it is called a "necessary evil."

Advantages of Friction

  • It enables us to walk without slipping by pushing the ground backward.
  • It makes writing on paper with a pen or pencil possible.
  • Vehicles can slow down and stop safely because of the friction between brake shoes and wheel rims.
  • Knitting, weaving, and tying a knot are only possible due to friction.
  • Friction generates heat that burns up meteors entering Earth's atmosphere, saving us from impact.

Disadvantages of Friction

  • It causes wear and tear on shoe soles, vehicle tyres, and machine parts.
  • A lot of fuel/energy is wasted simply in overcoming friction, reducing machine efficiency and vehicle mileage.
  • It produces unwanted heat and noise in machinery, which can cause damage.

7. Methods to Reduce and Increase Friction

Methods to Reduce Friction

  • Using Lubricants: Applying oil or grease to machine parts makes the surfaces smooth.
  • Polishing: Making surfaces (like furniture) extremely smooth.
  • Using Fine Powder: Sprinkling talcum powder on a carrom board acts as a dry lubricant.
  • Using Wheels and Rollers: Fixing wheels (like in suitcases) converts sliding friction into lower rolling friction.
  • Using Ball Bearings: Small metal balls placed between moving machine parts to reduce friction.
  • Streamlining: Objects moving in fluids (air or water) like aeroplanes, ships, cars, and fish are given a special shape to reduce fluid friction.
Concept

Streamlined Shape: A special shape that is narrow in front and gradually broadens, offering the least opposition (minimum friction) to the flow of air or water.

Methods to Increase Friction

  • Tyres of vehicles are provided with deep grooves to prevent skidding on wet roads.
  • Athletes and mountaineers wear special shoes with spikes or studs to prevent slipping.
  • Industrial machine belts have rough surfaces, and sometimes sticky powder is applied so they do not slip off pulleys.
  • Steps of staircases and ramps are made rough to prevent slipping.
  • Grinding stones in flour mills are kept rough for fine grinding of grains.
