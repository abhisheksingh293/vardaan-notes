Cell: Fundamental Unit of Life

1. Introduction to the Cell

All plants and animals are made up of tiny living units known as cells. The cell is essentially an accumulation of living substance, called protoplasm, bounded by an outer membrane (plasma membrane).

  • Protoplasm is a jelly-like, transparent, and granular substance that is the basis of life. It responds to stimuli like light, temperature, and chemicals. If protoplasm is removed, the cell dies.
  • In the nucleus, it is called nucleoplasm. Outside the nucleus, it is called cytoplasm.
Concept

The Cell Theory
Proposed by scientists to explain cellular life, it states:
1. All living organisms are made up of cells.
2. Cells are the basic structural unit of all organisms.
3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells.

2. Size and Shape of Cells

Cells are primarily microscopic, varying in size from 1/10th to 1/1000th of a millimetre. However, their size and shape are closely related to the specific tasks they perform within the organism.

Fact

While most cells are microscopic, eggs are giant cells. The largest single cell in a living organism is the egg of an ostrich, which can measure up to 25 cm long and weigh up to 1.5 kg!

There is no standard shape for cells. Depending on their function, they can be:

  • Nerve cells: Irregular, elongated, and tree-like to carry messages from one part of the body to another.
  • Guard cells (in leaves): Bean-shaped to open and close stomata.
  • Other shapes include spherical, rectangular, oval, polygonal, or spindle-shaped.

3. Structure of the Cell

Every cell is basically composed of three main parts: the cell membrane, the nucleus, and the cytoplasm.

3.1 Cell Membrane & Cell Wall

The cell membrane (plasma membrane) is a living, elastic, selectively permeable layer that encloses the protoplasm. It controls the entry and exit of substances.

In plant cells, an additional rigid layer called the cell wall is present outside the membrane, providing strength and shape.

3.2 The Nucleus

The nucleus is a dense structure that controls and regulates all metabolic activities of the cell.

  • It contains a jelly-like matrix called the nucleoplasm.
  • Inside the nucleus is a dense nucleolus and a network of chromatin threads.
  • Chromatin condenses into chromosomes, which contain DNA (Deoxyribonucleic acid) and carry hereditary genes from parent to offspring.

3.3 The Cytoplasm & Organelles

The cytoplasm is the portion of protoplasm between the nucleus and the cell membrane. It contains a transparent, semi-fluid matrix where living specialized structures called organelles are suspended. Each organelle performs a specific function.

  • Mitochondria: Produce chemical energy for the cell.
  • Ribosomes: Responsible for making proteins.

4. The Plant Cell

Plant cells have specific features designed to help them stay rigid, photosynthesize, and store large amounts of nutrients.

  • Cell Wall: Non-living, freely permeable, and made of complex chemical cellulose.
  • Plastids: Organelles found only in plant cells.
    • Chloroplasts: Contain the green pigment chlorophyll to trap solar energy for food.
    • Chromoplasts: Give red and yellow colours to flowers and fruits to attract insects.
    • Leucoplasts: Colourless pigments that store starch, fats, and proteins.
  • Vacuole: A large, central bubble-like organelle bound by a membrane called the tonoplast. It contains cell sap and makes the cell turgid (firm), helping the plant stand upright.
Important

Permeable vs. Selectively Permeable
The cell wall is a sieve (freely permeable), but the cell membrane is selectively permeable. If you place raisins in plain water, they swell (absorb water). If you place plump grapes in salt water, they shrivel (lose water). This demonstrates how cell membranes selectively control the movement of molecules based on outside conditions.

5. The Animal Cell

Animal cells are uniquely structured to allow mobility and function without the need to produce their own food.

  • No Cell Wall: Animals are mobile. The absence of a rigid cell wall allows animal cells (like white blood cells or Amoeba) to change shape and squeeze through tight spaces.
  • No Plastids: Animals obtain food by hunting or grazing; they do not synthesize food through photosynthesis, so they lack chlorophyll and chloroplasts.
  • Vacuoles: Usually absent. If present, they are small and temporary (e.g., a food vacuole in Amoeba that forms to digest food and then is thrown out).
  • Centrosome: Found just outside the nucleus in animal cells. It contains cylindrical structures called centrioles which initiate cell division.

6. Key Differences at a Glance

  • Cell Wall: Present in Plants (Cellulose) | Absent in Animals.
  • Vacuoles: Large and permanent in Plants | Small, temporary, or absent in Animals.
  • Plastids: Present in Plants | Absent in Animals.
  • Centrosome: Absent in Plants | Present in Animals (initiates cell division).
  • Stored Food: Plants store starch | Animals store glycogen.
