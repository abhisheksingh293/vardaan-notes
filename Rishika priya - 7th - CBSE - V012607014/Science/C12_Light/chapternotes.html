Light

1. Introduction to Reflection

The phenomenon where an object's picture is formed in water, or light bounces back off a surface, is known as the reflection of light. A plane mirror can change the direction of light falling on it due to this phenomenon.

  • Regular Reflection: Reflection of light from plane, shiny, or polished surfaces (like mirrors or stainless steel) in a well-defined, orderly manner. This often results in a glare.
  • Diffused Reflection: Reflection of light from non-shiny, non-polished, or irregular surfaces that takes place in an irregular, scattered manner.
Concept

Lateral Inversion

When you look in a plane mirror, the image does not appear upside down but is always erect. However, the 'right' side of the object appears as the 'left' side in the image, and vice-versa. This intriguing phenomenon is called lateral inversion.

2. Important Terms & Laws of Reflection

The reflection of light from a surface always takes place in a well-defined manner according to two simple rules known as the Laws of Reflection. Before stating the laws, we must understand a few key terms:

  • Point of Incidence: The point on the surface of the mirror where the incident ray falls.
  • Normal: A line drawn perpendicular to the surface at the point of incidence.
  • Incident Ray: The ray of light coming towards the mirror.
  • Reflected Ray: The ray of light which turns back after reflection from the mirror surface.
  • Angle of Incidence: The angle between the incident ray and the normal at the point of incidence.
  • Angle of Reflection: The angle between the normal and the reflected ray at the point of incidence.
Important

The Two Laws of Reflection

  • First Law: The incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence, and the reflected ray, all lie in the same plane.
  • Second Law: The angle of incidence is always equal to the angle of reflection.

3. Real and Virtual Images

The laws of reflection help us to understand how images are formed by mirrors.

  • Virtual Image: An image that appears to be formed through an apparent intersection of the reflected rays, but there is no actual intersection. It cannot be obtained on a screen (e.g., image formed by a plane mirror).
  • Real Image: An image that is formed through the actual intersection of the reflected light rays. It can be captured on a screen.
Fact

Properties of an Image Formed by a Plane Mirror

1. The image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it.
2. It is always of the same size as the object.
3. The line joining the image of a point object and the object itself is normal (perpendicular) to the plane mirror.
4. To see the full image of yourself, you need a plane mirror at least half of your height.

4. Spherical Mirrors

A spherical mirror is a special type of mirror whose reflecting surface is a part of a sphere. There are two primary types of spherical mirrors:

  • Concave Mirror: A spherical mirror whose reflecting surface is curved inwards (the inner side of the spherical glass section is polished).
  • Convex Mirror: A spherical mirror whose reflecting surface is curved outwards (the outer side is polished).

Key Terms for Spherical Mirrors

  • Centre of Curvature (C): The centre of the sphere of which the given mirror is a part.
  • Radius of Curvature (r): The radius of the sphere of which the given mirror is a part.
  • Pole (P): The mid-point or central point of the mirror's surface.
  • Principal Axis: The line joining the pole of the mirror and its centre of curvature.
  • Focus (F): The point on the principal axis where rays parallel to the axis actually meet (concave) or appear to diverge from (convex) after reflection.
  • Focal Length (f): The distance between the pole and the focus of the mirror.
Formula

There is a very simple relationship between the focal length (f) and the radius of curvature (r) of a spherical mirror. The focal length is always half the radius of curvature:
f = r / 2

5. Rules for Drawing Ray Diagrams

At every point, a spherical mirror reflects light following the laws of reflection. To find the location of an image, we use a few special rays:

  • A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through (or appears to diverge from) the focus of the mirror after reflection.
  • A ray directed towards the focus of a spherical mirror becomes parallel to the principal axis after reflection.
  • A ray directed towards the centre of curvature of a spherical mirror retraces its path after reflection because it falls normally on the surface.

6. Summary of Image Formation

Below is the summary of images formed when an object is placed at different distances in front of spherical mirrors.

Object Position Concave Mirror (Image Nature & Position) Convex Mirror (Image Nature & Position)
Beyond C Real, inverted, diminished, formed between F and C Virtual, erect, diminished, formed between P and F
At C Real, inverted, equal in size, formed at C Virtual, erect, diminished, formed between P and F
Between C and F Real, inverted, enlarged, formed beyond C Virtual, erect, diminished, formed between P and F
At F Real, inverted, highly enlarged, formed at infinity Virtual, erect, diminished, formed between P and F

Warning

When performing experiments with a concave mirror to find its focus using sunlight, take immense care to protect your eyes. Never look directly at the sun or the concentrated glare reflected by the mirror, as it can cause permanent eye damage.

7. Practical Uses of Mirrors

Concave Mirrors

  • Medical Use: Used by ENT doctors as 'head mirrors' to focus a lamp's light onto the throat or ear of a patient. Dentists use them to get a magnified image of teeth.
  • Ophthalmoscope: Eye specialists use an ophthalmoscope fitted with a concave mirror to direct a beam of light into a patient's eye to examine the retina.
  • Reflectors: Used as reflectors in torches and headlights of vehicles to get a powerful parallel beam of light.
  • Telescopes: Used in designing large reflector-type astronomical telescopes.

Convex Mirrors

  • Rear View Mirrors: Used as rear-view mirrors in motor vehicles. Because convex mirrors always form an erect, diminished image, they help drivers see the traffic behind them over a much wider range of view than a plane mirror could.

Plane Mirrors

  • Daily Life: Used for dressing up, combing hair, and shaving.
  • Scientific Instruments: Important parts of scientific meters, the Periscope (enabling us to see overhead objects out of direct sight lines), and toys like the Kaleidoscope (to form multiple beautiful patterns through repeated reflection).
