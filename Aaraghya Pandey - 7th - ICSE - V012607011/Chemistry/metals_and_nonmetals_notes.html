METALS AND NONMETALS

Everything around us falls into the broad category of either metals or nonmetals. Understanding their distinct physical and chemical properties is essential for grasping how materials interact in our world.

1. Physical Properties of Metals

Concept

Metals are a class of elements that are typically solid, shiny, and good conductors of heat and electricity. They are easily recognizable by their physical characteristics.

Metals exhibit several unique physical properties:

  • Lustre: Metals have a shiny appearance. For example, gold and silver are highly lustrous.
  • Hardness: Most metals are hard and difficult to break, though there are exceptions like sodium and potassium, which can be cut with a knife.
  • Malleability: The property by which metals can be beaten into thin sheets. Aluminium foil is a common example.
  • Ductility: The property by which metals can be drawn into thin wires. Copper and aluminium wires are widely used.
  • Sonority: Metals produce a ringing sound when struck. This is why bells are made of metals.
  • Conductivity: Metals are excellent conductors of both heat (high thermal conductivity) and electricity (high electrical conductivity).
  • Physical State: Most metals are solid at room temperature, except mercury, which is a liquid.

2. Physical Properties of Nonmetals

Nonmetals generally have properties that are opposite to those of metals.

  • Appearance: Nonmetals generally have a dull appearance and lack lustre (except iodine and graphite).
  • Brittleness: Solid nonmetals are soft and brittle, meaning they break easily into pieces when struck (e.g., sulphur and coal).
  • Non-malleable and Non-ductile: They cannot be beaten into sheets or drawn into wires.
  • Non-sonorous: They do not produce a ringing sound when struck.
  • Poor Conductors: They are generally poor conductors of heat and electricity (insulators), with the exception of graphite, which conducts electricity.
  • Physical State: Nonmetals can exist as solids, liquids, or gases at room temperature.

3. Chemical Properties

a) Reaction with Oxygen

Both metals and nonmetals react with oxygen, but the nature of the oxides formed differs.

Fact

Metals react with oxygen to form metallic oxides, which are basic in nature (they turn red litmus blue).
Example: Magnesium burns in air to form magnesium oxide.
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

Nonmetals react with oxygen to form non-metallic oxides, which are generally acidic in nature (they turn blue litmus red).
Example: Sulphur reacts with oxygen to form sulphur dioxide.
S + O₂ → SO₂

b) Reaction with Water

  • Metals: Some metals react vigorously with water (like sodium) to form metal hydroxides and release hydrogen gas. Others react slowly.
  • Nonmetals: Generally, nonmetals do not react with water, though they may be very reactive in air (e.g., phosphorus is stored in water to prevent it from catching fire).

c) Rusting and Its Prevention

Important

Rusting is a chemical process where iron reacts with oxygen and moisture in the air to form a reddish-brown flaky substance called rust (iron oxide). It weakens the metal.

Methods to prevent rusting include:

  • Painting, Oiling, or Greasing: Applying a protective layer over the metal surface.
  • Galvanization: Coating iron with a thin layer of a more reactive metal like zinc.
  • Alloying: Mixing iron with other metals or nonmetals to make it rust-resistant (e.g., stainless steel).

4. Uses of Metals and Nonmetals

Uses of Metals:

  • Copper and Aluminium: Used for making electrical wires and cooking utensils.
  • Gold and Silver: Used extensively in making jewellery.
  • Iron: Used in construction, bridges, automobiles, and machinery.
  • Zinc: Used for galvanizing iron to prevent rusting.
  • Mercury: Used in thermometers.

Uses of Nonmetals:

  • Oxygen: Essential for respiration in living beings and for combustion.
  • Nitrogen: Used in fertilizers to enhance plant growth and in food packaging to preserve freshness.
  • Chlorine: Used in water purification processes.
  • Iodine: Used as an antiseptic (tincture of iodine) applied on wounds.
  • Carbon: Used in the form of graphite in pencils and batteries, and as diamond in jewellery.

5. Metalloids and Alloys

Metalloids are elements that possess properties intermediate between those of metals and nonmetals. They are useful as semiconductors.
Examples: Silicon (used in computer chips and solar cells), Germanium, and Antimony.

An Alloy is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a nonmetal, designed to improve the properties of the base metal (like increased strength or resistance to corrosion).
Examples: Brass, Bronze, and Stainless Steel.
