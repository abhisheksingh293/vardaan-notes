Physical and Chemical Changes

Change is the transition of any substance from one form to another form. In our day-to-day life, we observe many changes taking place around us.

Fact

Desirable vs Undesirable Changes: Changes which make our life better and are useful (e.g., milk changing to curd, cooking of food) are desirable changes. Changes which are harmful to us (e.g., earthquakes, spoiling of food) are undesirable changes.

Changes can also be categorised on the basis of how quickly they happen:

  • Fast changes: Changes in which a substance gets converted into another form quickly (e.g., burning of paper).
  • Slow changes: Changes in which a substance gets converted into another form slowly (e.g., germination of a seed, rusting of iron).

Changes are broadly classified into two main types based on whether they are temporary or permanent: Physical changes and Chemical changes.

1. Physical Changes

Concept

A change in which a substance undergoes a change in its physical properties (like shape, size, colour, or physical state) but no new substance is formed is called a physical change.

In a physical change, the chemical composition of the substance remains exactly the same. Most physical changes are reversible, meaning the original substance can be obtained back.

Examples of Physical Changes

  • Change of State: Melting of ice into water, boiling water into steam, and freezing water back to ice.
  • Dissolving: Dissolving sugar or salt in water. (The sugar can be recovered by evaporating the water).
  • Change in Shape/Size: Stretching a rubber band, crushing a can, tearing paper, cutting a log of wood.
  • Other examples: Sublimation of ammonium chloride, melting of butter, drying of clothes.

Processes Involved in Physical Changes

  • Dissolving: The process in which a solute completely mixes with a solvent to form a homogeneous mixture.
  • Melting: The process of changing a solid into a liquid at a particular temperature (melting point).
  • Boiling: The process of changing a liquid into a gas at a particular temperature (boiling point).
  • Evaporation: The process of changing a liquid into a gas at any temperature below its boiling point.
  • Condensation: The process of changing a gas into a liquid on cooling.
Important

Physical changes are highly useful in daily life. For example, evaporating sea water helps us obtain common salt, and preparing alloys (mixing metals) improves their properties (like mixing gold with silver to make jewellery).

2. Chemical Changes

Concept

A change in which a substance undergoes a change to produce one or more new substances with entirely different properties is called a chemical change (or a chemical reaction).

Chemical changes are generally permanent and irreversible. You cannot easily get the original substances back.

Examples of Chemical Changes

  • Rusting of Iron: A shiny iron nail kept in moist air develops a brown layer of rust. You cannot get the iron back from the rust.
  • Burning (Combustion): Burning of coal, wood, or paper. Burning coal produces carbon dioxide gas and ash.
  • Curdling of Milk: Milk turning sour or into curd.
  • Cooking and Digestion: Cooking food and the digestion of food in our bodies.
  • Other examples: Ripening of fruits, bursting of a firecracker, respiration, photosynthesis in plants.
Fact

A chemical change is often accompanied by visual or sensory clues, such as the release or absorption of heat or light, the evolution of a gas (producing a hissing sound), or a change in smell or colour.

Uses of Chemical Changes

Chemical changes are essential for many modern conveniences:

  • Producing heat and electricity by burning fuels like coal and petrol.
  • Extracting useful metals (like iron and aluminium) from their ores.
  • Manufacturing daily use items like soaps, detergents, plastics, and medicines.

3. Differences Between Physical and Chemical Changes

Physical Change Chemical Change
No new substance is formed. The chemical composition remains the same. One or more new substances are formed with entirely different properties.
It is usually accompanied by a change in colour, shape, size, or physical state. It is usually accompanied by the release/absorption of heat or light, evolution of gas, or change in smell.
Most physical changes are reversible. Most chemical changes are irreversible.
Examples: Melting ice, tearing paper, dissolving salt in water. Examples: Rusting iron, burning coal, spoiling food.

4. Reversible and Irreversible Changes

  • Reversible Change: A change which can be undone or reversed to get the original substance back. (e.g., melting of ice, dissolving salt).
  • Irreversible Change: A change which cannot be undone or reversed. (e.g., burning a matchstick, cooking an egg).

5. Change of States in Matter

Matter can exist in three physical states: solid, liquid, or gas. The physical state of a substance can be changed by altering the temperature (heating or cooling). This is a classic example of a physical, reversible change.

  • Fusion (Melting): Solid to Liquid (Heating ice to 0°C turns it into water).
  • Vaporisation (Boiling): Liquid to Gas (Heating water to 100°C turns it into steam).
  • Condensation: Gas to Liquid (Cooling steam turns it back into water).
  • Solidification (Freezing): Liquid to Solid (Cooling water turns it into ice).
  • Sublimation: The process of changing a solid directly into a gas on heating, without passing through the liquid state (e.g., heating Ammonium Chloride, Iodine, or Naphthalene).
  • Deposition: The process of changing a gas directly into a solid on cooling.
