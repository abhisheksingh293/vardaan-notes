Matter and Its Composition

1. Material and Matter

If you look around, you will find a wide variety of objects like chairs, tables, pens, and notebooks. These objects come in different shapes, sizes, and colours. A substance from which an object is made is called a material (e.g., wood, plastic, glass, leather, steel).

To make the study of various objects easier, we group them based on certain criteria like their material, appearance, texture, or solubility. This process of grouping things is known as classification.

Concept

Matter: Anything that occupies space and has mass is called matter. The amount of matter contained in an object is called its mass, and the amount of space occupied by it is called its volume.

Fact

Air and Water are Matter: We cannot see air, but it has mass (a balloon filled with air is heavier than an empty one) and occupies space (it inflates the balloon). Similarly, water also has mass and occupies space inside a container.

2. Composition of Matter

Every substance or material around us is made up of an extraordinarily large number of tiny particles called molecules. These particles are incredibly small, beyond human imagination, and cannot be seen even with a microscope.

A molecule is the smallest particle of a substance that can independently exist in nature. A molecule may be made up of even smaller building blocks called atoms. For example, a single water molecule consists of two atoms of hydrogen and one atom of oxygen bonded together. A single atom generally cannot exist independently in nature.

Fact

About two thousand years ago, the great Indian philosopher Maharishi Kanada suggested that all forms of matter are composed of very small particles called anu. He further suggested that each anu may be made up of still smaller particles called parmanu.

3. Characteristics of Particles of Matter

The particles that make up matter exhibit certain fundamental properties:

  • Particles are in Continuous Motion: The tiny molecules are never at rest. They are moving continuously and are said to possess kinetic energy (energy due to motion).
  • Particles Attract Each Other: The molecules of matter attract each other with a certain force which holds them together. This is called the intermolecular force of attraction.
  • Particles have Space Between Them: The empty space existing between the molecules of matter is called intermolecular space.

4. States of Matter

Matter can be classified into three main categories based on its physical state: Solid, Liquid, and Gas. Because of the difference in molecular arrangement, these states exhibit different properties.

Solids

In solids, the molecules are very closely packed. They have fixed positions and can only vibrate about their fixed positions. The intermolecular forces of attraction are very strong, and the intermolecular space is negligible. Because of this arrangement, solids possess very little kinetic energy.

Liquids

In liquids, the molecules are not very closely packed. They are free to move around within the boundary of the liquid. The intermolecular forces are weaker than in solids, and the intermolecular space is greater. Due to their ability to move, liquid molecules possess more kinetic energy than solid molecules.

Gases

In gases, the molecules are very loosely packed. The intermolecular forces of attraction are extremely weak or negligible. The intermolecular space is maximum, allowing the molecules to move around freely in all directions. Thus, gaseous molecules possess the maximum kinetic energy.

Comparison of Properties

Property Solid Liquid Gas
Arrangement of molecules Very tightly packed Loosely packed Very loosely packed
Intermolecular forces Very strong Strong (but weaker than solid) Negligible
Intermolecular space Negligible Medium Maximum
Shape & Volume Definite shape & volume Definite volume, takes container's shape No definite shape or volume
Rigidity & Flow Rigid, cannot flow Not rigid, can flow Not rigid, can flow easily

5. Compressibility of Matter

Compressibility refers to how much a substance can be squeezed into a smaller volume by applying pressure.

  • Solids: Generally non-compressible. Because the particles are already tightly packed, pushing them closer is nearly impossible.
  • Liquids: Can be compressed slightly.
  • Gases: Can be compressed easily. The large intermolecular spaces allow the gas molecules to be pushed much closer together under pressure.

6. Change of States of Matter (Interconversion)

The process by which one state of matter gets converted into another state and comes back to its original state without any change in its chemical composition is called the interconversion of states of matter.

Matter can be changed from one state to another in two main ways: by changing temperature or by applying pressure.

By Changing Temperature

  • Melting (Fusion): The process of changing a solid into a liquid on heating (e.g., Ice to Water). The heat provides kinetic energy, overcoming the strong intermolecular forces.
  • Vaporisation (Boiling): The process of changing a liquid into a gas on heating (e.g., Water to Steam). The molecules gain even more energy and break free from each other.
  • Condensation: The process of changing a gas into a liquid on cooling (e.g., Steam to Water). Molecules lose kinetic energy and come closer together.
  • Freezing (Solidification): The process of changing a liquid into a solid on cooling (e.g., Water to Ice).
Important

Sublimation & Deposition: Some substances do not pass through the liquid phase. The direct conversion of a solid into a gas on heating is called sublimation. Examples include ammonium chloride, camphor, dry ice, iodine, and naphthalene. The reverse process (gas directly to solid on cooling) is called deposition.

By Applying Pressure

A gas can be liquefied by applying high pressure and simultaneously lowering its temperature. When high pressure is applied, the gas molecules are pushed closer together. Lowering the temperature reduces their kinetic energy, increasing the intermolecular attraction until the gas condenses into a liquid.
