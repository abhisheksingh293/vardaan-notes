ELEMENTS, COMPOUNDS AND MIXTURES

All the matter around us is not pure. Matter can be broadly classified into two categories: pure substances and impure substances. Pure substances consist of elements and compounds, whereas impure substances consist of mixtures.

Concept

Pure Substances: A pure substance is made up of only one kind of atoms or molecules and has definite composition and properties. It melts and boils at a fixed temperature.

Impure Substances (Mixtures): An impure substance contains two or more pure substances (elements or compounds or both) mixed in any proportion.

1. Elements

An element is a pure substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances. It is made up of similar kinds of atoms. For example: Carbon, Aluminium, Calcium, Silver, Gold, Nitrogen, Boron, Argon, and Hydrogen.

There are more than a hundred elements known at present. 92 of these occur naturally, and the rest are man-made.

Classification of Elements

  • Metals: Constitute the maximum number of elements (e.g., Sodium, Magnesium, Iron, Gold).
  • Nonmetals: Found in lesser numbers compared to metals (e.g., Nitrogen, Oxygen, Sulphur).
  • Metalloids: Elements showing properties of both metals and nonmetals (e.g., Silicon, Arsenic).
  • Noble (Inert) Gases: Elements which are quite unreactive and do not react with other elements or compounds (e.g., Helium, Neon, Argon, Xenon).
Fact

Symbols of Elements: Each element is represented by a short symbol. Many symbols are derived from the English name (like O for Oxygen), but some are derived from their Latin names:

  • Sodium ➔ Natrium ➔ Na
  • Potassium ➔ Kalium ➔ K
  • Iron ➔ Ferrum ➔ Fe
  • Copper ➔ Cuprum ➔ Cu
  • Silver ➔ Argentum ➔ Ag
  • Gold ➔ Aurum ➔ Au

2. Molecules and Atomicity

A molecule is formed when two or more atoms combine together chemically. It is the smallest particle of an element or a compound that can exist independently and shows all the properties of that substance.

  • Molecule of an Element: Formed when similar kinds of atoms combine (e.g., two oxygen atoms form an O₂ molecule, two nitrogen atoms form an N₂ molecule).
  • Molecule of a Compound: Formed when dissimilar atoms combine (e.g., two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom form an H₂O molecule).

The number of atoms that combine together to form a molecule is known as its atomicity. Based on this, molecules can be:

  • Monoatomic: Made of one atom (e.g., Helium - He, Neon - Ne).
  • Diatomic: Made of two atoms (e.g., Hydrogen - H₂, Oxygen - O₂).
  • Triatomic: Made of three atoms (e.g., Ozone - O₃).
  • Polyatomic: Made of many atoms (e.g., Sulphur - S₈).

3. Compounds

A compound is a pure substance made up of two or more elements chemically combined together in a fixed proportion by mass. Examples include Sodium chloride (NaCl), Water (H₂O), Carbon dioxide (CO₂), and Sugar.

Characteristics of Compounds

  • A compound cannot be separated into its constituent elements by simple physical methods. It can only be separated by chemical methods (like electrolysis).
  • The properties of a compound are entirely different from the properties of its constituent elements. For instance, Hydrogen burns, Oxygen supports burning, but Water (made of both) extinguishes fire!
  • A compound is formed when constituent elements combine in a fixed ratio by mass.
  • Energy (heat or light) is usually absorbed or released when a compound is formed.

4. Mixtures

A mixture is an impure substance made up of two or more substances (elements or compounds) mixed together in any ratio under any condition. They are not chemically combined.

Types of Mixtures

  • Homogeneous Mixture: A mixture in which the constituents are distributed uniformly (e.g., salt in water, milk, brass).
  • Heterogeneous Mixture: A mixture in which the constituents are not distributed uniformly (e.g., mixture of iron and sulphur powder, sand and water).
Important

Compounds vs. Mixtures

  • Nature: Compounds are pure substances; Mixtures are impure substances.
  • Combination: Elements in a compound combine chemically; components in a mixture are just mixed physically.
  • Ratio: Elements in a compound combine in a fixed ratio; components in a mixture can be in any ratio.
  • Properties: A compound has entirely new properties; a mixture retains the properties of its constituents.
  • Separation: Compounds cannot be separated by physical methods; mixtures can easily be separated by simple physical methods.

5. Separation of Mixtures

Because the components of a mixture are not chemically combined, they can be separated using various physical techniques based on their physical states and properties:

  • Evaporation: Used to recover a solid component that has dissolved in a liquid. The liquid is evaporated (turned into vapour), leaving the solid behind (e.g., obtaining common salt from seawater).
  • Distillation: A method of separating dissolved impurities in a liquid by heating the liquid first, converting it into vapours, and finally condensing the vapours back to pure liquid by cooling.
  • Fractional Distillation: A special type of distillation used to separate a mixture of liquids that have different boiling points (usually differing by about 20°C).
  • Sublimation: Used to separate a mixture containing a solid that sublimes (changes directly from solid to gas on heating). Ammonium chloride, camphor, naphthalene, and iodine can be separated this way.
  • Using a Separating Funnel: Used to separate two immiscible liquids (liquids that do not mix, like oil and water). They separate into distinct layers based on their densities.
  • Chromatography: A technique used to separate the coloured components of ink or dyes. It works on the principle that different coloured components have different levels of solubility in water.
