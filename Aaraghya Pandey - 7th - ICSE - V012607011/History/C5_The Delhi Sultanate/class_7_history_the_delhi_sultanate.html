THE DELHI SULTANATE

1. Introduction to the Delhi Sultanate

The establishment of the Delhi Sultanate marked a new era in Indian history following the defeat of Prithviraj Chauhan by Muhammad Ghori in the Second Battle of Tarain (1192 CE). This victory laid the foundation for Turkish and Afghan rule in India.

From 1206 to 1526, the subcontinent was governed by five powerful dynasties, collectively known as the Delhi Sultanate:

  • The Slave Dynasty (1206-1290)
  • The Khalji Dynasty (1290-1320)
  • The Tughlaq Dynasty (1320-1414)
  • The Sayyid Dynasty (1414-1451)
  • The Lodi Dynasty (1451-1526)
Important

The Turning Point: The Second Battle of Tarain (1192 CE) was the crucial event that enabled the foundation of Islamic empires in Northern India.

2. The Slave Dynasty (1206-1290)

Also known as the Mamluk Dynasty (meaning 'slave' or 'owned' in Arabic), this was the first dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate, established after the death of Muhammad Ghori in 1206.

Qutb-ud-din Aibak (1206-1210)

A former slave of Muhammad Ghori, he became the first independent Muslim ruler of northern India with Lahore as his capital.

  • Titles & Charity: Earned the title of Lakh Baksh (giver of lakhs) due to his immense generosity.
  • Architecture: Started the construction of the Qutub Minar (named after Sufi saint Khwaja Qutb-ud-din Bakhtiyar Kaki) and the Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque.
  • Death: Died accidentally in 1210 while playing polo (chaugan).

Shams-ud-din Iltutmish (1211-1236)

Iltutmish, Aibak’s son-in-law, is considered the real founder of the Delhi Sultanate because he shifted the capital from Lahore to Delhi and firmly consolidated the empire.

  • Amir-i-Chahalgani: Created a loyal group of forty Turkish nobles known as 'The Forty' to protect his rule.
  • Mongol Threat: Saved India from the invasion of Genghis Khan by refusing shelter to the Shah of Persia.
Concept

The Iqta System: Introduced by Iltutmish, this was a system where land assignments (Iqtas) were given to military officers in lieu of a cash salary, along with military responsibilities.

Raziya Sultan (1236-1240)

She was the first and only Muslim woman to rule over the Delhi Sultanate. Despite being an efficient, just, and brave ruler, she faced severe opposition from Turkish nobles who refused to take orders from a woman.

Ghiyas-ud-din Balban (1266-1286)

Balban ruled the empire with an "iron hand" (policy of blood and iron) to crush rebellions and secure the state.

  • Propagated the theory of Divine Rights, claiming the Sultan was God's representative on Earth.
  • Introduced the practice of Zaminbosi / Sijda, where people had to kneel and touch their forehead to the ground to greet the Sultan.
  • Created a dedicated military department called Diwan-i-Arz to counter Mongol threats.

3. The Khalji Dynasty (1290-1320)

Founded by Jalal-ud-din Firuz Khalji in 1290 after overthrowing the weak successors of Balban.

Ala-ud-din Khalji (1296-1316)

The greatest ruler of the Khalji dynasty and the first Sultan to expand his empire to the extreme South of India with the help of his trusted general, Malik Kafur.

Key Reforms of Ala-ud-din Khalji

  • Land Revenue Policy: Abolished small iqtas and brought lands under the Crown (Khalsa). Fixed revenue at 50% for the fertile Ganga-Yamuna Doab and 33% for other lands.
  • Market Regulations: Fixed the prices of daily commodities to maintain his large standing army at a low cost. Appointed a superintendent called Shahna-i-Mandi to ensure strict compliance.
  • Art & Architecture: A great patron of education and arts. Built the Siri Fort and the famous Alai Darwaza. The renowned Persian poet Amir Khusrau was his court poet.
Fact

Military Innovations: To maintain army efficiency, Ala-ud-din introduced Dagh (the branding of horses to prevent substitution) and Chehra / Huliya (descriptive identity rolls of soldiers).

4. The Tughlaq Dynasty (1320-1414)

Founded by Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq, this dynasty saw massive territorial expansion but also catastrophic administrative failures.

Muhammad-bin-Tughlaq (1325-1351)

A brilliant scholar of philosophy, mathematics, and logic, but known to historians as a "mixture of opposites" due to his visionary but poorly executed schemes.

  • Transfer of Capital (1327): Ordered the capital moved from Delhi to Devagiri (renamed Daulatabad) to control the South better and avoid Mongol attacks. It caused massive suffering, and the capital was later shifted back to Delhi.
  • Taxation in Doab: Increased taxes twenty-fold during a terrible famine. Peasants revolted, forcing him to withdraw the orders.
  • Khurasan Expedition: Raised and paid a massive army a year in advance to conquer Central Asia, only to abandon the costly plan later.
Concept

Token Currency: Muhammad-bin-Tughlaq introduced copper coins (jital) to replace silver coins (tanka). The plan failed disastrously because common people started forging the copper coins at home, bankrupting the treasury.

Firoz Shah Tughlaq (1351-1388)

Succeeded his cousin and adopted a policy to appease the ulemas (religious scholars) and nobles by making the iqta system hereditary.

  • Welfare Measures: Built canals, rest houses, and a special department (Diwan-i-Khairat) to help the poor and arrange marriages.
  • New Towns: Established Jaunpur, Firozabad, Firozpur, and Hissar.
  • Taxation: Abolished heavy burdens and levied only Islamic taxes: Kharaj (land tax), Zakat (religious tax), Khams (booty share), and Jizya (tax on non-Muslims).

5. Invasion of Timur (1398)

The Mongol conqueror Timur invaded a weakening Delhi Sultanate in 1398, plundering Delhi and leaving it utterly devastated. He did not stay in India but left behind Khizr Khan as his viceroy.

6. The Sayyid Dynasty (1414-1451)

Established by Khizr Khan, who acted as a representative of Timur. The Sayyid rulers (Mubarak Shah, Muhammad Shah, Alam Shah) were generally weak, and their reigns were plagued by constant rebellions by powerful nobles.

7. The Lodi Dynasty (1451-1526)

The Lodis were the last ruling family of the Sultanate and the first Afghan dynasty to rule Delhi.

Bahlul Lodi (1451-1489)

The founder of the Lodi dynasty who suppressed local revolts and conquered Jaunpur.

Sikandar Lodi (1489-1517)

Considered the greatest and wisest of the Lodi rulers. He moved the capital from Delhi to Agra, introduced welfare measures, and lowered commodity prices to support the people.

Ibrahim Lodi (1517-1526)

His arrogant behavior caused his nobles to revolt. Daulat Khan Lodi, the governor of Punjab, invited the Mughal ruler Babur from Kabul to invade India.

Important

The End of the Sultanate: In 1526, Babur defeated Ibrahim Lodi in the First Battle of Panipat. This historic victory marked the definitive end of the Delhi Sultanate and birthed the majestic Mughal Empire.
