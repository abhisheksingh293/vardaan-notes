LIGHT ENERGY

The world around us has beautiful objects that can only be admired when light shines on them. Without light, we cannot see anything. When light falls on an object, it is reflected from it and reaches our eyes, enabling us to see.

Concept

Reflection of Light: When light falls on the surface of an object, the object sends the light back. The bouncing back of light into its original medium after striking a surface is called reflection of light.

1. Behavior of Objects Towards Light

  • Opaque Objects: Objects which block the light falling on them completely.
  • Transparent Objects: Objects which transmit the light falling on them completely.
  • Translucent Objects: Objects which allow partial transmission of light and block it partially.

2. Types of Reflection

Reflection depends upon the nature of the reflecting surface:

Regular Reflection

  • Occurs when a beam of light falls on a smooth and highly polished surface (like a mirror or still water).
  • Almost the entire light gets reflected in a definite direction (reflected rays travel parallel to each other).
  • Results in the formation of clear images.

Irregular (Diffused) Reflection

  • Occurs when a beam of light falls on a rough and uneven surface.
  • Light gets reflected in different directions (scattered).
  • Results in blurred or hazy images. Remember, most daily objects are visible due to diffused reflection!

3. Terms Related to Reflection of Light

Definitions
  • Incident ray: A ray of light falling on a reflecting surface.
  • Reflected ray: The ray of light that bounces off the reflecting surface.
  • Point of incidence: The point on the reflecting surface where the incident ray strikes.
  • Normal: An imaginary line drawn perpendicular to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence.
  • Angle of incidence (i): The angle between the incident ray and the normal.
  • Angle of reflection (r): The angle between the reflected ray and the normal.
Important

Laws of Reflection:

  1. The angle of incidence is always equal to the angle of reflection (∠i = ∠r).
  2. The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal all lie in the same plane.

Note: These laws hold true for ALL types of mirrors (plane or curved).

4. Mirrors and Types of Images

A mirror is a smooth polished surface that reflects light completely. They are broadly classified into Plane Mirrors (flat surface) and Curved Mirrors (spherical, either concave or convex).

Real vs. Virtual Image

  • Real Image: An image that can be obtained on a screen. It is formed when reflected rays actually meet. Real images are always inverted. (e.g., Cinema screen images).
  • Virtual Image: An image that cannot be obtained on a screen. It is formed when reflected rays appear to meet when produced backwards. Virtual images are always erect (upright). (e.g., Our image in a dressing mirror).
Fact

Characteristics of an Image Formed by a Plane Mirror:

  • The image is upright (erect) and virtual (cannot be formed on a screen).
  • The image is of the same size as the object.
  • The image is formed as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it.
  • The image is laterally inverted (the left side of the object appears as the right side of the image, e.g., the word AMBULANCE written backwards on vehicles).

Uses of Plane Mirrors

  • As looking glasses and dressing mirrors.
  • By barbers to show the backside of a customer's head.
  • In solar cookers for reflecting the sun's rays into the interior.
  • In optical instruments like periscopes and kaleidoscopes.
  • In an optician's room to double the effective length of the testing room.

5. Speed of Light

Light travels incredibly fast. The speed of light depends on the nature of the medium it passes through.

Fact Data
  • Speed of light in Vacuum or Air: 3 × 10⁸ m/s (3,00,000 km/s).
  • Speed of light in Water: 2.25 × 10⁸ m/s.
  • Speed of light in Glass: 2.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

6. Colours of Light

The light emitted by the sun is white light. When white light passes through a glass prism, it splits into its seven constituent colours. This process is called dispersion of white light.

The seven colours are Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, and Red (VIBGYOR). A rainbow is formed due to the dispersion of sunlight by small water drops hanging in the sky.

Primary and Secondary Colours

  • Primary Colours: Red, Green, and Blue. They cannot be formed by combining other colours, but combining these three produces white light.
  • Secondary Colours (Colour Addition): Colours obtained by mixing two primary colours.
    • Blue + Red = Magenta (Purple)
    • Green + Red = Yellow
    • Blue + Green = Cyan (Peacock Blue)
Important Note

How we see the colour of an object:

  • Opaque Objects: An opaque object appears to be the colour of the light it reflects (absorbing the rest). A red object reflects red and absorbs all other colours. A black object absorbs all colours, and a white object reflects all colours.
  • Transparent Objects: A transparent object appears to be the colour of the light it allows to pass through it (transmits).
