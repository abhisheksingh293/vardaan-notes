Light Energy

1. Reflection of Light

When light falls on an object, the object sends the light back. The process of sending back the light rays which fall on the surface of an object is called reflection of light. We are able to see the beautiful objects around us only because they reflect light into our eyes.

Types of Reflection

  • Regular Reflection: When a beam of light falls on a smooth and highly polished surface (like a mirror), all the reflected rays travel parallel to each other in a definite direction. This results in the formation of clear images.
  • Irregular or Diffused Reflection: When a beam of light falls on a rough or unpolished surface (like a wall, book, or rough water), the light gets reflected in different directions. This type of reflection forms blurred or hazy images.
Concept

Terms Related to Reflection:

  • Incident Ray: A ray of light falling on a reflecting surface.
  • Reflected Ray: The ray of light that bounces off the reflecting surface.
  • Point of Incidence: The point on the reflecting surface where the incident ray strikes.
  • Normal: An imaginary line drawn perpendicular to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence.
  • Angle of Incidence: The angle between the incident ray and the normal.
  • Angle of Reflection: The angle between the reflected ray and the normal.

2. Laws of Reflection

The reflection of light by any surface always follows two strict laws, known as the laws of reflection:

  1. The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence, all lie in the same plane.
  2. The angle of incidence is always equal to the angle of reflection.
Important

If a ray of light falls perpendicularly (at 90 degrees) on a reflecting surface, it retraces its path. In this case, both the angle of incidence and the angle of reflection are zero.

3. Mirrors and Images

A mirror is a highly polished surface that reflects most of the light falling on it. A plane mirror has a flat reflecting surface, while a spherical mirror has a curved reflecting surface.

Real and Virtual Images

  • Real Image: The image which can be obtained on a screen is called a real image. It is formed when light rays actually intersect after reflection.
  • Virtual Image: The image which cannot be obtained on a screen is called a virtual image. It is formed when light rays appear to intersect when produced backwards. The image formed by a plane mirror is always virtual.

Characteristics of Images Formed by a Plane Mirror

The image formed by a standard plane mirror possesses the following specific characteristics:

  • The image forms an upright (erect) image of the object.
  • The image is of the same size as the object.
  • The image is formed as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it.
  • The image is a virtual image which cannot be formed on a screen.
  • The image is laterally inverted (the left side of the object appears as the right side of the image, and vice-versa).

Uses of Plane Mirrors

Plane mirrors are utilized in various everyday applications:

  • They are used as looking glasses in homes and dressing tables.
  • They are used in barbershops to show the customer the back of their head.
  • They are used in solar cookers for reflecting the rays of the sun into the cooker.
  • They are used in constructing optical devices like periscopes and kaleidoscopes.

4. Speed of Light

Light travels at an incredibly fast speed. In a vacuum or in the air, the speed of light is $3 \times 10^8$ m/s (which is 3,00,000 km/s). It takes about 8 minutes for light to travel from the Sun to the Earth.

Fact

The speed of light depends on the nature of the transparent medium it travels through. In mediums like glass or water, the speed of light is less than its speed in a vacuum.

5. Colours of Light

Constituent Colours of White Light

White light (like sunlight) is actually composed of seven different colours. The phenomenon of splitting white light into its constituent colours is called dispersion. These seven colours can be remembered by the acronym VIBGYOR:

  • Violet
  • Indigo
  • Blue
  • Green
  • Yellow
  • Orange
  • Red

Primary and Secondary Colours (Colour Addition)

The foundation of all colours in light starts with three primary colours:

  • Primary Colours: Red, Blue, and Green. These colours cannot be formed by mixing any other colours. When mixed together in equal proportions, they produce white light.
  • Secondary Colours: These are formed by the combination of two primary colours.
    • Red + Blue = Magenta
    • Red + Green = Yellow
    • Blue + Green = Cyan (Peacock Blue)

Appearance of Colour of Objects

The world is colourful because of how objects interact with light through a process called Colour Subtraction.

  • Opaque Objects: When white light falls on an opaque object, it absorbs some colours and reflects others. The object appears to be the colour of the light it reflects. A red rose appears red because it reflects red light and absorbs all other colours. A black object absorbs all colours, while a white object reflects all colours.
  • Transparent Objects: A transparent object appears to be the colour of the light it allows to pass through it. A piece of red glass appears red because it transmits red light and absorbs the rest.
