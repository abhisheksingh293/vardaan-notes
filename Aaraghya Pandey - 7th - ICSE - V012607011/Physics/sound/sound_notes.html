Sound

1. Sources of Sound

Sound is a form of energy produced by vibrating bodies. When particles or objects vibrate, they transfer their energy to the molecules of the surrounding medium, producing sound. The sound is produced as long as the object continues vibrating.

Musical Instruments

Different musical instruments produce different types of sound. They generally fall into four categories:

  • String Instruments: Examples include guitar, violin, sitar, and veena. They produce sound by the vibrations of stretched strings. The frequency depends upon the length, material, thickness, and tightness of the string.
  • Percussion Instruments: Examples include drum, dholak, and tabla. In these instruments, a stretched leather membrane is made to vibrate by striking it.
  • Wind Instruments: Examples include trumpet, flute, and shehnai. When air is blown into them, the enclosed air column vibrates to produce sound.
  • Reed Instruments: Examples include harmonium and mouth organ. They have thin metal strips known as reeds that vibrate when air is blown through them.

Sound Produced by Human Beings

Humans have a voice box in their throat called the larynx, located at the upper end of the windpipe. Inside it, two ligaments called vocal cords are stretched across the larynx. When air is expelled from the lungs through the narrow slit between them, the vocal cords vibrate and produce sound.

2. Propagation of Sound

For sound to travel from one place to another, a material medium is essential. The vibrating object passes its motion to the air molecules around it, which pass it to the next layer, creating a wave.

  • Sound can travel through Solids, Liquids, and Gases: Sound travels fastest through solids (e.g., steel, string), followed by liquids (e.g., water), and slowest through gases (e.g., air).
  • Sound cannot travel through vacuum: There are no particles in a vacuum to pass on the vibrations. Hence, we cannot hear sound on the moon.
  • Sound travels in all directions: Sound spreads outwards from the source in all directions through the air.

3. Sound has a Wave Motion

Wave motion is the transfer of sound energy through a medium due to the to-and-fro vibrations of the medium particles. Medium particles do not move permanently from their positions; only the energy moves forward.

Concept

Sound waves are Longitudinal Waves:

The particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction in which the sound wave moves. This creates alternate regions:

  • Compressions (C): Regions where medium particles are crowded together (high density).
  • Rarefactions (R): Regions where medium particles are spread apart (low density).

Terms Related to a Wave

  • Vibration: One complete to-and-fro motion of a particle about its mean position.
  • Amplitude (A): The maximum displacement of the vibrating particles from their mean undisturbed position. SI unit is metre (m).
  • Time Period (T): The time taken by a vibrating particle to complete one vibration. SI unit is second (s).
  • Frequency (v or n): The number of vibrations completed by a particle in one second. SI unit is Hertz (Hz).
  • Wavelength (λ): The length of one complete wave (the distance between two successive compressions or rarefactions). SI unit is metre (m).

4. Characteristics of Sound

We can distinguish between various sounds using three important characteristics:

1. Loudness

Loudness is the characteristic that distinguishes a feeble (soft) sound from a loud sound of the same frequency. It is expressed in decibels (dB). Loudness depends primarily on the amplitude of the vibration. Larger amplitude produces a louder sound.

2. Pitch

Pitch is the characteristic that distinguishes a shrill (high-pitched) sound from a hoarse or flat (low-pitched) sound. Pitch depends on the frequency of the vibrations. Higher frequency means a higher pitch.

3. Quality or Timbre

Timbre is the characteristic that enables us to distinguish between sounds of the same pitch and loudness produced by different musical instruments (e.g., distinguishing a flute from a violin).

5. Speed of Sound

The speed of sound depends on the nature and density of the medium. The speed of sound in air at 0°C is approx. 331 m/s (or about 340 m/s at room temperature).

Fact

Speed of Sound vs. Speed of Light:

The speed of light ($3 \times 10^8$ m/s) is vastly greater than the speed of sound. This is why during a thunderstorm, the flash of lightning is seen almost instantly, while the sound of the thunder is heard a few seconds later.

6. Audible and Inaudible Sounds

Although all vibrating bodies produce sound waves, the human ear is only sensitive to a specific range of frequencies.

Important

Frequency Ranges:

  • Audible Range (Sonic): 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. A normal human ear can only hear sounds within this range.
  • Infrasonic Sound: Frequencies lower than 20 Hz (e.g., produced during earthquakes, by elephants, whales).
  • Ultrasonic Sound: Frequencies higher than 20,000 Hz (e.g., heard or produced by dogs, bats, dolphins). Used in SONAR and medical imaging.

7. Reflection of Sound

Just like light, sound can change direction and bounce back when it falls on a hard surface. This is called the reflection of sound. It obeys the laws of reflection (angle of incidence = angle of reflection).

Echo

An echo is the repetition of sound caused by the reflection of sound waves from a hard surface (like a tall building or a cliff). To hear a distinct echo, the minimum distance between the source of sound and the reflecting body must be 17 metres.

Reverberation

When sound is produced in a big hall, it suffers multiple reflections from the walls, ceiling, and floor. The repeated reflection that results in the persistence of sound is called reverberation.

Absorption of Sound

Materials with loose, rough, or fluffy textures (like carpets, thermocol, heavy curtains, gunny bags) absorb sound rather than reflecting it. These materials are used to soundproof rooms and reduce unwanted reverberation in auditoriums.
