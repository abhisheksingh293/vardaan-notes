WEATHER AND CLIMATE

1. Introduction

The earth's atmosphere is constantly changing. These atmospheric changes directly affect our daily lives, influencing our clothing, agriculture, housing, and routine activities. To understand these changes, it is essential to distinguish between two fundamental concepts: Weather and Climate.

Concept

Weather: The state of the atmosphere over a short period of time at a specific place. It is highly variable and can change from hour to hour or day to day.

Climate: The average weather conditions of a given location recorded over a relatively long period of time (typically 30 to 35 years).

2. Difference Between Weather and Climate

  • Duration: Weather is a short-term phenomenon, while climate is a long-term pattern.
  • Area Covered: Weather relates to a relatively small area (like a city), whereas climate covers a vast geographical region.
  • Stability: Weather changes frequently and rapidly. Climate remains relatively constant for decades.
  • Study: The scientific study of weather is known as Meteorology (scientists are called Meteorologists). The study of climate is called Climatology.

3. Elements of Weather and Climate

The fundamental elements that constitute both weather and climate are identical. They interact continuously to shape the atmospheric conditions of any place. The major elements are:

  • Temperature
  • Atmospheric Pressure
  • Humidity
  • Precipitation
  • Winds and Clouds
Fact

Stevenson's Screen: This is a special wooden box painted white with louvered sides, used to house meteorological instruments. Wood is a poor conductor of heat, and the white color reflects bright sunlight. This ensures the instruments measure the true temperature of the moving air, rather than direct solar heating.

4. Measuring Weather Elements

I. Temperature

Temperature refers to the degree of hotness or coldness of the air. It is measured using an instrument called a Thermometer, which usually employs the Celsius (°C) or Fahrenheit (°F) scale.

  • Six's Maximum and Minimum Thermometer: Specifically designed to record the highest (maximum) and lowest (minimum) temperatures reached during a single day.
  • Thermograph: An advanced instrument that provides a continuous, automated record of temperature changes on graph paper attached to a revolving cylinder.

II. Atmospheric Pressure

The pressure exerted by the weight of the air on the earth's surface is known as atmospheric pressure. It decreases with increasing altitude and is measured in millibars (mb) using an instrument called a Barometer (or a continuously recording Barograph).

III. Winds

Air in horizontal motion across the earth's surface is called wind. Winds naturally blow from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure.

  • Anemometer: An instrument used to measure the exact speed of the wind.
  • Wind Vane: An instrument used to observe and determine the direction from which the wind is blowing.

IV. Humidity

Humidity is the amount of moisture or water vapour present in the atmosphere. The capacity of the air to hold moisture depends entirely on its temperature.

  • Humidity is measured using an instrument called a Hygrometer, the most common type being the Wet Bulb and Dry Bulb Thermometer.

V. Precipitation

The falling of accumulated moisture from the atmosphere back to the earth's surface—in the form of rain, snow, or hail—is called precipitation. Rainfall, the most common form, is measured using a Rain Gauge.

Important

Meteorological Map Lines:

  • Isotherms: Imaginary lines drawn on a map connecting places that experience the exact same temperature at a given time.
  • Isobars: Imaginary lines on a map connecting places that share the same atmospheric pressure.
  • Isohyets: Imaginary lines joining places on a map that receive the same amount of rainfall during a given period.
