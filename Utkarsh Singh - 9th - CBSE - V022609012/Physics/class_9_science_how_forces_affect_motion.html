How Forces Affect Motion

1. The Concept of Force

A force is an underlying cause that leads to a change in the position or velocity of an object. Whenever we push, pull, kick, or squeeze something, we are applying a force.

  • Causes of Force: It can make an object move from rest, change its speed, change its direction, or even change its shape.
  • Vector Quantity: Force has both magnitude (strength) and direction. A change in either of these alters the effect of the force on the object.
Concept

Force Definition

Force is a push or pull upon an object resulting from the object's interaction with another object. The SI unit of force is the newton (N).

We can measure the magnitude of force (like the gravitational pull or weight of an object) using a spring balance. The reading gives a direct measure of the force applied on the spring.

2. Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

In most real-life scenarios, multiple forces act on an object simultaneously. The overall effect depends on the net force.

Balanced Forces

When multiple forces acting on an object are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction, they cancel each other out. This is called a state of balanced forces.

  • The net force is zero.
  • The object does not change its state of motion (if at rest, it remains at rest; if moving, it keeps moving with constant velocity).

Unbalanced Forces

When the acting forces are unequal, they do not cancel out completely, leaving a non-zero net force.

  • The object accelerates in the direction of the net force.
  • Forces in the same direction add up; forces in opposite directions subtract.

3. The Force of Friction

Friction is a force that arises between the surfaces in contact and always acts in a direction opposite to the direction of motion (or applied force).

  • To set an object in motion, the applied force must be greater than the force of friction.
  • Once moving, if the applied force equals the frictional force, the net force becomes zero, and the object moves with a constant velocity.
  • If the applied force is removed, friction is the unbalanced force that eventually brings the object to rest.
Important

Normal vs. Gravitational Force

When an object is placed on a surface, the Earth pulls it downward (Gravitational Force or Weight), but the surface pushes it equally upward (Normal Force). Because these balance out, an object at rest on a table does not fall through it.

4. Newton's First Law of Motion (Law of Inertia)

Based on Galileo's thought experiments with frictionless surfaces, Isaac Newton formulated his first law of motion:

"An object at rest remains at rest and an object in motion continues to move with a constant velocity, unless a net force acts upon the object."

  • Inertia: The natural tendency of objects to resist any change in their state of rest or uniform motion.
  • A net force is required to change velocity, not to maintain a constant velocity in a frictionless world.

5. Newton's Second Law of Motion

While the first law describes what happens when there is no net force, the second law explains the behavior of an object when an unbalanced net force *is* applied.

"When a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force. The magnitude of the acceleration is proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass of the object."

Mathematical Expression

F = m × a
(Where F = Force, m = mass, a = acceleration)

Fact

Defining One Newton

One newton (1 N) is defined as the amount of force required to produce an acceleration of 1 m/s² on an object having a mass of 1 kg.

Real-world Applications of the Second Law

  • Catching a Cricket Ball: A fielder pulls their hands backward while catching a fast ball. This increases the time taken to stop the ball, thereby decreasing the acceleration, which ultimately reduces the impact force on the hands.
  • Airbags in Vehicles: In a collision, airbags increase the duration over which the passenger's head hits the dashboard, resulting in smaller acceleration and reduced injury risk.
  • Breaking a Coconut: Bringing it down at high velocity against a hard surface forces it to stop in a fraction of a second. The resulting massive acceleration requires a huge opposing force, shattering the shell.

6. Newton's Third Law of Motion

Forces never act alone; they always exist in pairs resulting from interactions between objects.

"Whenever one object is exerting a force on a second object, the second object is simultaneously exerting an equal and opposite force on the first object."

Important

Key Rule for the Third Law

Action and Reaction pairs never cancel each other out because they act on two different objects. Also, equal forces do not guarantee equal accelerations if the masses of the two objects differ (e.g., Earth and a falling fruit).

Examples of Action and Reaction

  • Walking/Running: You push the ground backward with your foot (Action). The force of friction from the ground pushes your foot forward with an equal force (Reaction), propelling you.
  • Rowing a Canoe: The paddle pushes water backward, and the water pushes the paddle forward.
  • Rocket Launch: The engine expels hot gases downwards at high speed. The gases exert an equal upward force on the rocket, lifting it off the ground.
  • Gun Recoil: A fired bullet moves forward with immense force; an equal reactionary force pushes the heavy gun backwards (recoil). The gun's acceleration is much smaller due to its larger mass compared to the bullet.

7. Forces Acting on a System of Objects

When analyzing two or more objects connected together (like two boxes tied by a string being pulled), it is often simpler to treat them as a single system.

  • Internal Forces: Forces that objects within the system exert on each other (e.g., tension in the connecting string). These cancel out within the system boundary.
  • External Forces: Forces applied from outside the system (e.g., the hand pulling the boxes).
  • By treating connected bodies as one system, we can calculate total acceleration using Newton's second law by simply dividing the external net force by the total combined mass: a = Fexternal / (m1 + m2).
