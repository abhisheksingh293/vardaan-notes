EXPLORING THE WONDERS OF LIGHT

1. Refraction of Light

Light travels in a straight line, but its speed changes when a ray of light moves from one medium to another. For instance, the speed of light in air is 3 × 10⁸ m/s, and in glass, it is 2 × 10⁸ m/s.

Concept

Refraction of light is the bending of light when it travels from one medium to another. It occurs whenever a light ray encounters a boundary that separates two optically different media.

The speed of light slows down on entering an optically denser medium (like glass or water compared to air) and increases on entering an optically rarer medium.

Terms Related to Refraction

  • Incident Ray: The ray of light that falls on the boundary separating two media.
  • Refracted Ray: The ray of light that gets deviated from its original path and bends upon entering the second medium.
  • Point of Incidence: The point on the boundary from where the incident ray enters the second medium.
  • Normal: A perpendicular line drawn to the boundary at the point of incidence.
  • Angle of Incidence (∠i): The angle the incident ray makes with the normal.
  • Angle of Refraction (∠r): The angle the refracted ray makes with the normal.

2. Laws of Refraction

Whenever light suffers refraction at the boundary of two optically different media, it obeys two laws:

  • First Law: The incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence, and the refracted ray all lie in the same plane.
  • Second Law (Snell's Law): For a given pair of media, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is always constant. This constant is the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first.

sin i / sin r = constant (refractive index)

Important

The second law of refraction is popularly known as Snell's law, named after Willebrord Snellius who discovered it in 1621.

3. Refractive Index

The change of speed of light when entering a second medium is denoted by a constant called the refractive index. It indicates how much the speed of light has slowed down in a medium compared to a vacuum.

Concept

Refractive Index (μ) = Speed of light in vacuum (or air) / Speed of light in a medium.

Since it is a ratio of two similar quantities (speeds), it has no units.

Fact

The refractive index of Air is approximately 1.0003, Water is 1.33, Glass is 1.5, and Diamond is 2.42.

Properties of Refraction

  • When light enters a denser medium (e.g., air to glass), it bends towards the normal (∠r < ∠i).
  • When light travels to a rarer medium (e.g., glass to air), it bends away from the normal (∠r > ∠i).
  • A ray travelling along the normal suffers no refraction (∠i = 0, ∠r = 0).

4. Effects and Examples of Refraction

Refraction is responsible for many optical illusions we see in everyday life.

  • A pencil immersed slanting in water appears bent and shorter.
  • Objects completely submerged in water appear raised (e.g., a coin at the bottom of a glass appears closer than it actually is). This makes swimming pools appear shallower than their actual depth.
  • The sun is visible shortly before actual sunrise and after actual sunset due to atmospheric refraction.
  • The twinkling of stars is caused by the refraction of starlight through multiple layers of Earth's atmosphere with varying temperatures and densities.

5. Refraction through a Parallel-Sided Glass Slab

When a light ray passes through a rectangular glass slab:

  1. It refracts first at the air-glass interface, bending towards the normal.
  2. It refracts again at the glass-air interface, bending away from the normal.
  3. The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but is slightly displaced sideways. This is called lateral displacement or lateral shift.

6. Refraction of Light through a Prism

Concept

A prism is an optically transparent device made of glass with two triangular bases and three rectangular sides (refracting surfaces). The angle between any two refracting surfaces is the angle of the prism.

Unlike a glass slab, the emergent ray is not parallel to the incident ray in a prism. The incident ray deviates from its original path by an angle known as the angle of deviation (d). In a prism, the refracted ray always bends towards the base of the prism.

7. Dispersion of Light

When white light (like sunlight) passes through a prism, it splits into seven different colours: Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, and Red (VIBGYOR). This band of colours is known as a spectrum.

Concept

Dispersion is the phenomenon of splitting of white light into its component colours.

This happens because the refractive index of glass is different for different colours, causing each colour to travel at a different speed inside the prism. Violet bends the most, while red bends the least.

Fact

A rainbow is a natural demonstration of dispersion. Water droplets in the atmosphere act like tiny prisms, refracting and dispersing sunlight into a spectrum.

8. Spherical Mirrors

A spherical mirror is a smooth, highly polished curved surface that is a part of a hollow sphere, with one side reflecting and the other side opaque.

Types of Spherical Mirrors

  • Concave Mirror: Polished on the outside so the inner hollow surface reflects light. (Like the inner bowl of a spoon).
  • Convex Mirror: The inner hollow surface is opaque, and the outer bulging surface reflects light. (Like the back of a spoon).

Terms Related to Spherical Mirrors

  • Pole (P): The geometrical centre of the spherical mirror.
  • Centre of Curvature (C): The centre of the hollow sphere of which the mirror forms a part.
  • Radius of Curvature (R): The radius of the hollow sphere of which the mirror is a part (distance PC).
  • Principal Axis: The straight line joining the pole and the centre of curvature.
  • Focus (F): A point on the principal axis where rays parallel to it actually meet (concave) or appear to meet (convex) after reflection.
  • Focal Length (f): The distance between the pole and the focus. It is half the radius of curvature (f = R/2).

9. Real and Virtual Images

Important

A Real Image is formed when reflected light rays actually meet. It is always inverted and can be obtained on a screen (e.g., cinema screen).

A Virtual Image is formed when reflected rays appear to meet when produced backwards. It is always erect and cannot be obtained on a screen (e.g., image in a plane mirror).

10. Image Formation by Spherical Mirrors

Concave Mirror:

The nature and size of the image depend on the object's position:

  • At Infinity: Image at Focus (F); Real, inverted, highly diminished.
  • Beyond C: Image between F and C; Real, inverted, diminished.
  • At C: Image at C; Real, inverted, same size.
  • Between C and F: Image beyond C; Real, inverted, magnified.
  • At F: Image at infinity; Real, inverted, highly magnified.
  • Between F and Pole (P): Image behind the mirror; Virtual, erect, magnified.

Convex Mirror:

Regardless of the object's position, a convex mirror always forms an image that is virtual, erect, and diminished, located behind the mirror between the pole and focus.

11. Uses of Spherical Mirrors

Uses of Concave Mirrors:

  • Shaving mirrors and make-up mirrors (forms an erect, magnified image when the face is close).
  • Dentists' mirrors to see enlarged images of teeth.
  • Reflectors in searchlights, torches, and automobile headlights to produce a powerful parallel beam.
  • Reflecting telescopes and solar cookers (to concentrate sunlight).

Uses of Convex Mirrors:

  • Rear-view mirrors in vehicles because they provide a wider field of view and always produce erect images.
  • Streetlight reflectors to diverge light over a wider area.
  • Vigilance mirrors in departmental stores and parking lots.
