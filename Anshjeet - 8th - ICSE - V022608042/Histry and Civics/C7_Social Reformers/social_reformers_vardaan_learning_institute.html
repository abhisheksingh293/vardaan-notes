Social Reformers

The 19th century in India was a period of social and cultural revival and regeneration. The people of India, subjected to economic and political distress under British rule, lacked political rights. Western education brought awareness among the educated Indians, making them realize the critical need for political rights and social reforms. This era saw the rise of numerous social reformers who challenged existing orthodoxies and worked tirelessly to eradicate social evils.

1. Reform Movements in Bengal

Bengal was the epicentre of the early social reform movements, spearheaded by visionary leaders.

Raja Rammohan Roy and the Brahmo Samaj

Raja Rammohan Roy, born in a Bengali Brahmin family in 1772, was a pioneer of social reform. He was a scholar of multiple languages including Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian, English, Greek, Hebrew, Latin, and French. Rabindranath Tagore referred to him as the Father of the Indian Renaissance and the Prophet of Indian Nationalism.

  • He vehemently opposed idol worship and rituals, aiming to rid the Hindu religion of these practices.
  • He believed in the basic unity of all religions.
  • He was a strong advocate for the upliftment of women through education and worked towards the abolition of polygamy and child marriage.
  • He founded the Brahmo Sabha in 1828, which later evolved into the Brahmo Samaj, an important organisation for religious reform.
Fact

One of Raja Rammohan Roy's greatest achievements was his successful campaign to abolish the Sati system in 1829, with the crucial help of the Governor-General, Lord William Bentinck.

Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar

Born in a poor Brahmin family in 1820, Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar was another prominent reformer from Bengal. The Sanskrit College of Calcutta conferred upon him the title of Vidyasagar (ocean of learning).

  • He challenged the caste system by enrolling students from lower castes to study Sanskrit, a privilege previously reserved for Brahmins.
  • He made significant contributions to the Bengali language, writing a Bengali primer, Barna Parichaya, which is still in use today.
Important

His greatest contribution was championing the cause of widow remarriage and women's education. It was due to his relentless efforts that the Widow Remarriage Act was passed in 1856, making widow remarriage legal in India.

Ramakrishna Paramhansa and Swami Vivekananda

Ramakrishna Paramhansa (1836-86) was a saintly figure who lived at the Dakshineswar temple near Calcutta. His simplicity and profound wisdom attracted many followers. He emphasized that the best way to serve God was through serving humanity.

His most famous disciple was Narendra Nath Dutta, widely known as Swami Vivekananda. Swami Vivekananda carried his guru's message forward and founded the Ramakrishna Mission in 1897. The motto of the mission was to serve mankind, leading to the establishment of schools, colleges, hospitals, and relief efforts during natural disasters.

2. Reform Movements in Northern India

The wave of social reform also swept through northern India, significantly led by Swami Dayanand Saraswati.

Swami Dayanand Saraswati and the Arya Samaj

Born in Kathiawar in 1824 and originally named Mool Shankar, Swami Dayanand Saraswati became an ascetic at a young age. He started preaching in 1863, with a central message emphasizing a return to the roots of Hindu philosophy.

  • He propagated the motto "Back to the Vedas," asserting that the Vedas contained all essential knowledge.
  • He published his views in Hindi, making his teachings accessible to the masses. His most important work is the Satyarth Prakash.
  • He founded the Arya Samaj in Bombay in 1875.
Concept

The Arya Samaj was guided by ten principles. Its members strongly opposed child marriage and actively encouraged widow remarriage. They also established a network of Dayanand Anglo-Vedic (DAV) Schools and colleges to promote education.

3. Reform Movements in Western India

Western India saw the emergence of influential reformers who fought against deep-rooted caste and gender inequalities.

Jyotiba Phule and the Satyashodhak Samaj

Jyotirao Govindrao Phule, commonly known as Jyotiba, played a critical role in bringing about social reforms in Maharashtra.

  • In 1851, he started a school specifically for girls from lower castes. He even educated his own wife so she could teach in that school.
  • In 1873, he founded the Satyashodhak Samaj (Truth Seekers' Society), an association open to people of all castes and religions.
  • He vehemently opposed the domination of Brahmins and challenged orthodox practices, including the necessity of Brahmin priests for marriage ceremonies and idol worship.

The Prarthana Samaj

The Prarthana Samaj was founded in Bombay in 1867 by leaders like Mahadev Govind Ranade and Ramakrishna Bhandarkar. They strongly condemned the caste system and the practice of untouchability, advocating for the upliftment of women and widow remarriage.

4. Reform Movements among Muslims

The reform spirit also influenced the Muslim community, with leaders striving to modernize thoughts and practices.

Sir Syed Ahmed Khan and the Aligarh Movement

The most prominent social reformer among the Muslims was Sir Syed Ahmed Khan (1817-98). He believed that the progress of the Muslim community lay in embracing modern education while maintaining good relations with the British rulers.

  • In 1864, he founded the Scientific Society to translate English books on science and other subjects into Urdu.
  • In 1875, he established the Mohammedan Anglo-Oriental College at Aligarh, which later evolved into the Aligarh Muslim University (AMU) in 1920.
  • He advocated for raising the status of women, abolishing the purdah system, spreading education among women, and condemning polygamy.

This organized effort to modernize the Muslim community came to be known as the Aligarh Movement.

5. Impact of the Reform Movements

The social reformers of the 19th century had a profound and lasting impact on Indian society. Their collective efforts led to significant changes:

  • The practice of Sati and female infanticide were banned.
  • The Widow Remarriage Act of 1856 was passed.
  • Education for girls was popularized and expanded.
  • The marriageable age for girls was raised, culminating later in acts like the Sharda Act of 1929.
  • The rigidities of the caste system were challenged and weakened.
  • The adoption of western education brought modern, democratic, and nationalistic ideas to the forefront, paving the way for the Indian national movement.
