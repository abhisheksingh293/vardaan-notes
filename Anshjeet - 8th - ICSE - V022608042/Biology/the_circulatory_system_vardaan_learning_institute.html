The Circulatory System

The circulatory system, also known as the cardiovascular system, is one of the most vital organ systems in the body. It is responsible for transporting blood, oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and waste products to and from the body's cells. By maintaining this constant flow, the circulatory system ensures the proper functioning of all bodily systems and helps in maintaining homeostasis.

1. Blood

Blood is a fluid connective tissue that transports essential substances throughout the body. It consists of plasma and blood corpuscles (cells).

Concept

Composition of Blood: Blood consists of Plasma (55%) which is the liquid part, and Blood Cells (45%) which includes Red Blood Cells, White Blood Cells, and Platelets.

Plasma

Plasma is the liquid component of blood. It is a thick fluid which makes up 55% of the total blood volume. Plasma is 91% water and the rest of the total volume is made up of dissolved ions, suspended proteins (like albumin, globulin, and fibrinogen), dissolved gases, nutrient molecules, and waste products. Albumin is the major protein in plasma.

Blood Cells

Human blood consists mainly of three types of blood cells:

  • Red Blood Cells (RBCs): These are biconcave, non-nucleated, and disc-like cells. Blood contains 40% RBCs. They contain a protein pigment called haemoglobin that gives a red colour to the blood (O2 carrier).
  • White Blood Cells (WBCs): These cells are very few in number. They mainly protect the body against pathogens and build internal immunity.
  • Platelets: These are fewer in number than RBCs. Platelets help in the clotting of blood at the site of a wound or an injury.

2. Functions of Blood

The human circulatory system and blood serve the following important functions:

  • Transport of respiratory gases (Oxygen and Carbon Dioxide).
  • Transport of nutrients to the respective organs.
  • Transport of hormones from endocrine glands to the cells/tissues.
  • Destruction of pathogens (disease-causing germs).
  • Transport of metabolic wastes to the excretory organs for removal.
  • Regulation of body temperature and maintaining efficient body metabolism.

3. Types of Circulatory System

The circulatory system is broadly of two types:

  • Open Circulatory System: Blood flows through open body cavities (sinuses) and not in closed vessels. This system is commonly found in insects.
  • Closed Circulatory System: Blood flows through closed tube-like vessels under sufficiently high pressure. This system is found in all vertebrates, including humans.
Fact

Body Fluids: Body fluids are fluids such as blood, lymph, milk, and saliva which are produced in the body and then either circulated within the body or secreted outside it.

4. Human Circulatory System

The human circulatory system is a complex network that transports essential substances. It comprises three main components:

  • Blood: The fluid medium.
  • Heart: The pumping organ.
  • Blood Vessels: The pathways (Arteries, Veins, Capillaries).

5. Heart - The Pumping Organ

The heart is a muscular organ located in the chest cavity, slightly towards the left side. It serves as the pump that drives blood circulation throughout the body. It is protected by the rib-cage and enclosed within a double-layered membrane called the pericardium.

Structure of the Human Heart

The human heart is about the size of a human fist and is divided into four different chambers:

  • Atria (Two upper chambers): Thin-walled blood receiving chambers. The Right Atrium receives deoxygenated blood from the body, while the Left Atrium receives oxygenated blood from the lungs.
  • Ventricles (Two lower chambers): Thicker and more muscular chambers responsible for pumping blood. The Right Ventricle pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs, while the Left Ventricle pumps oxygenated blood to the entire body.

The right and left regions of the heart are separated by a muscular wall called the septum.

Valves Inside the Heart

Valves ensure the unidirectional flow of blood and prevent its backflow:

  • Tricuspid valve: Present between the right atrium and right ventricle.
  • Pulmonary valve: Present between the right ventricle and pulmonary artery.
  • Bicuspid (Mitral) valve: Present between the left atrium and left ventricle.
  • Aortic valve: Present between the left ventricle and aorta.

Working of the Heart

The heart follows a well-coordinated process involving the contraction and relaxation of its chambers:

  • Right Side (Deoxygenated Blood): Deoxygenated blood enters the right atrium via the Vena Cava. The atrium contracts, pushing blood into the right ventricle. The ventricle then pumps this blood to the lungs via the pulmonary artery.
  • Left Side (Oxygenated Blood): Oxygen-rich blood from the lungs enters the left atrium via pulmonary veins. The atrium contracts, sending blood to the left ventricle, which then forcefully pumps it into the aorta to be distributed across the body.
Important

Heart Sounds: The rhythmic contraction (Systole) and relaxation (Diastole) of chambers create the heartbeat. The "Lubb" sound is caused by the closing of the bicuspid and tricuspid valves, and the "Dubb" sound is caused by the closing of the pulmonary and aortic valves.

Cardiac Cycle & Pacemaker

The Cardiac Cycle refers to the sequence of events in one heartbeat. Normal human heartbeats are about 72 times per minute.

A Pacemaker is an electrical conducting system that generates and regulates the pumping action. If the natural pacemaker (SA node) is damaged, an artificial pacemaker (a small medical device) is surgically implanted in the chest to prevent the heart from beating too slowly.

6. Blood Vessels

Blood vessels are pathways through which blood flows. Angiology is the branch of science that deals with the circulatory system.

Types of Blood Vessels

  • Arteries: Strong, thick-walled, muscular vessels that carry oxygenated blood from the heart to other body parts. They are located deep within the body and blood flows under high pressure.
  • Veins: Thin, tube-like, elastic vessels closer to the skin surface. They carry deoxygenated blood from the body back to the heart. They contain valves to prevent backflow and operate under low pressure.
  • Capillaries: Short, tiny blood vessels found within tissues. They bring about the exchange of substances between blood and cells.

Blood Vessels of the Heart

  • Vena Cava: The superior and inferior vena cava bring deoxygenated blood from the upper and lower body to the right auricle.
  • Pulmonary Veins: Bring oxygenated blood from the lungs to the left auricle.
  • Pulmonary Trunk: Arises from the right ventricle, carrying deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
  • Aorta: Leaves the left ventricle carrying oxygenated blood to all body parts.
  • Coronary Artery & Veins: Supply oxygenated blood to the heart walls and bring back deoxygenated blood from the heart muscle.

7. Double Circulation

Because blood flows twice through the heart during one complete cycle, it is called double circulation:

  • Pulmonary Circulation: Circulation of deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs, and oxygenated blood from the lungs back to the left auricle.
  • Systematic Circulation: Circulation of oxygenated blood from the left ventricle through the aorta to body parts, and deoxygenated blood back to the right auricle via veins.

8. Lymphatic System

The lymphatic system is closely associated with the circulatory system and plays a role in maintaining fluid balance and immunity. It consists of:

  • Lymph Plasma: Interstitial fluid formed when blood plasma leaks through capillaries. It lacks RBCs but contains glucose, proteins, and minerals.
  • Lymph Corpuscles: Consists of leucocytes (white blood cells) and specialized lymphocytes that elicit immune responses.
  • Lymphoid Organs: Includes lymph nodes, spleen (the largest lymphatic organ), tonsils, adenoids, and thymus.
Concept

Functions of Lymph: Lymph supplies nutrition to parts where blood cannot reach, absorbs fats in the intestines, drains excess tissue fluid, and acts as a defense mechanism by filtering bacteria and microbes in lymph nodes.

9. Blood Groups

Discovered by Karl Landsteiner in 1900, the ABO Blood Group System is based on the presence or absence of antigens (A and B) on RBCs.

  • Group A: Contains antigen A and antibody B.
  • Group B: Contains antigen B and antibody A.
  • Group AB: Contains both A & B antigens, and NO antibodies. (Universal Recipient)
  • Group O: Contains neither antigen, but has BOTH antibodies. (Universal Donor)

10. Heart Related Conditions

  • Palpitations: Perceived abnormality of the heartbeat characterized by hard, fast, or irregular beats (often caused by stress/anxiety).
  • High Blood Pressure (Hypertension): Blood flows through vessels at higher than normal pressures, straining the heart and damaging organs.
  • Heart Attack: Sudden interruption of blood supply to the heart due to a blood clot, causing a part of the cardiac muscle to die.
  • Cardiac Arrest: Severe medical condition where the heart suddenly stops beating effectively, stopping blood flow to the brain and lungs.

11. Keeping the Heart Healthy

To lower the risk of cardiovascular diseases, one must adopt a healthy lifestyle:

  • Don't smoke or use tobacco: Chemicals damage blood vessels and lower blood oxygen.
  • Get moving: 30 to 60 minutes of daily physical activity controls weight and lowers blood pressure.
  • Eat a heart-healthy diet: Consume vegetables, fruits, legumes, and healthy fats.
  • Maintain a healthy weight: Being overweight raises the risk of diabetes and cholesterol.
  • Get quality sleep & manage stress: Sleep deficiency and stress negatively impact blood pressure. Activities like yoga and meditation help manage stress.
