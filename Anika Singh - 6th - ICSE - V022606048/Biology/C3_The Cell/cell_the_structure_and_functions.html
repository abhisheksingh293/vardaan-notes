CELL - THE STRUCTURE AND FUNCTIONS

1. Introduction to the Cell

Every organism begins its life as a single cell. Even the largest banyan tree or an elephant begins life from a single cell, which undergoes repeated divisions to become a complete living organism.

Concept

A cell is defined as the basic structural and functional unit of all living things. Because of this, cells are widely known as the building blocks of life.

Fact

The branch of biology which deals with the study of cells is called cytology.

2. Discovery of the Cell

  • Antony Van Leeuwenhoek (1632-1723): Developed a simple microscope using a single biconvex lens. He was the first to see cells (blood cells in the capillaries of a frog's foot-web) and recognized them as living units, though he did not call them cells.
  • Robert Hooke (1665): Developed a compound microscope with two lenses. He examined a thin slice of dead cork (bark of trees) and observed a cluster of box-like cubicles piled together. He was the first to coin the term "cell" because they reminded him of the cells of monks living in a monastery.
Important

The word cell is derived from the Latin word cella (compartment). Note that Robert Hooke had only seen the walls of dead cells. A cell is only considered living when it contains a jelly-like substance called protoplasm.

3. Cells: Numbers, Shapes, and Sizes

Number of Cells

  • Unicellular Organisms: Organisms formed of just one single cell. Examples include Bacteria, Yeast, Amoeba, Paramecium, and Chlamydomonas.
  • Multicellular Organisms: The body of most plants and animals are made up of millions and billions of cells. Examples include Rose, humans, lions, fish, and earthworms.

Cell Shape

There is a great variety in the shape of cells. The specific shape is determined by the function of the cell:

  • Spherical: Red blood cells are spherical or disc-shaped to hold more oxygen and flow easily through fine blood capillaries.
  • Oval: Chlamydomonas is a unicellular green algae with an oval-shaped body.
  • Cylindrical: Spirogyra (a multicellular green algae) and cardiac muscles of the heart (for easy contraction and relaxation).
  • Amoeboid (Irregular): The fresh water Amoeba and the white blood cells in our body have no definite shape.
  • Cubical/Rectangular: The cells of leaves and most plant parts.
  • Slipper-shaped: Paramecium, often called the "slipper-shaped animalcule".
  • Spindle-shaped: Smooth muscle cells are thin, long, and tapering at the ends, resembling a spindle.

Cell Size

  • Largest cell: The ostrich egg.
  • Longest cell: Nerve cells (can be up to 3 metres long).
  • Smallest cell: Found among bacteria (0.2 - 0.5 micrometre).

4. Cell Theory

Three scientists—Schleiden, Schwann, and Virchow—formulated the Cell Theory, which states:

  • Every living organism is made up of one or many cells.
  • The cell is the structural unit of all living organisms.
  • The cell is the functional unit of all living organisms.
  • All cells arise from pre-existing cells.

5. Cell - The Functional Unit of Life

Every function inside the body of living organisms is the result of cellular activity.

In Plants

  • Root cells: Responsible for the absorption of water and dissolved minerals from the soil.
  • Leaf cells: The chlorophyll (green pigment) containing cells utilise sunlight, carbon dioxide, and water to prepare food (glucose) through the process of photosynthesis.

In Animals

  • Muscle cells: Allow movement of body parts through their ability to contract and relax.
  • Nerve cells: Conduction of messages in the form of impulses.
  • Gland cells: Secrete enzymes that digest food.
  • Skin cells: Protect the body from germs and ultraviolet rays, and help in regulating body temperature.

6. Structure of a Cell

A plant or animal cell viewed under a compound microscope shows three essential parts:

I. Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane)

  • It is the outermost covering in animal cells (lies inside the cell wall in plant cells).
  • It is very thin, delicate, flexible, and is a living structure.
  • It is selectively permeable: it has fine pores that allow the entry of certain molecules only, while holding back others.

II. Cell Wall (Only in Plant Cells)

  • It lies outside the cell membrane and is a non-living structure made up of cellulose.
  • It gives shape and rigidity to the plant cell.
  • It is freely permeable, allowing substances in solution to enter and leave without hindrance.
  • It protects the cell from mechanical injuries and disease-causing agents.

III. Cytoplasm

  • A semi-liquid (viscous), colourless, and translucent substance found between the nucleus and the cell membrane.

IV. Nucleus

  • A small spherical mass mostly located towards the centre of the cytoplasm. It regulates and coordinates all life processes of the cell.
  • It is enclosed by a delicate, porous nuclear membrane containing a dense nucleoplasm.
  • Inside the nucleoplasm is a smaller darker body called the nucleolus and a network of chromosomes.
  • Chromosomes contain units called genes, which transmit characteristics from parents to offspring.
Fact

The number of chromosomes is definite in each species. Every cell of the human body has 46 chromosomes (occurring in 23 pairs), while a pea plant cell has 14 chromosomes (7 pairs).

V. Cell Organelles

Minute structures found in the cytoplasm, visible only under an electron microscope, are called cell organelles. Just as there are organs in an organism, organelles perform specific functions inside a cell.

  • Plastids (Found only in plant cells): Depending on the pigment they contain, there are three types:
    • Chloroplasts: Green plastids that trap solar energy for photosynthesis.
    • Chromoplasts: Contain yellow and red pigments, imparting varied colours to flowers and fruits to attract insects for pollination.
    • Leucoplasts: Colourless plastids occurring in seeds. They store starch, fats, and proteins.
  • Vacuoles: Non-living inclusions in the cytoplasm bound by a membrane, filled with water and substances in solution form called cell sap.
Concept

Protoplasm is the living substance of the cell. It consists of the cytoplasm and the nucleus combined.

7. Differences Between Plant and Animal Cells

  • Size: Plant cells are usually larger; animal cells are usually smaller.
  • Cell Wall: Plant cells have a definite cell wall; animal cells do not.
  • Cytoplasm: Plant cells have a thin layer of cytoplasm (not as dense); animal cells have dense, granular cytoplasm filling almost the entire cell.
  • Vacuoles: In plant cells, they are prominent, fewer in number, and store water/nutrients. In animal cells, they are small, numerous, and concerned with excretion or secretion.
  • Plastids: Present in plant cells; completely absent in animal cells.

8. Cell Division

New cells need to be produced in organisms for four primary reasons:

  1. Replacement
  2. Repair
  3. Reproduction
  4. Growth
Important

Cell division is a strict necessity for the existence of all living organisms, allowing them to grow, heal, and reproduce.
