Elements, Compounds and Mixtures

1. Introduction to Matter

Everything around us that occupies space and has mass is called Matter. Heat, light, sound, and radiation waves are not considered matter because they have no mass and do not occupy space.

Based on chemical constitution, matter can be classified into two major categories:

  • Pure Substances: Elements and Compounds.
  • Impure Substances: Mixtures.

2. Elements

An element is a pure substance made up of only one kind of atoms. It cannot be broken into two or more simpler substances by any physical or chemical methods. Examples include iron, sulphur, zinc, copper, and oxygen.

2.1 The Atom (Basic Unit)

An atom is the smallest particle of an element which may or may not exist independently. An atom is further divisible into:

  • Nucleus: The center of the atom containing positively charged particles (Protons) and particles with no charge (Neutrons).
  • Orbits: Paths surrounding the nucleus in which negatively charged particles called Electrons revolve.
Concept

Molecules & Atomicity
A molecule is the smallest unit of a substance that can exist independently and possess all the properties of that substance.
Atomicity is the number of atoms present in one molecule of an element. E.g., Hydrogen (H₂) is diatomic, Ozone (O₃) is polyatomic.

2.2 Symbols and Classification of Elements

An atom of an element is denoted by a symbol (a short abbreviation). Elements in the periodic table are arranged in horizontal rows called periods and vertical columns called groups.

  • Metals: Placed on the left of the periodic table. They have lustre, are malleable (can be beaten into sheets), ductile (can be drawn into wires), and are good conductors of heat and electricity. E.g., Copper, Iron.
  • Non-metals: Placed on the right. They are non-lustrous, non-malleable, non-ductile, and poor conductors. E.g., Sulphur, Carbon.
  • Metalloids: Show properties of both metals and non-metals. E.g., Silicon, Boron.
  • Noble Gases: Unreactive inert elements present on the extreme right of the periodic table. E.g., Helium, Neon, Argon.
Fact

Latin Names of Elements
Many common element symbols are derived from their Latin names. For example:
• Potassium (K) = Kalium
• Sodium (Na) = Natrium
• Iron (Fe) = Ferrum
• Copper (Cu) = Cuprum
• Gold (Au) = Aurum

3. Compounds

A compound is a pure substance made up of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed proportion. For example, Water (H₂O) and Carbon dioxide (CO₂).

Characteristics of a Compound:

  • Elements are present in a definite proportion.
  • A compound has properties entirely different from the properties of the elements it is made from (e.g., Hydrogen and Oxygen are gases, but together they form liquid Water).
  • Constituents cannot be separated by physical methods.

3.1 Chemical Formula and Valency

A chemical formula is a symbolic representation of one molecule of a compound showing the number of atoms present. The combining capacity of an atom is called its valency.

How to write a formula (Criss-Cross Method): Write symbols → Write valencies below → Divide by a common factor if possible → Interchange valencies and write as subscripts.

4. Mixtures

A mixture is an impure substance made up of two or more elements or compounds mixed together mechanically in any proportion.

  • Homogeneous Mixtures: Same composition and properties throughout. E.g., salt and water, alcohol and water (miscible).
  • Heterogeneous Mixtures: Different composition and properties throughout. E.g., oil and water, chalk and water, sand and water (immiscible).
Important

Iron & Sulphur Activity
If you mix iron filings and sulphur powder, they form a mixture. You can separate the iron using a magnet.
However, if you heat this mixture, they combine chemically to form a dark grey compound called Iron Sulphide (FeS). A magnet will no longer attract the iron because its original properties are lost!

5. Comparative Study: Compounds vs Mixtures

  • Composition: Compounds have fixed proportions; Mixtures have variable proportions.
  • Properties: Compounds do not retain original properties; Mixtures retain original properties of their components.
  • Separation: Compounds cannot be separated physically; Mixtures can be separated by physical methods.
  • Energy: Energy is usually given out/absorbed when making compounds; No energy change occurs when making mixtures.

6. Methods of Separating Mixtures

Constituents of a mixture can be separated by physical methods to remove unwanted substances or to obtain useful pure components.

6.1 Separating Solid-Solid Mixtures

  • Hand Picking: Based on size, color, or shape differences. E.g., picking stones from rice.
  • Sieving: Based on size differences. Larger particles stay on the sieve, smaller ones pass through. E.g., bran from wheat flour.
  • Magnetic Separation: Separating magnetic particles (like iron) from non-magnetic particles (like wood or plastic) using a magnet.
  • Sublimation: Used for a solid that directly changes into a gas on heating (e.g., Iodine, Ammonium Chloride, Camphor).

6.2 Separating Solid-Liquid Mixtures

  • Sedimentation & Decantation: For insoluble, heavy solids. Solid settles at the bottom (Sedimentation), and clear liquid is carefully poured out (Decantation).
  • Loading: Adding a chemical like alum to a mixture to make fine suspended particles (like clay) heavy so they settle down faster.
  • Evaporation: Used to separate a soluble solid from a liquid by heating it until the liquid turns into vapor, leaving the solid behind. E.g., obtaining salt from sea water.
  • Filtration: Passing a mixture through filter paper. The liquid that passes through is the filtrate, and the solid left on the paper is the residue.
Concept

Why is Filtration preferred?
Filtration is much better and more sensitive than sedimentation and decantation because it removes even the smallest, lightest solid particles that refuse to settle down. It also prevents the risk of particles mixing back into the liquid during pouring.
