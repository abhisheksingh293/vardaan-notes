FORCE

1. Force as a Push or a Pull

In our daily life, we frequently push or pull things. Opening a door, kicking a football, pushing a box, or lifting a school bag all require effort. The push or pull on an object is known as force.

Concept

Force is defined as a push or a pull applied on an object that changes or tends to change the state of rest, the state of uniform motion, or the direction/shape of an object.

When a body moves closer to us on the application of force, it is called a pull. When it moves away from us, it is called a push.

2. Effects of a Force

A force applied on an object may affect it in several ways. While force itself cannot be seen, we can easily see and feel its effects.

  • A force can cause motion in a stationary object: When you lift a book, ride a bicycle, or push a table, you apply force to make it move.
  • A force can stop a moving object: A goalkeeper applying force with his hands stops a moving football. Applying brakes stops a moving vehicle.
  • A force can change the direction of motion: A batsman hits a ball with his bat, changing its direction. In carrom, the striker changes direction upon hitting the edge.
  • A force can change the speed of a moving object: Pushing a moving bicycle from behind increases its speed, while pushing it from the front decreases its speed.
  • A force can change the shape and size of a body: Squeezing a lemon, inflating a balloon, stretching a spring, or rolling dough all involve a change in shape and size due to applied force.
Important

It is important to remember that all motions are produced by force, but not all forces produce motion. For example, pushing a heavy steel almirah may not result in movement if the applied force is insufficient to overcome friction.

3. SI Unit of Force

The magnitude of force applied on an object is measured in specific units.

Fact

The standard SI Unit of force is the newton (N). It is named in honor of the great physicist Sir Isaac Newton.

4. Resultant Force

When two or more forces act simultaneously on an object, the net single force acting on the object is called the resultant force.

  • Forces in the Same Direction: The resultant force is the total sum of the magnitudes of the forces (Resultant Force = Force A + Force B).
  • Forces in Opposite Directions: The resultant force is the difference between the magnitudes of the two forces (Resultant Force = Force A - Force B). The object moves in the direction of the bigger force.

5. Types of Forces

Forces are broadly divided into two main groups based on how they interact with objects.

Contact Forces

A contact force acts when two objects are in actual physical contact with each other. Examples include:

  • Muscular force: Force applied using parts of the body (e.g., arms or legs).
  • Mechanical force: Force produced by a machine.
  • Frictional force: The opposing force created between two surfaces in contact.

Non-Contact Forces

A force that acts between two objects without actual physical contact is called a non-contact force. This force can act through empty space. Examples include:

  • Gravitational force: The pull exerted by the earth on objects.
  • Magnetic force: Force exerted by magnets.
  • Electrostatic force: Force exerted by electrically charged bodies.

6. Force of Friction

When a ball rolls on the ground, it gradually slows down and stops. This occurs because the ground exerts a force opposing its motion. This opposing force is known as friction.

Concept

Friction is a contact force that comes into action whenever a body moves or tries to move over a surface. It always acts in the opposite direction to the motion.

Properties of Friction

  • It depends on the roughness or smoothness of the surfaces in contact. Rough surfaces produce more friction.
  • It increases as the mass (weight) of the moving object increases.
  • Friction produces heat (e.g., rubbing palms together).
  • Friction causes wear and tear on contacting surfaces.

Kinds of Friction

  • Static friction: Comes into play when an object tends to move, but there is no actual motion yet.
  • Sliding friction: The frictional force exerted when an object actually slides over a surface.
  • Rolling friction: The frictional force exerted when an object (like a wheel) rolls over a surface. Rolling friction is much smaller than sliding friction.

7. Advantages and Disadvantages of Friction

Advantages

  • Writing on paper is possible because friction provides grip for a pen/pencil.
  • We can walk on the ground without slipping because of friction.
  • Moving vehicles can be slowed down and stopped using brakes.
  • Lighting a matchstick is possible because friction generates heat.

Disadvantages

  • It reduces the efficiency of machines by causing energy loss (heat and noise).
  • It causes wear and tear in machine parts, shoe soles, and vehicle tyres.
  • Vehicles give less mileage because fuel is spent overcoming friction.
Important

Because friction causes wear and wastes energy, it is considered an "evil." However, because we cannot walk, write, or stop without it, it is a necessary evil.

8. Methods to Reduce and Increase Friction

Reducing Friction

In machines and certain activities, we try to minimize friction to save energy and prevent wear.

  • Applying lubricants like oil or grease between moving parts.
  • Using ball bearings and rollers to convert sliding friction into rolling friction.
  • Sprinkling fine powder on surfaces (e.g., carrom boards).
  • Streamlining: Shaping bodies (like airplanes, fish, and birds) to taper at the ends, minimizing fluid friction.

Increasing Friction

In situations where a strong grip is required, friction is intentionally increased.

  • Tyres of vehicles have deep grooves to prevent skidding.
  • Athletes wear shoes with spikes to get better grip on the ground.
  • Staircase steps and ramps are made rough to prevent slipping.
