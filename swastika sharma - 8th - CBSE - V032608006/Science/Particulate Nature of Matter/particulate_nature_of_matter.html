Particulate Nature of Matter

1. Introduction to Matter

Concept

Matter is defined as anything that occupies space and has weight (mass). It is composed of a large number of extremely tiny building blocks called constituent particles.

You cannot see these individual particles with the naked eye. For example, a single piece of chalk or a drop of water consists of millions of tiny constituent particles tightly packed together.

Fact

The ancient Indian philosopher Maharishi Kanad was the first to speak about the idea of an indivisible particle of matter, which he named Parmanu. Similarly, the English word 'atom' is derived from the Greek word 'atomos', which means indivisible.

2. What Decides the State of Matter?

Matter mainly exists in three distinct physical states: solid, liquid, and gas. The physical state of any substance is primarily determined by two interacting factors:

  • Interparticle Forces (Intermolecular Forces): These are the attractive forces that hold the constituent particles of matter together.
  • Interparticle Space (Interparticle Distance): This is the empty space or physical distance existing between the individual particles of matter.
Important

The Rule of States: An increase in the distance between particles reduces the strength of the interparticle force. The varying strength of this force is what ultimately decides whether a substance exists as a solid, liquid, or gas.

3. Properties of the States of Matter

A. Solid State

  • Forces and Space: Solids have the strongest interparticle forces of attraction and the minimum interparticle space.
  • Shape and Volume: They possess a fixed, definite shape and a definite volume.
  • Particle Movement: The strong forces hold particles in fixed positions. They cannot move freely and can only vibrate to and fro about their fixed positions.
  • Compressibility: Because particles are densely packed, solids cannot be compressed easily.

Observation: When a solid is heated, it may eventually reach its melting point—the minimum temperature at which a solid melts to become a liquid at atmospheric pressure.

B. Liquid State

  • Forces and Space: Liquids have weaker interparticle forces than solids, and possess a little more interparticle space.
  • Shape and Volume: They have a definite volume but do not have a fixed shape. They take the shape of the container they are placed in.
  • Particle Movement: Particles can move around and easily slide past one another within the boundary of the liquid, allowing liquids to flow freely.

Observation: The specific temperature at which a liquid starts boiling rapidly and turns into vapour at atmospheric pressure is called its boiling point.

C. Gaseous State

  • Forces and Space: Gases feature extremely weak interparticle forces of attraction and the maximum interparticle space.
  • Shape and Volume: They have neither a fixed shape nor a definite volume. They expand endlessly to occupy the entire space available to them.
  • Particle Movement: Particles move very rapidly, freely, and randomly in all possible directions.
  • Compressibility: Gases are highly compressible due to the vast empty spaces between their particles.

Examples: Oxygen (which you breathe), Carbon dioxide, and Water vapour.

4. Movement of Particles and Thermal Energy

Concept

Thermal Energy is the internal heat energy of particles. It determines the physical state and continuous motion of atoms or molecules within a given substance.

Particles in all states of matter are in a state of constant, uninterrupted motion. However, the extent of this motion varies significantly based on energy:

  • Thermal energy is intrinsically the lowest in solids (hence only vibrations occur) and highest in gases (leading to rapid, chaotic movement).
  • When you provide heat to a substance, its thermal energy increases. This causes the particles to move or vibrate much more vigorously.
  • For example, if you place a few crystals of potassium permanganate in water, its particles naturally spread throughout the liquid due to continuous random motion. You will observe that this movement is much faster in hot water than in cold water.
